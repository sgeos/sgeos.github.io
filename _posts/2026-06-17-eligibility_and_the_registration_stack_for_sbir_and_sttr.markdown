The [agency survey][related_post_agencies] ended on the first strategic choice,
which house to knock on.
Before a company can knock at all it must pass two gates, and this article is
about both.
The first gate is eligibility, the question of what the company must be to
compete, and the second is registration, the work of getting the company known
to the federal systems that accept a proposal.
The two are different in kind, since eligibility is a property of the company
that is either true or false on the day it applies, while registration is a
sequence of accounts and identifiers that takes real calendar time to assemble,
and that second fact is the practical heart of this article, because the
registrations take weeks and a company that starts them late will miss a
deadline it was otherwise ready to meet.
The usual caution holds with full force, that the thresholds, the percentages,
the forms, and the systems all change, so the specifics below are current-as-of
and the authoritative source is the current policy directive, the
[Small Business Administration][ref_sba] [size rules][research_size], and the
live solicitation.

## Eligibility, What the Company Must Be

The first gate is a set of facts about the company.
It must be organized for profit and located in the United States, it must be
small, which for these programs means no more than five hundred employees, and
crucially that count includes the employees of any affiliates, the other
companies that share common ownership or control, so a small company owned by a
large one is not small for this purpose.
The employee standard is the program's own rather than the size standard tied to
an industry code in the [North American Industry Classification System][ref_naics]
that governs much of federal contracting, so the five-hundred-employee line is
the one that matters here.
The programs are open to every qualifying small business and are not
socioeconomic set-asides, so they are distinct from the women-owned,
veteran-owned, and disadvantaged-business programs the Small Business
Administration also runs, which an applicant should not confuse with them.
A company that is genuinely small, for-profit, and American has cleared the first
part of the gate, and the rest of eligibility is about who owns it and who does
the work.

## The Ownership Rules and the Investor Exception

Ownership is where eligibility turns subtle.
The default rule is that the company must be more than half owned and controlled
by individuals who are citizens or permanent residents of the United States, or
by other small businesses that are themselves so owned, which keeps the program
with domestic small business rather than with foreign or large owners.
There is an exception that the agency survey flagged, since a reauthorization
allowed agencies to fund companies majority-owned by multiple investment
concerns, the [venture capital][ref_venture_capital],
[private equity][ref_private_equity], and [hedge fund][ref_hedge_fund] firms,
provided no single one holds a majority, but the exception is permissive rather
than universal, so an agency must choose to use it and only some do.
The consequence for an investor-backed company is the one the survey drew, that
it may be eligible at an agency that adopted the exception and barred at one that
did not, so ownership must be checked against the specific agency rather than the
program in general.

## The Principal Investigator and the Work

Eligibility also reaches the people and the place of the work.
The [principal investigator][ref_pi] who leads the project is constrained
differently by the two programs, since SBIR requires the investigator to be
primarily employed by the small business, more than half their time, at the time
of the award and through the project, while STTR relaxes this and lets the
investigator sit at either the company or the
[research institution][ref_ffrdc] it partners with.
The work itself must be split in the proportions the
[orientation article][related_post_intro] gave,
the small business performing at least two thirds of Phase I and at least half
of Phase II under SBIR, and at least forty percent under STTR with at least
thirty percent at the research institution, and the work must be performed in
the United States absent a specific waiver.
These rules together ensure that the company, and not a subcontractor or a
foreign site, is genuinely doing the research the award funds.

## The Performance Benchmarks

A repeat applicant faces one more gate.
The programs guard against the company that wins award after award without ever
advancing or commercializing the work, the pattern the strategy article will
call the mill, by setting performance benchmarks, a minimum rate at which past
Phase I awards progressed to Phase II and a minimum commercialization from past
Phase II awards.
A company with a long enough history that falls below these benchmarks is barred
for a time from new Phase I awards, so eligibility for an experienced applicant
depends on its track record and not only on its size and ownership.
A first-time or early applicant is not yet measured this way, but the benchmarks
are the reason the commercialization reporting that a later article treats is not
optional bookkeeping but a condition of staying eligible.
A separate rule binds the applicant who pursues several agencies at once with one
technology, the strategy the survey encouraged, since a company may not be paid
by more than one award for the same work, so it must disclose its prior, current,
and pending support and certify that the proposed effort is not essentially
equivalent to work already funded.

## National-Security Eligibility

The newest gate is national security.
The reauthorization the orientation article described added a requirement that
agencies screen applicants for foreign ties, so a company now discloses its
foreign ownership, its foreign relationships, and any participation in a foreign
government talent program, and an agency may deny an award on national-security
grounds.
The scrutiny falls most heavily at the defense and mission agencies, as the
survey noted, but the disclosure obligation is general, and it is a genuine
eligibility gate rather than a formality, since an award can be withheld on it.
A company with foreign investors, foreign founders, or foreign research ties
should expect the question and prepare to answer it.
Export control is the near neighbor of this gate, since a foreign national on the
technical team can raise questions under regimes such as the
[arms-traffic regulations][ref_itar] the regulatory article named, which can
limit who may perform parts of the work even when the company's ownership is
clean.
All of eligibility, moreover, is something the company certifies rather than
something the agency merely checks, at the proposal, at the award, and through
performance, so a misstatement of size, ownership, or work is not a clerical slip
but exposure under the [False Claims Act][ref_false_claims], which is why the
facts above are worth getting right.

## The Registration Stack, In Order

A company that is eligible still cannot submit until it is registered, and the
registrations come in an order.
The foundation is an identity account through [Login.gov][research_login], which
secures access to the federal systems.
Next is registration in the [System for Award Management][ref_sam], the central
federal registry, which issues the unique entity identifier that replaced the
older [data universal numbering system][ref_duns] number and assigns a
[commercial and government entity code][ref_cage], and which for a contract
agency also captures the representations and certifications a contract requires.
Then the company registers in the program's own [company registry][research_sbir_gov]
to obtain its small-business control identifier, the number that ties it to the
SBIR and STTR system.
Last is the specific agency's submission system, the
[Defense SBIR and STTR Innovation Portal][research_dod] for the Department of
Defense, the electronic research
administration and the federal grants system for the National Institutes of
Health through [Grants.gov][ref_grants_gov], and the equivalent at each other
agency, the systems the agency survey named.
Every one of these registrations is free, and the government never charges for
them, so a company should be wary of the third parties that solicit a fee to
perform a registration it can complete itself.

## Why the Stack Gates the Calendar

The registrations are not instantaneous, and that is the trap.
The registration in the central [award system][research_sam] in particular
requires the entity to be validated, a step that has at times taken weeks, and the other accounts
take their own time, so the whole stack is a multi-week undertaking rather than
an afternoon of forms.
A company that discovers a fitting solicitation with three weeks left to its
deadline may simply be unable to register in time, which means the registrations
must be started long before a specific proposal is in view, ideally as soon as
the company decides to pursue the programs at all.
The registrations also expire, since the central award registration must be
renewed each year, so a lapsed registration can bar a company that was eligible
and once registered, and keeping the stack current is a standing obligation
rather than a one-time task.

## Scale and the Small-Company Case

For a small or new company, the kind that might have prototyped the
[aircraft][related_post_prototyping] of the engineering series, this is the first
real labor of the campaign.
It is mostly one-time setup, the accounts and identifiers assembled once and then
renewed, but it is genuine work, and for a founder unfamiliar with federal
systems it is the least glamorous and most easily underestimated part of the
whole effort.
The practical advice is to treat the registrations as the long pole of the first
campaign, to start them before a topic is even chosen, and to keep them current
afterward so that the next opportunity is not lost to a lapsed account.
With eligibility confirmed and the stack assembled, the company is finally able
to do the thing the next articles take up, find a topic and write a proposal.

## Out of Scope

Several matters are left aside.
The exact employee threshold, the ownership percentages, the investor-exception
cap, and the benchmark rates are given as current-as-of, since they change and
the policy directive and the solicitation are authoritative.
The detailed determination of affiliation is a legal question that can turn on
the facts of a particular ownership structure and is tested through the size
protests the Small Business Administration adjudicates, which are beyond this
overview.
The step-by-step screens of each agency portal change too often to document
here, and nothing in this article is legal advice on a company's eligibility or
its foreign-ownership exposure.
The programs of other countries remain with the dedicated analogs article.

## Conclusion

Eligibility and registration are the gate every applicant passes before it can
compete, the one a property of the company and the other a stack of accounts that
takes weeks to build.
Eligibility asks whether the company is a genuinely small, American, mostly
domestically owned for-profit doing its own research, with a qualifying
investigator, a clean enough national-security profile, and a good enough track
record if it has one, and registration asks only that the company do the
unglamorous work of getting itself into the federal systems early enough to meet
a deadline.
Neither gate is intellectually hard, but both are unforgiving, and the company
that clears them early is the one free to spend its energy on the topic and the
proposal that the rest of the series takes up.
