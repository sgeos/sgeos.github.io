This fourth article of the series treats the 1789-1861 period from the constitutional founding through the eve of the Civil War, during which the American ethnoreligious landscape underwent substantial expansion, division, and reconstitution. Coverage includes the Second Great Awakening from Kentucky camp meetings through Charles Finney's burned-over district revivals, the Methodist and Baptist explosion producing the largest denominational formations, the Restoration Movement and Millerite formations, Mormon founding under Joseph Smith and subsequent Utah migration under Brigham Young, Irish Catholic mass migration accelerated by the Great Famine, German Protestant, Catholic, and Jewish migration alongside the 1848 Revolutionary refugees, Scandinavian Lutheran migration from the 1840s forward, early Chinese migration to the West Coast beginning with the 1849 Gold Rush, nativist mobilization culminating in the Know-Nothing movement, abolitionism as religious movement, the American Colonization Society and Liberian settlement as parallel religious response to slavery, Southern proslavery theology, denominational splits preceding the political split, Indian Removal and Trail of Tears, Cherokee syncretic Christianity, missionary movements to Native peoples, African American religious formation continuing under slavery, Nat Turner and Denmark Vesey, free Black church expansion, Mexican Catholic incorporation via the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, Transcendentalism, antebellum social reform including temperance women's rights and prison reform, and the systematic application of the six-axis analytical framework to the antebellum substrates.

## The Post-Founding Religious Landscape 1789-1861

The 1789-1861 period exhibits substantial expansion and reorganization of the American ethnoreligious formation across the dimensions treated in this article. The population of the United States grew from approximately

$$P_{\text{US}}(1789) \approx 3.9 \times 10^6, \quad P_{\text{US}}(1861) \approx 3.1 \times 10^7$$

reflecting substantial natural increase combined with substantial migration inflow. The composition changed substantially, with the European-descent free population, the African-descent enslaved and free populations, and the indigenous population each following distinct trajectories.

The African-descent population grew from approximately

$$P_{\text{African, US}}(1789) \approx 7.5 \times 10^5, \quad P_{\text{African, US}}(1861) \approx 4.5 \times 10^6$$

with the proportion enslaved declining slightly through Northern emancipation offset by continuing Southern expansion,

$$f_{\text{enslaved}}(1789) \approx 0.90, \quad f_{\text{enslaved}}(1861) \approx 0.89$$

The indigenous population within the territory that would become the United States continued to decline through removal, warfare, disease, and demographic pressure. Estimates place

$$P_{\text{indigenous, US territory}}(1789) \approx 3 \times 10^5, \quad P_{\text{indigenous, US territory}}(1861) \approx 2.5 \times 10^5$$

with substantial regional variation and continued displacement to lands west of the Mississippi treated below.

Immigration across the period was substantial, with approximately

$$N_{\text{immigrants, US, 1820 to 1861}} \approx 5 \times 10^6$$

arriving across the four decades of the pre-Civil War period. The ethnoreligious composition of the migration included substantial Irish Catholic (approximately 1.5 million from 1846-1855 alone), German Protestant Catholic and Jewish (approximately 1.5 million across the period), British and Scandinavian Protestant, and smaller streams. The [Daniels 1990][book_daniels_1990] Coming to America, [Bodnar 1985][book_bodnar_1985] The Transplanted, and [Handlin 1951][book_handlin_1951] The Uprooted provide the comprehensive treatments.

The religious geography of the antebellum United States reflected substantial denominational expansion and reorganization. By 1850 the largest denominational formations by adherent count were

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Methodist Episcopal Church (all branches)} &\approx 1.3 \times 10^6 \\
\text{Baptist (all conventions)} &\approx 8.2 \times 10^5 \\
\text{Presbyterian (all synods)} &\approx 4.9 \times 10^5 \\
\text{Congregationalist} &\approx 2.0 \times 10^5 \\
\text{Roman Catholic} &\approx 1.6 \times 10^6 \\
\text{Lutheran} &\approx 1.6 \times 10^5 \\
\text{Episcopal} &\approx 1.4 \times 10^5
\end{aligned}$$

per the 1850 Census of Religious Bodies, with Catholic populations having overtaken any single Protestant denomination through substantial immigration. The [Finke and Stark 1992][book_finke_stark_1992] The Churching of America 1776-2005, [Gaustad and Barlow 2001][book_gaustad_barlow_2001] New Historical Atlas of Religion in America, and [Ahlstrom 1972][book_ahlstrom_1972] provide the quantitative and geographic treatments.

## The Second Great Awakening

The Second Great Awakening, conventionally dated from approximately 1795 through approximately 1845 with substantial regional variation, produced the religious revival that would reshape the American religious landscape and provide the religious foundation for the antebellum reform movements. The [Hatch 1989][book_hatch_1989] The Democratization of American Christianity, [Cross 1950][book_cross_1950] The Burned-over District, [Boles 1972][book_boles_1972] The Great Revival, [Johnson 1978][book_johnson_1978] A Shopkeeper's Millennium, [Long 1998][book_long_1998] Great Awakenings, [Butler 1990][book_butler_1990] Awash in a Sea of Faith, [Sandeen 1970][book_sandeen_1970] The Roots of Fundamentalism, [Douglas 1977][book_douglas_1977] The Feminization of American Culture, and [Boyer 1978][book_boyer_1978] Urban Masses and Moral Order in America provide the comprehensive treatments.

Kentucky camp meetings from the 1790s forward produced the frontier revival pattern that would shape subsequent evangelical practice. James McGready (1763-1817), Presbyterian minister at Red River (Logan County, Kentucky), initiated the 1800 Gasper River sacramental meeting that produced the first documented camp meeting. The [1801 Cane Ridge Revival][ref_cane_ridge_revival_1801] in Bourbon County, Kentucky under Barton Warren Stone (1772-1844), reached reported attendance of

$$N_{\text{Cane Ridge attendance}} \sim 1 \times 10^4 \text{ to } 2 \times 10^4$$

across the several days of the meeting, comprising approximately one-tenth of the Kentucky population at that date. The [Boles 1972][book_boles_1972] The Great Revival, [Conkin 1990][book_conkin_1990] Cane Ridge, and [Johnson 1955][book_johnson_1955] The Frontier Camp Meeting provide the comprehensive treatments.

Charles Grandison Finney (1792-1875), Presbyterian evangelist and later Congregationalist minister, produced the Second Great Awakening in the burned-over district of western New York from approximately 1824 through 1832. Finney's new measures including protracted meetings, the anxious bench for those considering conversion, direct address to individuals in the audience, and evangelistic prayer meetings reshaped the American revival practice. His 1835 [Finney 1835][book_finney_1835] [Lectures on Revivals of Religion][ref_finney_lectures_on_revivals_1835] provided the systematic treatment, subsequently extended in the [Systematic Theology][ref_finney_systematic_theology]. Finney subsequently served as President of Oberlin College from 1851 through 1866. The [Hardman 1987][book_hardman_1987] Charles Grandison Finney and [Hambrick-Stowe 1996][book_hambrick_stowe_1996] Charles G. Finney and the Spirit of American Evangelicalism provide the biographical treatments.

The burned-over district of western New York, the region between the Hudson Valley and Lake Erie described by Finney as having been so frequently burned over by the fires of revival, produced substantial religious innovation. The region provided the setting for Mormon founding treated below, the Millerite movement culminating in the 1844 Great Disappointment, the Fox Sisters' 1848 initiation of American Spiritualism at Hydesville, the Shaker communities including Sodus Bay and others, the Oneida Community under John Humphrey Noyes from 1848, and additional radical religious formations. The [Cross 1950][book_cross_1950] The Burned-over District provides the foundational treatment.

Lyman Beecher (1775-1863), Connecticut Congregationalist minister and later Presbyterian, provided the institutional consolidation of the Second Great Awakening in New England and the Midwest. His 1826 removal to Boston, subsequent removal to Cincinnati as president of Lane Theological Seminary from 1832 through 1852, and fathering of the Beecher family (Harriet Beecher Stowe, Henry Ward Beecher, Catharine Beecher, and others) provided the extended intergenerational Second Great Awakening influence. The [Rugoff 1981][book_rugoff_1981] The Beechers treats the family.

Timothy Dwight (1752-1817), President of Yale from 1795 through his death, provided the institutional New England Congregationalist Awakening leadership. His revival preaching at Yale from 1802 forward shaped the Congregationalist ministerial formation for two subsequent decades. Nathaniel William Taylor (1786-1858) subsequently developed the New Haven Theology providing the systematic theological reformulation of Calvinist orthodoxy that would shape antebellum New England theology. The [Kling 1993][book_kling_1993] A Field of Divine Wonders and [Marsden 1970][book_marsden_1970] The Evangelical Mind and the New School Presbyterian Experience treat the developments.

Second Great Awakening voluntary religious societies produced substantial institutional infrastructure that would shape antebellum American religious and social life. The founding dates of the major benevolent empire organizations form the chronological cluster

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{American Bible Society} &: 1816 \\
\text{American Colonization Society} &: 1816 \\
\text{American Sunday School Union} &: 1824 \\
\text{American Tract Society} &: 1825 \\
\text{American Temperance Society} &: 1826 \\
\text{American Home Missionary Society} &: 1826 \\
\text{American Peace Society} &: 1828 \\
\text{American Anti-Slavery Society} &: 1833
\end{aligned}$$

providing the interlocking network of religious voluntary organizations that historians have termed the benevolent empire. The [American Bible Society founding 1816][ref_american_bible_society_1816], [American Sunday School Union founding 1824][ref_american_sunday_school_union_1824], and the earlier 1810 organization of the [American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions][ref_abcfm_1810] provided the institutional infrastructure. The [Foster 1960][book_foster_1960] An Errand of Mercy provides the foundational treatment.

## Denominational Growth and the Methodist Explosion

Antebellum denominational growth was substantial across the evangelical denominations, with Methodists and Baptists overtaking the colonial establishment traditions. The [Finke and Stark 1992][book_finke_stark_1992] The Churching of America 1776-2005 documents the quantitative reversal.

Methodist growth from the 1784 organization treated in [A268][related_post_a268_awakening_and_founding] to the 1850 peak reached approximately

$$P_{\text{Methodist adherents, US}}(1850) \approx 2.7 \times 10^6, \quad \frac{P_{\text{Methodist}}}{P_{\text{US}}}(1850) \approx 0.12$$

representing approximately one-eighth of the American population. The growth mechanism combined the circuit-rider system providing itinerant ministry to frontier populations, the class-meeting weekly small-group discipline providing sustained community formation, and the accessible theological framework combining Arminian free-will theology with perfectionist sanctification doctrine. The [Wigger 1998][book_wigger_1998] Taking Heaven by Storm, [Richey 1991][book_richey_1991] Early American Methodism, and [Andrews 2000][book_andrews_2000] The Methodists and Revolutionary America (already cited) treat the growth pattern.

Baptist growth similarly accelerated through the period, reaching approximately

$$P_{\text{Baptist adherents, US}}(1850) \approx 1.6 \times 10^6$$

across the various Baptist formations including Regular, Separate, Freewill, Primitive, and other branches. The 1814 formation of the General Missionary Convention of the Baptist Denomination in the United States of America (Triennial Convention) provided the first American Baptist denominational structure combining autonomy of local congregations with coordinated missionary activity. The [McBeth 1987][book_mcbeth_1987] The Baptist Heritage and [Leonard 2003][book_leonard_2003] Baptists in America provide the comprehensive treatments.

Presbyterian growth continued through the period but at slower rate than Methodist and Baptist expansion, reaching approximately

$$P_{\text{Presbyterian adherents, US}}(1850) \approx 4.9 \times 10^5$$

with substantial denominational divisions treated below. The 1801 Plan of Union between Congregationalists and Presbyterians provided the cooperative missionary framework for the expansion into the western territories, subsequently dissolved during the 1837 Presbyterian Old School / New School split. The [Longfield 2013][book_longfield_2013] Presbyterians and American Culture, [Marsden 1970][book_marsden_1970] The Evangelical Mind and the New School Presbyterian Experience, and [Trinterud 1949][book_trinterud_1949] The Forming of an American Tradition (already cited) treat the developments.

Congregationalist growth stagnated through the period as the Massachusetts and Connecticut base failed to replicate through frontier expansion under the 1801 Plan of Union that delivered Congregationalist frontier populations into Presbyterian denominational structures. Congregationalist populations reached only approximately

$$P_{\text{Congregationalist adherents, US}}(1850) \approx 2.0 \times 10^5$$

representing the eastern New England base without substantial frontier extension. The [Youngs 1976][book_youngs_1976] The Congregationalists treats the pattern.

Episcopal (formerly Anglican) growth was similarly modest, reaching approximately

$$P_{\text{Episcopal adherents, US}}(1850) \approx 1.4 \times 10^5$$

reflecting the post-Revolutionary institutional reconstruction described in [A268][related_post_a268_awakening_and_founding] and the limited appeal of the liturgical Anglican tradition against the evangelical Methodist and Baptist alternatives on the frontier. The [Mullin 1986][book_mullin_1986] Episcopal Vision and American Reality (already cited) treats the pattern.

## Restoration Movement, Millerites, and Radical Sects

The antebellum period produced substantial religious innovation across formations departing from established denominational traditions. The [Marini 1982][book_marini_1982] Radical Sects of Revolutionary New England (already cited), [Butler 1990][book_butler_1990] (already cited), and [Bushman 2005][book_bushman_2005] Joseph Smith Rough Stone Rolling provide the comprehensive treatments.

The Restoration Movement, initiated by Barton Warren Stone (also of Cane Ridge fame) through the 1804 [Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery][ref_stone_last_will_and_testament_1804] and by Thomas Campbell (1763-1854) through his 1809 [Declaration and Address of the Christian Association of Washington][ref_campbell_declaration_and_address_1809] and his son Alexander Campbell (1788-1866), sought to restore first-century Christianity through rejection of denominational traditions and return to the New Testament as sole authoritative source. The 1832 union at Lexington, Kentucky between the Stone and Campbell movements produced the Christian Church / Disciples of Christ / Churches of Christ tradition that would shape the American frontier religious landscape. The [Foster and Blowers 2004][book_foster_blowers_2004] The Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement and [Hughes 1996][book_hughes_1996] Reviving the Ancient Faith provide the comprehensive treatments.

The Millerite movement, based on William Miller's (1782-1849) biblical prophetic calculations predicting the Second Coming of Christ on dates between March 1843 and October 1844 as set forth in his 1836 [Evidence from Scripture and History of the Second Coming of Christ][ref_miller_evidence_second_coming_1836], produced the most substantial millennial mobilization of the antebellum period. Approximately

$$P_{\text{Millerite adherents, US peak, 1844}} \sim 5 \times 10^4 \text{ to } 1 \times 10^5$$

anticipated the Second Coming on the 22 October 1844 date. The failure of the expected event produced the Great Disappointment. The [Rowe 2008][book_rowe_2008] God's Strange Work and [Numbers and Butler 1987][book_numbers_butler_1987] The Disappointed provide the comprehensive treatments.

Post-Disappointment Millerite reorganization produced successor formations. The Seventh-day Adventist Church, formally organized 1863 under Ellen G. White (1827-1915) and James White (1821-1881) with emphasis on the Saturday sabbath and the investigative judgment doctrine, provided the largest continuing Millerite successor. The [Land 1998][book_land_1998] Adventism in America and [Land 2005][book_land_2005] The A to Z of the Seventh-day Adventists treat the formation. The Advent Christian Church (organized 1860) and other smaller successor bodies provided the alternative continuing formations.

Spiritualism, initiated by the Fox Sisters' 1848 Hydesville rappings and rapidly institutionalized through spiritualist newspapers, conferences, and mediums, produced the alternative religious formation that would attract antebellum women and reform-oriented Americans. The [Braude 1989][book_braude_1989] Radical Spirits and [Cox 2003][book_cox_2003] Body and Soul provide the comprehensive treatments. Spiritualism combined religious innovation with women's rights advocacy, abolitionism, and other reform commitments in ways that produced substantial interconnection with the broader antebellum reform movements.

The [Oneida Community][ref_oneida_community_documents] under John Humphrey Noyes (1811-1886), established 1848 in Oneida, New York, produced the perfectionist communal formation combining Christian perfectionist theology with complex marriage arrangements, communal economy, and eugenic reproduction practices. Comparable pietist communal settlements included the [Shaker Millennial Laws][ref_shaker_millennial_laws] governing the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Appearing and the [Amana Colonies][ref_amana_colonies_documents] of the Community of True Inspiration in Iowa. The community persisted until 1881, subsequently reorganizing as the Oneida Community Ltd. joint-stock company (later Oneida Limited silverware). The [Foster 1981][book_foster_1981] Religion and Sexuality treats the Oneida experiment alongside comparable Shaker and Mormon formations.

## Mormon Founding and Utah Migration

The founding of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints under Joseph Smith (1805-1844) provided the most distinctive American religious formation of the antebellum period and produced the movement that would shape subsequent American religious and political history. The [Bushman 2005][book_bushman_2005] Joseph Smith Rough Stone Rolling, [Arrington 1985][book_arrington_1985] Brigham Young American Moses, [Arrington and Bitton 1979][book_arrington_bitton_1979] The Mormon Experience, [Bowman 2012][book_bowman_2012] The Mormon People, [Turner 2016][book_turner_2016] The Mormon Jesus, [Brodie 1945][book_brodie_1945] No Man Knows My History, [Shipps 1985][book_shipps_1985] Mormonism The Story of a New Religious Tradition, [Givens 2007][book_givens_2007] People of Paradox, [Brooke 1994][book_brooke_1994] The Refiner's Fire, and [Quinn 1998][book_quinn_1998] Early Mormonism and the Magic World View provide the comprehensive treatments.

Joseph Smith, born at Sharon, Vermont, and raised at Palmyra, New York in the burned-over district treated above, reported the 1820 First Vision experience in which the Father and the Son appeared to instruct him that existing denominations were in error. Subsequent visitations from the angel Moroni beginning 1823 led Smith to the location where he reportedly obtained the golden plates that would provide the source for the [Book of Mormon 1830][book_book_of_mormon_1830] [primary text][ref_book_of_mormon_1830] published at Palmyra by E. B. Grandin. Smith's 1842 [Wentworth Letter][ref_wentworth_letter_1842] to John Wentworth of the Chicago Democrat provided the earliest systematic statement of Latter-day Saint doctrine and history intended for external audience.

The 1830 organization of the Church of Christ (later renamed The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints in 1834 and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in 1838) at Fayette, New York provided the institutional founding. The early membership grew rapidly, with

$$P_{\text{LDS members}}(1830) \approx 30, \quad P_{\text{LDS members}}(1844) \approx 2.5 \times 10^4, \quad P_{\text{LDS members}}(1861) \approx 1 \times 10^5$$

through missionary activity to the American northeast, midwest, and British Isles. The 1837 opening of the British mission produced substantial British and later Scandinavian conversions that would provide substantial Utah migration in the following decades.

Mormon communal settlements followed the pattern of formation, conflict with neighbors, and forced removal. Kirtland, Ohio (1831-1838) provided the first substantial Mormon community and the site of the 1836 Kirtland Temple dedication. Independence, Missouri (1831 declared as the New Jerusalem) and subsequent Missouri settlements at Far West and Adam-ondi-Ahman experienced expulsion following the 1838 Mormon War and Governor Boggs's 1838 Extermination Order requiring Mormons to leave Missouri. Nauvoo, Illinois (1839-1846) provided the largest Mormon community reaching approximately fifteen thousand at peak. The June 1844 destruction of the [Nauvoo Expositor][ref_nauvoo_expositor_1844] press by order of the Nauvoo City Council, following the newspaper's single 7 June 1844 issue exposing plural marriage and other doctrinal developments, provided the proximate cause of Smith's arrest at Carthage. The 1844 assassination of Joseph and Hyrum Smith at Carthage Jail marked the violent end of Joseph Smith's leadership.

Brigham Young (1801-1877) subsequently led the 1846-1847 migration of the majority Mormon population to the Great Basin, arriving at the Salt Lake Valley on 24 July 1847. The Deseret settlement expanded rapidly to reach

$$N_{\text{Mormon settlements, 1860}} \approx 3.5 \times 10^2$$

communities across the Great Basin, Colorado Plateau, and southern California by 1860. Nauvoo, Illinois had reached a peak population of

$$P_{\text{Nauvoo, peak}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^4$$

by 1844 before the 1846 evacuation to Utah, providing the staging population for the migration. The compound annual growth rate of Mormon membership from 1830 through 1861 was substantial,

$$r_{\text{LDS growth, 1830 to 1861}} = \frac{\ln(1 \times 10^5 / 30)}{31 \text{ years}} \approx 0.26 \text{ per year}$$

though the earliest years reflect founding-era small-base multiplicative gain. Sustained growth after the 1830s founding period continued at approximately eight to ten percent per year through the antebellum decades, reflecting both the missionary conversion and the high fertility characteristic of Mormon communities. The [Arrington 1985][book_arrington_1985] Brigham Young American Moses and [Bagley 2002][book_bagley_2002] Blood of the Prophets treat the Utah settlement.

Mormon distinctive theology, developed through Joseph Smith's 1830-1844 revelations subsequently canonized in the [Doctrine and Covenants 1835][book_doctrine_covenants_1835] [primary text][ref_doctrine_and_covenants] and the [Pearl of Great Price 1851][book_pearl_of_great_price_1851], included plural marriage (polygamy) revealed to Smith approximately 1831 and formally taught to the inner circle from 1841 forward, eternal marriage sealing, vicarious baptism for the dead, temple ordinances, priesthood organization including Aaronic and Melchizedek priesthoods, plurality of gods doctrine, and human potential for divinity. The [Givens 2009][book_givens_2009] The Book of Mormon A Very Short Introduction and [Turner 2016][book_turner_2016] treat the theological framework.

Plural marriage practice, publicly acknowledged by the Church only in 1852 under Brigham Young after substantial private practice from the 1840s, produced substantial conflict with the American federal government and broader American society. The 1856 Republican Party platform denounced polygamy and slavery as the twin relics of barbarism. The 1862 Morrill Anti-Bigamy Act, 1882 Edmunds Act, 1887 Edmunds-Tucker Act, and 1890 Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints v. United States decision produced the federal legal pressure that resulted in the 1890 Woodruff Manifesto formally suspending Church endorsement of new plural marriages. The practice continued among fundamentalist Mormon splinter groups treated in later articles.

## Irish Catholic Mass Migration

Irish migration to the United States accelerated through the antebellum period from earlier eighteenth-century patterns, producing the Irish Catholic population that would reshape the American religious landscape. The [Kenny 2000][book_kenny_2000] The American Irish (already cited), [Miller 1985][book_miller_1985] Emigrants and Exiles, [Diner 1983][book_diner_1983] Erin's Daughters in America, [Meagher 2005][book_meagher_2005] The Columbia Guide to Irish American History, [Dolan 1985][book_dolan_1985] The American Catholic Experience, [Handlin 1941][book_handlin_1941] Boston's Immigrants, [O Grada 1999][book_ograda_1999] Black '47 and Beyond, [Guinnane 1997][book_guinnane_1997] The Vanishing Irish, and [Dolan 2002][book_dolan_2002] In Search of an American Catholicism provide the comprehensive treatments.

Pre-Famine Irish migration from approximately 1815 through 1845 comprised approximately

$$N_{\text{Irish arrivals, 1815 to 1845}} \approx 8 \times 10^5$$

combining Protestant Scots-Irish (continuing from eighteenth-century patterns treated in [A267][related_post_a267_colonial_folkways] and [A268][related_post_a268_awakening_and_founding]) with substantial Catholic Irish from the southern and western Irish counties. The Catholic proportion increased through the period from approximately one-third to approximately two-thirds of the arriving Irish population per the [Miller 1985][book_miller_1985] treatment.

The Great Famine (An Gorta Mór, the Great Hunger) from 1845 through 1852 produced the catastrophic Irish population loss through starvation and disease combined with substantial emigration. The demographic collapse can be characterized as

$$P_{\text{Ireland}}(1845) \approx 8.5 \times 10^6, \quad P_{\text{Ireland}}(1851) \approx 6.5 \times 10^6, \quad \Delta P \approx -2 \times 10^6$$

with approximately

$$N_{\text{Famine dead}} \approx 1 \times 10^6, \quad N_{\text{Famine emigrated}} \approx 1 \times 10^6$$

during the six-year period. Irish arrivals to the United States during the 1846-1855 period totaled approximately

$$N_{\text{Irish arrivals, 1846 to 1855}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^6$$

producing the single largest immigration wave of the antebellum period and reshaping the urban populations of the Northeast. The [Kinealy 1994][book_kinealy_1994] This Great Calamity and [Gray 1995][book_gray_1995] The Irish Famine treat the Irish context.

Urban concentration of the Irish Catholic immigrant population was substantial. By 1855 approximately

$$f_{\text{Irish-born, NYC 1855}} \approx 0.28, \quad f_{\text{Irish-born, Boston 1855}} \approx 0.35, \quad f_{\text{Irish-born, Philadelphia 1855}} \approx 0.17$$

per the 1855 state census reconstructions in [Ernst 1949][book_ernst_1949] Immigrant Life in New York City. The Irish concentration in the urban labor markets, particularly in domestic service (approximately three-quarters of Irish immigrant women), construction and industrial labor (approximately three-quarters of Irish immigrant men), and transportation-and-service industries produced the occupational patterns that would shape Irish American economic and political development for the following century.

Catholic parochial infrastructure expanded to serve the Irish immigrant population. The creation of substantial new Catholic dioceses (Boston 1808, New York 1808, Philadelphia 1808, Bardstown/Louisville 1808, Charleston 1820, Cincinnati 1821, Richmond 1820, and numerous subsequent additions) provided the institutional framework. The 1852 First Plenary Council of Baltimore under Archbishop Francis Kenrick provided the American Catholic ecclesiastical coordination that would shape the parochial and educational institutional development. The [O'Toole 2008][book_otoole_2008] The Faithful (already cited) and [Hennesey 1981][book_hennesey_1981] American Catholics (already cited) treat the institutional development.

Catholic parochial school formation began during the antebellum period through the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore's 1884 requirement that every Catholic parish establish a parochial school, though substantial pre-1884 parochial schools existed particularly in the Irish and German Catholic communities. The Catholic school system by 1900 would enroll approximately eight hundred thousand students. The [Walch 2003][book_walch_2003] Parish School provides the treatment.

Irish Catholic political mobilization produced the urban political machines that would shape antebellum and postbellum urban politics. The Tammany Hall organization in New York became Irish-controlled by the 1830s and would provide the model for subsequent Irish Catholic urban political machines in Boston, Philadelphia, Chicago, and other cities through the twentieth century. The [Erie 1988][book_erie_1988] Rainbow's End provides the comprehensive treatment.

## German Protestant, Catholic, and Jewish Migration

German-speaking migration to the United States continued through the antebellum period, producing the diverse ethnoreligious formation that combined Lutheran, Reformed, Catholic, Jewish, and Freethinker traditions. The [Faust 1909][book_faust_1909] The German Element in the United States, [Nadel 1990][book_nadel_1990] Little Germany, [Kamphoefner 1987][book_kamphoefner_1987] The Westfalians, [Roeber 1993][book_roeber_1993] Palatines Liberty and Property (already cited), [Conzen 1976][book_conzen_1976] Immigrant Milwaukee, and [Luebke 1974][book_luebke_1974] Bonds of Loyalty provide the comprehensive treatments.

Total German-speaking arrivals across the 1820-1860 period reached approximately

$$N_{\text{German arrivals, 1820 to 1860}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^6$$

concentrated in the 1848-1854 wave that included substantial Forty-Eighter Revolutionary refugees. The religious composition included approximately

$$\begin{aligned}
f_{\text{Lutheran and Reformed}} &\approx 0.55 \\
f_{\text{Catholic}} &\approx 0.35 \\
f_{\text{Jewish}} &\approx 0.05 \\
f_{\text{Freethinker and other}} &\approx 0.05
\end{aligned}$$

per the [Kamphoefner 1987][book_kamphoefner_1987] reconstruction, though substantial regional variation existed.

Lutheran institutional consolidation continued through the period. The 1847 founding of the Lutheran Church-Missouri Synod under Carl F. W. Walther (1811-1887) at Chicago provided the conservative confessional Lutheran denominational structure serving the Saxon immigrant population arriving from 1839 forward under the Martin Stephan leadership. The 1867 founding of the General Council of the Evangelical Lutheran Church in North America provided the alternative moderate Lutheran denominational structure. The [Nelson 1998][book_nelson_1998] The Lutherans in North America (already cited) and [Meyer 1987][book_meyer_1987] Log Cabin to Luther Tower treat the developments.

German Catholic institutional development combined the parochial infrastructure serving Catholic Irish populations with German-language parishes and religious orders serving specifically German-speaking populations. The Society of the Divine Word (arrived 1875), Redemptorists (arrived 1839), German Franciscans, and other religious orders provided the German-Catholic ministerial infrastructure. The Catholic Central Verein of America (founded 1855) provided the German Catholic lay organization coordinating mutual aid, insurance, and advocacy activities. The [Barry 1953][book_barry_1953] The Catholic Church and German Americans treats the development.

German Jewish migration from approximately 1830 forward produced the American Reform Jewish tradition that would reshape American Judaism. Isaac Mayer Wise (1819-1900), arriving 1846 and settling at Albany then Cincinnati, provided the institutional leadership. His 1854 assumption of the Cincinnati pulpit at B'nai Yeshurun, his 1855 organization of the Cleveland Conference proposing Union of American Israelites, his 1873 organization of the Union of American Hebrew Congregations, his 1875 founding of Hebrew Union College at Cincinnati, and his 1889 organization of the Central Conference of American Rabbis provided the institutional foundation for American Reform Judaism. The [Sarna 2004][book_sarna_2004] American Judaism (already cited), [Wise 1901][book_wise_1901] Reminiscences, and [Meyer 1988][book_meyer_1988] Response to Modernity treat the developments.

The 1885 Pittsburgh Platform under Isaac Mayer Wise, Kaufmann Kohler, and colleagues provided the classical Reform Jewish theological statement, though this document falls somewhat outside the pre-1861 window of this article and receives fuller treatment in article five. The [Meyer 1988][book_meyer_1988] treats the document.

B'nai B'rith, founded 1843 in New York by Henry Jones and eleven other German Jewish immigrants, provided the first American Jewish fraternal and mutual-aid organization. Isaac Leeser's 1843 founding of [The Occident and American Jewish Advocate][ref_leeser_occident_1843] at Philadelphia provided the first sustained American Jewish periodical. The 1854 Cleveland lodge, subsequent national organization, and extension of the mutual-benefit and philanthropic activities provided the institutional infrastructure that would shape American Jewish communal life. The [Grusd 1966][book_grusd_1966] B'nai B'rith and [Diner 1992][book_diner_1992] A Time for Gathering treat the formation.

Freethinker refugees from the 1848 European revolutions produced substantial German-speaking populations committed to rationalist, secular, and radical political positions. The Freethinker communities in Milwaukee, Cincinnati, St. Louis, and other Midwestern cities established Freidenker halls, German-language newspapers, and radical political organizations that shaped German-American cultural life. The [Wittke 1952][book_wittke_1952] Refugees of Revolution treats the formation.

## Early Chinese Migration to the West Coast

Chinese migration to the United States began with the 1849 California Gold Rush and continued through the subsequent decades until the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act treated in article five halted the migration. The [Chan 1991][book_chan_1991] Asian Americans, [Kwong and Miscevic 2005][book_kwong_miscevic_2005] Chinese America, [Yung 1995][book_yung_1995] Unbound Feet, [Takaki 1989][book_takaki_1989] Strangers from a Different Shore, [Chang 2003][book_chang_2003] The Chinese in America, [Sandmeyer 1939][book_sandmeyer_1939] The Anti-Chinese Movement in California, and [Lee 2003][book_lee_2003] At America's Gates provide the comprehensive treatments.

Chinese arrivals during the 1849-1861 period totaled approximately

$$N_{\text{Chinese arrivals, 1849 to 1861}} \approx 4.4 \times 10^4$$

concentrated in California mining regions and San Francisco. The overwhelming majority came from the Pearl River Delta region of Guangdong Province, particularly the Sze Yup (Four Districts) and Sam Yup (Three Districts) areas, producing the Cantonese-speaking American Chinese formation that would shape American Chinese cultural and religious life. The [Chen 2000][book_chen_2000] Chinese San Francisco and [Nee and Nee 1972][book_nee_nee_1972] Longtime Californ treat the San Francisco development.

Religious traditions among the arriving Chinese included substantial combination of Confucian ethical and ancestral practice, Buddhist devotional practice (particularly Pure Land Buddhism through the Amitabha and Kuan Yin devotion), Daoist ritual practice, and folk religious practice centered on deities including Tin Hau (Mazu, patroness of seafarers), Guan Yu (deified general and patron of merchants and secret societies), and ancestral spirits. The Chinese temples established in San Francisco from 1852 forward including the Tin Hau Temple (Waverly Place, 1852) and subsequent temples provided the institutional infrastructure. The [Chen 2000][book_chen_2000] and [Fessler 1983][book_fessler_1983] Chinese in America treat the religious formation.

The Chinese Six Companies (Chinese Consolidated Benevolent Association), organized 1854 in San Francisco as the coordinating body of the district (huiguan) and family (hui) associations, provided the institutional infrastructure for the Chinese American community. The organization combined mutual-aid, dispute-resolution, and advocacy functions with connection to the homeland communities through the credit-ticket system that financed most Chinese immigration. The [Lai 2004][book_lai_2004] Becoming Chinese American treats the institutional formation.

Anti-Chinese violence and legal disabilities emerged rapidly following the initial migration. The 1850 California Foreign Miners Tax specifically targeted Chinese miners with the twenty-dollars-per-month tax that reduced their mining participation. The 1854 People v. Hall California Supreme Court decision extended the existing prohibition on Native American and African American testimony against white defendants to include Chinese, denying Chinese Americans legal protection against violence by whites. The pattern of anti-Chinese mob violence at mining camps and urban locations produced substantial Chinese casualties across the antebellum and postbellum periods. The [Pfaelzer 2007][book_pfaelzer_2007] Driven Out treats the violence pattern.

Central Pacific Railroad Chinese labor from 1865 forward produced the largest single Chinese employment during the transcontinental railroad construction. Approximately

$$N_{\text{Chinese railroad workers, Central Pacific 1865 to 1869}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^4, \quad f_{\text{Central Pacific workforce Chinese}} \approx 0.90$$

comprised approximately ninety percent of the Central Pacific workforce. The Chinese contribution to the 1869 completion of the transcontinental railroad at Promontory Summit, Utah, though unrecognized in contemporary and subsequent historiography, provided the critical labor without which the railroad completion timeline would have been impossible. The [Chang 2019][book_chang_2019] Ghosts of Gold Mountain provides the modern treatment.

## Nativism and the Know-Nothing Movement

Antebellum nativism produced substantial anti-Catholic and anti-immigrant mobilization that would culminate in the Know-Nothing (American Party) movement of the 1850s. The [Higham 1955][book_higham_1955] Strangers in the Land (already cited), [Anbinder 1992][book_anbinder_1992] Nativism and Slavery, [Billington 1938][book_billington_1938] The Protestant Crusade, [Knobel 1996][book_knobel_1996] America for the Americans, [Gienapp 1987][book_gienapp_1987] The Origins of the Republican Party, and [Holt 1978][book_holt_1978] The Political Crisis of the 1850s provide the comprehensive treatments.

The [Ursuline Convent burning at Charlestown, Massachusetts][ref_charlestown_ursuline_convent_burning_1834] on 11 August 1834 by a Protestant mob provided the inflection point for antebellum anti-Catholic mobilization. Samuel F. B. Morse's 1834 [Foreign Conspiracy Against the Liberties of the United States][ref_morse_foreign_conspiracy_1834], published serially in the New-York Observer, provided the systematic conspiratorial framework alleging papal and Habsburg designs on American institutions. The incident, provoked by sensational lecture tours by former nuns (particularly Rebecca Reed and later Maria Monk) and by the broader Protestant fear of specifically expanding Catholic populations, produced the model of anti-Catholic mob violence that would recur through the subsequent decades. The [Franchot 1994][book_franchot_1994] Roads to Rome and [Cohen 2015][book_cohen_2015] The Ursuline Convent Burning treat the episode.

The 1836 publication of [Monk 1836][book_monk_1836] [Awful Disclosures of the Hotel Dieu Nunnery][ref_awful_disclosures_of_maria_monk_1836], a fictional convent narrative that historians have subsequently established as fraudulent, provided the most widely-circulated anti-Catholic text of the antebellum period, selling approximately three hundred thousand copies before the Civil War. The work established the anti-Catholic pornographic-conspiracy genre that would recur through subsequent nativist literature. The [Franchot 1994][book_franchot_1994] treatment covers the reception.

The [Philadelphia Bible Riots of 1844][ref_philadelphia_nativist_riots_1844], occurring in May and July, produced the most substantial anti-Catholic mob violence of the antebellum period. The conflict arose from the dispute over Catholic children in Philadelphia public schools being permitted to use the Douay Bible rather than the King James Bible for the mandatory school Bible reading. The May 1844 riots and July 1844 riots killed approximately

$$N_{\text{Philadelphia Bible Riots dead}} \approx 20, \quad N_{\text{Catholic churches burned}} = 2$$

with substantial additional property damage. The St. Michael's and St. Augustine's Catholic churches were burned by Protestant mobs. The [Feldberg 1975][book_feldberg_1975] The Philadelphia Riots of 1844 provides the comprehensive treatment.

The 1849 founding of the Order of the Star Spangled Banner in New York by Charles B. Allen provided the secret nativist organization that would expand into the American Party. The requirement that members respond to questions about the organization with I know nothing produced the popular Know-Nothing designation. The 1854 emergence of the American Party as public political vehicle produced substantial electoral success, with the Party electing governors of Massachusetts, Delaware, Rhode Island, New Hampshire, and Kentucky, and substantial Congressional representation. The [Anbinder 1992][book_anbinder_1992] provides the comprehensive treatment.

American Party political program combined anti-Catholic anti-immigrant provisions (extending naturalization waiting periods to twenty-one years, restricting officeholding to native-born Protestants, imposing Bible reading in public schools using the Protestant version) with broader nativist commitments. The 1856 Millard Fillmore American Party presidential campaign captured approximately

$$f_{\text{Fillmore popular vote, 1856}} \approx 0.22$$

representing substantial national nativist support before the 1857-1860 party disintegration under the pressure of slavery-related sectional divisions. The subsequent absorption of Northern Know-Nothings into the Republican Party and Southern Know-Nothings into the Constitutional Union Party marked the end of the antebellum nativist party organization.

## Abolitionism as Religious Movement

Abolitionism emerged in the antebellum period as a religious movement whose evangelical Protestant theological framework, voluntary-society institutional infrastructure, and direct-action tactics were shaped by the Second Great Awakening treated above. The [Sinha 2016][book_sinha_2016] The Slave's Cause (already cited), [Foner 1970][book_foner_1970] Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men, [Stewart 1976][book_stewart_1976] Holy Warriors, [Mayer 1998][book_mayer_1998] All on Fire, [Blight 2018][book_blight_2018] Frederick Douglass, [Sinha 2020][book_sinha_2020] The Rise and Fall of the Second American Republic, [Kraditor 1969][book_kraditor_1969] Means and Ends in American Abolitionism, [Jeffrey 1998][book_jeffrey_1998] The Great Silent Army of Abolitionism, and [Wyatt-Brown 1969][book_wyatt_brown_1969] Lewis Tappan and the Evangelical War Against Slavery provide the comprehensive treatments.

William Lloyd Garrison (1805-1879), Boston-based abolitionist editor and organizer, provided the most prominent radical abolitionist leadership. Garrison's 1 January 1831 first issue of [The Liberator][book_liberator_1831] [periodical archive][ref_liberator_periodical_1831] and 1833 founding of the American Anti-Slavery Society at Philadelphia, producing the [Declaration of Sentiments of the American Anti-Slavery Convention][ref_american_anti_slavery_declaration_1833] under Garrison's authorship, provided the institutional foundation for immediatist abolition (demanding immediate rather than gradual emancipation). The 1837 murder of Elijah Lovejoy at Alton, Illinois for his abolitionist newspaper publication provided the first American abolitionist martyrdom. The [Mayer 1998][book_mayer_1998] All on Fire provides the Garrison biography.

Frederick Douglass (approximately 1817-1895), formerly enslaved, provided the most influential Black abolitionist leadership. His 1845 [Douglass 1845][book_douglass_1845] [Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass][ref_douglass_narrative_1845] and 1855 revised and expanded [My Bondage and My Freedom][ref_douglass_my_bondage_1855] provided the canonical slave narrative that mobilized Northern antislavery sentiment. His subsequent 1847 founding of [The North Star][ref_north_star_frederick_douglass_paper] newspaper at Rochester, participation in Seneca Falls Convention 1848, 1852 [What to the Slave is the Fourth of July][ref_douglass_fourth_of_july_1852] address at Rochester, and decades of speaking and organizing established Douglass as the most prominent African American public figure of the nineteenth century. The [Blight 2018][book_blight_2018] Frederick Douglass Prophet of Freedom provides the definitive modern biography.

The Grimké sisters (Sarah 1792-1873 and Angelina 1805-1879), formerly slaveholders themselves from Charleston, South Carolina, converted to Quakerism and moved to Philadelphia, then became prominent abolitionist speakers whose public female speaking advanced both abolition and women's rights. Angelina Grimké's 1836 [Appeal to the Christian Women of the South][ref_grimke_appeal_1836] and Sarah Grimké's 1838 [Letters on the Equality of the Sexes][ref_grimke_letters_on_equality_1838] provided the systematic textual foundation. Their 1837-1838 speaking tour of New England produced substantial controversy and mobilized women's rights concerns alongside the abolitionist message. The [Lerner 1998][book_lerner_1998] The Grimké Sisters from South Carolina provides the treatment.

Theodore Dwight Weld (1803-1895), converted under Charles Finney's revival preaching, provided the most influential organizational leadership of the antebellum abolitionist movement. Weld's 1834 Lane Debates at Lane Theological Seminary (under Lyman Beecher noted above), 1836 founding of the Anti-Slavery Society of Ohio, 1839 [Weld 1839][book_weld_1839] [American Slavery As It Is][ref_weld_american_slavery_as_it_is_1839] compendium, and systematic mobilization of the Ohio antislavery constituency provided the Western abolitionist organizational base. The [Abzug 1980][book_abzug_1980] Passionate Liberator treats the Weld biography.

Harriet Beecher Stowe (1811-1896), daughter of Lyman Beecher and wife of Calvin Stowe, produced the most widely-influential antebellum abolitionist text. Her 1852 [Stowe 1852][book_stowe_1852] [Uncle Tom's Cabin][ref_stowe_uncle_toms_cabin_1852] sold approximately

$$N_{\text{Uncle Tom's Cabin US sales, first year}} \approx 3 \times 10^5$$

establishing the book as the bestseller of the nineteenth century. The text mobilized substantial Northern antislavery sentiment through its narrative treatment of slavery's moral impact on both enslaved and enslaver. Lincoln's documented (though possibly apocryphal) 1862 greeting of Stowe as the little woman who made this great war captures the cultural understanding of Stowe's political impact. The [Hedrick 1994][book_hedrick_1994] Harriet Beecher Stowe treats the biography.

John Brown (1800-1859) provided the most militant antebellum abolitionist leadership through his 1856 Pottawatomie Massacre of proslavery settlers in Kansas, his October [1859 raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia][ref_harpers_ferry_raid_1859], and his subsequent trial, conviction, and execution on 2 December 1859. Brown's religious framing of his violent action within the evangelical theological framework shaped subsequent American religious debate over the legitimacy of political violence. The [Reynolds 2005][book_reynolds_2005] John Brown Abolitionist provides the biography.

The Underground Railroad, the informal network of routes and safe houses assisting specifically escaped enslaved persons in reaching the Northern states or Canada, operated through the antebellum period with substantial participation from Quaker, African Methodist Episcopal, and other religious communities. The estimates of persons assisted through the network range widely, with

$$N_{\text{Underground Railroad assisted, 1830 to 1861}} \sim 3 \times 10^4 \text{ to } 1 \times 10^5$$

per the [Bordewich 2005][book_bordewich_2005] Bound for Canaan and [Foner 2015][book_foner_2015] Gateway to Freedom treatments. Harriet Tubman (approximately 1822-1913), formerly enslaved, provided the most prominent African American Underground Railroad conductor through her approximately thirteen return trips to Maryland leading approximately seventy enslaved persons to freedom.

## Southern Proslavery Theology

Southern white religious defense of slavery provided the theological counterpoint to abolitionist mobilization and produced substantial literature developing the proslavery position. The [Genovese 1998][book_genovese_1998] A Consuming Fire, [Snay 1993][book_snay_1993] Gospel of Disunion, [Noll 2006][book_noll_2006] The Civil War as a Theological Crisis, [Faust 1981][book_faust_1981] The Ideology of Slavery, [Tise 1987][book_tise_1987] Proslavery, and [Fox-Genovese and Genovese 2005][book_fox_genovese_genovese_2005] The Mind of the Master Class provide the comprehensive treatments.

Biblical defense of slavery centered on proslavery interpretation of the Curse of Ham (Genesis 9:20-27), Old Testament Hebrew slavery provisions, New Testament apostolic silence on slavery, and Pauline exhortations to slave obedience. The proslavery biblical arguments exploited the literal-hermeneutical tradition that American Protestantism had inherited from Reformation antecedents. The [Haynes 2002][book_haynes_2002] Noah's Curse and [Harrill 2006][book_harrill_2006] Slaves in the New Testament treat the biblical interpretations.

Thornton Stringfellow (1788-1869), Baptist minister at Culpeper, Virginia, produced the most systematic antebellum biblical defense of slavery. His 1841 [Stringfellow 1841][book_stringfellow_1841] A Brief Examination of Scripture Testimony on the Institution of Slavery and subsequent works provided the canonical Southern Baptist proslavery position. The [Rice 1849][book_rice_1849] A Debate on Slavery preserves the Rice-Blanchard debate that established the systematic proslavery-antislavery theological confrontation.

James Henley Thornwell (1812-1862), Presbyterian minister and President of South Carolina College, provided the most systematic Southern Presbyterian proslavery theological framework. His 1850 [Thornwell 1850][book_thornwell_1850] Rights and Duties of Masters and subsequent works integrated the proslavery position with the broader Southern Presbyterian confessional Calvinist theology. Thornwell's 1861 role in organizing the Presbyterian Church in the Confederate States of America (later Presbyterian Church in the United States) provided the institutional culmination. The [Farmer 1986][book_farmer_1986] The Metaphysical Confederacy treats the Thornwell theology.

Southern proslavery theology combined biblical defense with paternalist framing of slavery as beneficial to enslaved persons through Christianization, patriarchal household ordering, and protection from the alleged evils of the Northern industrial wage-labor system. George Fitzhugh (1806-1881), Virginia proslavery theorist, produced the most extreme proslavery articulation in his 1854 [Fitzhugh 1854][book_fitzhugh_1854] Sociology for the South and 1857 [Fitzhugh 1857][book_fitzhugh_1857] Cannibals All!, which argued that slavery was preferable to free labor and advocated the extension of slavery to poor whites. The [Wish 1943][book_wish_1943] George Fitzhugh treats the figure.

The Southern proslavery religious position shaped the denominational splits treated below and provided the theological foundation for the Confederate religious self-understanding that would shape the Civil War and postbellum religious history treated in article five.

## Denominational Splits Preceding the Political Split

The antebellum denominational splits along North-South lines provided the institutional foreshadowing of the political split that would produce the Civil War. The [Ahlstrom 1972][book_ahlstrom_1972] (already cited), [Goen 1985][book_goen_1985] Broken Churches Broken Nation, and [Noll 2006][book_noll_2006] The Civil War as a Theological Crisis provide the comprehensive treatments.

The [1837 Presbyterian Old School / New School split][ref_old_school_new_school_split_1837], though initially about theological questions concerning the role of the 1801 Plan of Union with Congregationalists, reflected North-South tensions over slavery and reform movements. The Old School (predominantly Southern) maintained confessional Calvinist orthodoxy and opposition to abolitionism. The New School (predominantly Northern) accommodated Congregationalist influence and abolitionist commitments. The reunion of Old and New School branches in 1869 (in the North) and 1864 (in the South) would occur after the Civil War produced additional Presbyterian denominational reorganization.

The 1844 Methodist Episcopal Church split, provoked by the General Conference decision that Bishop James Osgood Andrew of Georgia could not exercise episcopal functions while he owned slaves, produced the [Plan of Separation][ref_methodist_plan_of_separation_1844] and the formation of the Methodist Episcopal Church, South at the 1845 Louisville Convention. The split divided the largest American denomination on North-South lines and provided the most substantial denominational division of the antebellum period. The reunion would not occur until 1939 with the formation of The Methodist Church, and full reunification with the United Brethren tradition only in 1968 with the United Methodist Church formation. The [Mathews 1965][book_mathews_1965] Slavery and Methodism treats the split.

The 1845 Baptist split, provoked by the refusal of the Baptist Home Mission Society to appoint slaveholder missionaries, produced the founding of the [Southern Baptist Convention][ref_southern_baptist_convention_1845] at Augusta, Georgia in May 1845. The Southern Baptist Convention would subsequently become the largest single Protestant denomination in the United States by the mid-twentieth century, reaching approximately

$$P_{\text{Southern Baptist Convention}}(2020) \approx 1.4 \times 10^7$$

though with substantial subsequent decline through the 2020s treated in later articles. The 1995 Southern Baptist Convention formal repudiation of the 1845 founding's proslavery grounding marked the denominational acknowledgment of the historical error. The [Leonard 2003][book_leonard_2003] Baptists in America (already cited) and [Barnes 1954][book_barnes_1954] The Southern Baptist Convention treat the developments.

The 1861 Presbyterian Church in the Confederate States of America organization (subsequently renamed Presbyterian Church in the United States and often called Southern Presbyterian) provided the third major Northern-Southern denominational split immediately preceding the outbreak of the Civil War. The denomination would reunite with the Northern Presbyterian Church in 1983 as the Presbyterian Church (USA). The [Thompson 1963][book_thompson_1963] Presbyterians in the South treats the history.

The antebellum denominational splits along North-South lines cluster in the 1837-1861 interval,

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Presbyterian Old School / New School} &: 1837 \\
\text{Methodist Episcopal / Methodist Episcopal Church South} &: 1844\text{-}1845 \\
\text{Baptist / Southern Baptist Convention} &: 1845 \\
\text{Presbyterian US / Presbyterian CS} &: 1861
\end{aligned}$$

preceding the political split of 1861 by intervals ranging from zero years (Presbyterian US/CS) to twenty-four years (Presbyterian Old/New School). Henry Clay's 1855 observation attributed to Calhoun that the denominational splits foreshadowed the political division was prescient. The religious dimensions of the political split are treated more fully in article five.

## Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears

The [Indian Removal Act of 1830][ref_indian_removal_act_1830] signed by President Andrew Jackson and subsequent implementation through treaties and coercion produced the forced removal of approximately

$$N_{\text{Native persons removed east of Mississippi, 1830 to 1850}} \approx 6 \times 10^4$$

from their ancestral lands east of the Mississippi River to Indian Territory (later Oklahoma) west of the Mississippi. The [Saunt 2020][book_saunt_2020] Unworthy Republic, [Wallace 1993][book_wallace_1993] The Long, Bitter Trail, [Anderson 1991_removal][book_anderson_1991_removal] Cherokee Removal, [Perdue and Green 2007][book_perdue_green_2007] The Cherokee Nation and the Trail of Tears, [Ehle 1988][book_ehle_1988] Trail of Tears, [Foreman 1932][book_foreman_1932] Indian Removal, [Satz 1975][book_satz_1975] American Indian Policy in the Jacksonian Era, [Inskeep 2015][book_inskeep_2015] Jacksonland, and [Remini 2001][book_remini_2001] Andrew Jackson and His Indian Wars provide the comprehensive treatments.

The 1830 Indian Removal Act, enacted 28 May 1830 by a narrow margin (28-19 in Senate, 102-97 in House), authorized the President to negotiate removal treaties with Native peoples east of the Mississippi. Jackson's 1829 first annual message advocated removal as in the Native peoples' interest, though the historical assessment of the motivation identifies the settler pressure for the Native lands (particularly the gold-bearing Cherokee lands in northern Georgia) and Southern political interest as the substantial drivers. The [Anderson 1991_removal][book_anderson_1991_removal] treatment covers the political process.

The Cherokee Nation resistance produced the most substantial legal challenge to removal. The [Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831)][ref_cherokee_nation_v_georgia_1831] Supreme Court decision under Chief Justice John Marshall declared the Cherokee a "domestic dependent nation" without standing to sue in federal court. The 1832 [Worcester v. Georgia][ref_worcester_v_georgia_1832] decision reversed this by holding that the Georgia state jurisdiction did not extend into the Cherokee Nation. Jackson's reported response "John Marshall has made his decision, now let him enforce it" (probably apocryphal but capturing the dynamic) reflects the federal executive refusal to enforce the Supreme Court decision protecting Cherokee sovereignty.

The 1835 [Treaty of New Echota][ref_treaty_of_new_echota_1835], signed by a minority faction of the Cherokee including Major Ridge, John Ridge, and Elias Boudinot without the authorization of the Cherokee National Council or Principal Chief John Ross, provided the fraudulent legal basis for Cherokee removal. Approximately

$$N_{\text{Cherokee Treaty of New Echota signers}} \approx 100, \quad P_{\text{Cherokee opposing treaty}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^4$$

The opposition of the overwhelming Cherokee majority was expressed through the Ross-led Cherokee National Council petition to Congress opposing the treaty, but the Senate ratified the treaty by a single vote in 1836. The 1838-1839 forced removal (Trail of Tears) killed approximately

$$N_{\text{Cherokee dead on Trail of Tears}} \approx 4 \times 10^3, \quad \frac{N_{\text{dead}}}{P_{\text{Cherokee removed}}} \approx 0.25$$

representing approximately one-fourth of the Cherokee population removed. The execution of the Ridges and Boudinot in 1839 by Cherokee traditionalists at Tahlequah in Indian Territory marked the violent resolution of the removal-era Cherokee political conflict. The [Perdue and Green 2007][book_perdue_green_2007] provides the comprehensive treatment.

Choctaw removal from Mississippi under the 1830 Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek produced the first implementation of the Removal Act. Approximately

$$N_{\text{Choctaw removed, 1831 to 1833}} \approx 1.3 \times 10^4, \quad N_{\text{Choctaw dead}} \sim 2.5 \times 10^3$$

The removal established the administrative and logistical precedent for subsequent removals. The [Debo 1934][book_debo_1934] The Rise and Fall of the Choctaw Republic treats the process.

Chickasaw removal from Mississippi and Alabama under the 1832 and 1834 treaties produced comparatively lower casualties through the Chickasaw negotiation of better financial terms. Approximately

$$N_{\text{Chickasaw removed, 1837 to 1838}} \approx 4 \times 10^3$$

reached Indian Territory. The [Gibson 1971][book_gibson_1971] The Chickasaws treats the removal.

Creek removal from Alabama and Georgia under the 1832 Treaty of Cusseta produced substantial violence including the 1836 Creek War with additional casualties. Approximately

$$N_{\text{Creek removed, 1834 to 1837}} \approx 2 \times 10^4, \quad N_{\text{Creek dead}} \sim 3.5 \times 10^3$$

The [Green 1982][book_green_1982] The Politics of Indian Removal treats the Creek removal.

Seminole resistance produced the most substantial armed opposition to removal. The Second Seminole War (1835-1842) can be characterized as

$$t_{\text{Second Seminole War}} = 7 \text{ years}, \quad N_{\text{US soldier dead}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^3, \quad C_{\text{federal cost}} \approx 4 \times 10^7 \text{ USD}$$

making it the most expensive Indian war in United States history to that date. The war ended with approximately

$$N_{\text{Seminole removed to Indian Territory}} \approx 3 \times 10^3, \quad N_{\text{Seminole remaining in Florida}} \approx 5 \times 10^2$$

with the remaining Seminole population continuing in the Everglades and eventually forming the modern Seminole Tribe of Florida and Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida. The [Missall and Missall 2004][book_missall_missall_2004] The Seminole Wars treats the conflict.

## Cherokee Syncretic Christianity and Sequoyah's Syllabary

The Cherokee adaptation strategy in the decades before removal produced substantial religious, educational, and political innovation that would shape Cherokee and broader Native religious history. The [McLoughlin 1984][book_mcloughlin_1984] The Cherokees and Christianity (already cited), [McLoughlin 1986][book_mcloughlin_1986] Cherokee Renascence, [Perdue 1998][book_perdue_1998] Cherokee Women (already cited), [Wilkins 1970][book_wilkins_1970] Cherokee Tragedy, [Conley 2007][book_conley_2007] A Cherokee Encyclopedia, and [Champagne 1992][book_champagne_1992] Social Order and Political Change provide the comprehensive treatments.

Sequoyah (approximately 1770-1843), also known as George Guess, produced the Cherokee syllabary approximately 1821, providing the first indigenously-developed writing system for a North American Native language. The syllabary comprises

$$N_{\text{Sequoyah syllabary characters}} = 85, \quad t_{\text{Sequoyah syllabary development}} \approx 12 \text{ years}$$

and permitted widespread Cherokee literacy within a short period after adoption, with

$$f_{\text{Cherokee literate in syllabary by 1830}} \sim 0.5 \text{ to } 0.9$$

per contemporary estimates. The rapid adoption reflected Cherokee cultural investment in literacy and written record. The [Cushman 2011][book_cushman_2011] The Cherokee Syllabary treats the development.

The Cherokee Phoenix, the first Native American newspaper, began publication 21 February 1828 at New Echota, Georgia. The bilingual English-Cherokee newspaper provided the first vehicle for Cherokee political and cultural communication both within the Cherokee Nation and to external audiences. The paper's publication was suppressed by Georgia authorities during the removal crisis, resuming under different names in Indian Territory. The [Perdue 1996][book_perdue_1996] Cherokee Editor treats the history.

The Cherokee institutional development can be characterized as the rapid sequence

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Sequoyah syllabary completed} &: 1821 \\
\text{Cherokee National Council reorganized} &: 1827 \\
\text{Cherokee Constitution ratified} &: 26 \text{ July } 1827 \\
\text{Cherokee Phoenix first issue} &: 21 \text{ February } 1828 \\
\text{Indian Removal Act} &: 1830 \\
\text{Worcester v. Georgia} &: 1832 \\
\text{Treaty of New Echota} &: 1835 \\
\text{Trail of Tears} &: 1838\text{-}1839
\end{aligned}$$

The 1827 Cherokee Constitution, modeled on the United States Constitution, established the Cherokee National Council, elected Principal Chief, and judicial system providing the formal Cherokee governance structure. The constitution's provisions restricting citizenship to Cherokee descent, Christian religious commitments among officeholders, and slavery accommodation reflect the complex Cherokee accommodation to American cultural and political patterns. The [McLoughlin 1986][book_mcloughlin_1986] treatment covers the development.

Cherokee Christian conversion proceeded through the antebellum decades. The Moravian mission at Spring Place from 1801, the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions Congregationalist and Presbyterian missions from 1817, and the Baptist missions from 1820 produced substantial Cherokee conversions. Samuel Worcester (1798-1859), Congregationalist missionary at New Echota and later Park Hill in Indian Territory, provided the most substantial missionary presence and defender of Cherokee sovereignty against Georgia jurisdiction (the plaintiff in Worcester v. Georgia noted above). Worcester's 1828 arrest and imprisonment by Georgia authorities and Supreme Court appeal established the institutional-legal issue at stake in removal. The [Bass 1936][book_bass_1936] Cherokee Messenger treats the Worcester biography.

Cherokee religious syncretism combined Christian conversion with substantial continuation of Cherokee traditional religious practices including Green Corn Ceremony, stomp dance, traditional healing practices, and specifically Cherokee cosmological framework. The pattern would persist through the removal, Indian Territory period, and subsequent Cherokee history into the present. The [Fogelson 1961][book_fogelson_1961] Cherokee Notions of Power treats the traditional religious continuity.

## Continued Missionary Movements to Native Peoples

Missionary activity to Native peoples continued through the antebellum period, expanding beyond the pre-Revolutionary patterns treated in earlier articles to include Western missions to Plains and Pacific Northwest peoples. The [Berkhofer 1965][book_berkhofer_1965] Salvation and the Savage (already cited), [Bowden 1981][book_bowden_1981] American Indians and Christian Missions, and [Beidler 2013][book_beidler_2013] Murder on the Reservation provide the comprehensive treatments.

The [American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions][ref_abcfm_1810], founded 1810 by Congregationalists, Presbyterians, and Dutch Reformed leaders, provided the dominant nondenominational Protestant missionary organization operating among Native peoples. The ABCFM missions to the Cherokee (from 1817), Choctaw (from 1818), Chickasaw (from 1819), and Creek (from 1822) in the Southeast, and subsequent missions to Pacific Northwest peoples treated below, provided the Protestant missionary infrastructure that would shape the nineteenth-century Native religious formation. The [Andrew 1976][book_andrew_1976] Rebuilding the Christian Commonwealth treats the ABCFM history.

Marcus Whitman (1802-1847) and Narcissa Prentiss Whitman (1808-1847) established the 1836 Waiilatpu mission to the Cayuse near present Walla Walla, Washington. The mission became the most prominent Pacific Northwest Protestant mission and provided the way-station for Oregon Trail settlers from approximately 1843 forward. The November 1847 Whitman Massacre, in which

$$N_{\text{Whitman Massacre dead}} \approx 14, \quad f_{\text{Cayuse population dead from 1847 measles}} \approx 0.5$$

with the approximately half of the Cayuse population killed by the 1847 measles epidemic providing the proximate motivation, marked the end of the Waiilatpu mission and produced the 1848 Cayuse War. The [Jeffrey 1991][book_jeffrey_1991] Converting the West and [Jetté 2004][book_jette_2004] At the Hearth of the Crossed Races treat the mission.

Jesuit missions to the Pacific Northwest and Rocky Mountain peoples under Pierre-Jean De Smet (1801-1873) from 1840 forward produced the Catholic missionary presence to the Salish, Coeur d'Alene, Blackfoot, and other peoples. De Smet's extensive travels, his negotiating role in treaties, and his advocacy for Native peoples with the federal government shaped the Catholic Native mission tradition. The [Killoren 1994][book_killoren_1994] Come, Blackrobe treats the De Smet biography.

Mission schools and later boarding schools expanded through the antebellum period. Approximately

$$N_{\text{Native mission schools, US, 1860}} \approx 3.5 \times 10^2$$

operated across mission and reservation locations by 1860. The institutional pattern would expand through the late nineteenth century under the federal boarding school policy treated in article six. The [Adams 1995][book_adams_1995] Education for Extinction treats the subsequent boarding school system.

The 1849 transfer of the Bureau of Indian Affairs from the War Department to the newly-created Department of the Interior marked the institutional reorganization of federal Native policy. The President Ulysses Grant's 1869 Peace Policy would subsequently distribute Indian agencies among Protestant denominations for administration, producing the direct federal-religious institutional partnership that would shape late nineteenth-century Native experience. The [Prucha 1976][book_prucha_1976] American Indian Policy in Crisis treats the developments.

## African American Religious Formation Under Slavery

African American religious formation under slavery continued the patterns established in the colonial period treated in [A267][related_post_a267_colonial_folkways] and [A268][related_post_a268_awakening_and_founding] while expanding through the Second Great Awakening. The [Raboteau 1978][book_raboteau_1978] Slave Religion (already cited), [Genovese 1974][book_genovese_1974] Roll Jordan Roll (already cited), [Blassingame 1972][book_blassingame_1972] The Slave Community (already cited), [Sinha 2016][book_sinha_2016] (already cited), [Levine 1977][book_levine_1977] Black Culture and Black Consciousness, [Frey 1991][book_frey_1991] Water from the Rock (already cited), and [Boles 1988][book_boles_1988] Masters and Slaves in the House of the Lord provide the comprehensive treatments.

The extension of Baptist and Methodist evangelical missions to enslaved populations during the Second Great Awakening increased the enslaved population's Christian identification. Approximately

$$f_{\text{enslaved Christian identification}}(1860) \approx 0.30 \text{ to } 0.50$$

per estimates, though the quantitative reconstruction faces substantial methodological difficulty given the documentary limitations. The formal Christian identification combined with traditional African religious retention producing the documented syncretic patterns treated in the previous articles.

The ring shout, hush harbor, and invisible institution patterns treated in [A267][related_post_a267_colonial_folkways] continued through the antebellum period. The ring shout ceremonies documented across the Sea Islands and low country continued and spread to other Southern regions through the enslaved population's mobility patterns. The hush harbor tradition of secret religious meetings outside slaveholder surveillance provided the location for autonomous religious life that combined Christian and African elements. The [Wilmore 1998][book_wilmore_1998] Black Religion and Black Radicalism treats the patterns.

Slaveholder-authorized religious instruction of enslaved persons expanded through the antebellum period, particularly after the Nat Turner rebellion treated below produced slaveholder anxiety about unsupervised religious meetings among enslaved persons. Charles Colcock Jones (1804-1863), Georgia Presbyterian minister and slaveholder, produced the most systematic antebellum plantation missionary work. His 1842 [Jones 1842][book_jones_1842] The Religious Instruction of the Negroes in the United States and decades of missionary work provided the model. The plantation missionary work combined the Christian message with proslavery framing that attempted to reconcile the Christian gospel with the institution. The [Erskine 1996][book_erskine_1996] Black People and the Reformed Church in America treats the Reformed missionary work, the [Cornelius 1999][book_cornelius_1999] Slave Missions and the Black Church treats the broader plantation missionary infrastructure.

Enslaved religious life resisted the slaveholder-authorized framing through alternative theological interpretations. The enslaved Christian theological framework emphasized themes including Exodus narrative (with slaveholder as Pharaoh and eventual divine deliverance), Sermon on the Mount ethics prioritizing the poor and oppressed, Book of Revelation apocalyptic imagery, and New Testament universal-human-dignity teaching. The spirituals as documented in [Thurman 1975][book_thurman_1975] Deep River and Krehbiel and others provide the expressive form of the enslaved religious life. The [Cone 1972][book_cone_1972] The Spirituals and the Blues treats the theological content of the spirituals.

## Nat Turner, Denmark Vesey, and Religious Resistance

Two antebellum enslaved rebellions produced substantial religious dimensions and consequences for the broader African American religious formation. The [Aptheker 1943][book_aptheker_1943] American Negro Slave Revolts, [Egerton 1999][book_egerton_1999] He Shall Go Out Free, [Robertson 1999][book_robertson_1999] Denmark Vesey, [Foner 1971][book_foner_1971] Nat Turner, [Greenberg 2003][book_greenberg_2003] Nat Turner A Slave Rebellion in History and Memory, and [French 2004][book_french_2004] The Rebellious Slave provide the comprehensive treatments.

Denmark Vesey (approximately 1767-1822) planned the extensive 1822 Charleston insurrection that would have involved approximately six to nine thousand enslaved and free Black participants across substantial Low Country plantations. Vesey, formerly enslaved and free through the 1799 purchase of his own freedom with lottery winnings, developed the plot through the African Methodist Episcopal Church at Charleston (founded 1817 by Morris Brown), providing the institutional infrastructure for organization. The plot was betrayed before the 16 July 1822 planned uprising. Approximately

$$N_{\text{Vesey plot executed}} = 35, \quad N_{\text{Vesey plot deported}} \approx 30$$

with Vesey himself hanged on 2 July 1822. The Charleston AME Church was closed under city ordinance following the plot exposure, producing substantial African Methodist decline in the Deep South until the post-Civil War reorganization. The [Egerton 1999][book_egerton_1999] and [Robertson 1999][book_robertson_1999] treatments provide the comprehensive analyses.

Nat Turner (1800-1831), Virginia enslaved Baptist preacher and self-reported religious visionary, led the August 1831 Southampton County, Virginia rebellion that killed approximately

$$N_{\text{Nat Turner rebellion white dead}} \approx 60, \quad N_{\text{Turner rebellion enslaved dead in aftermath}} \approx 200$$

with the rebellion suppressed within duration

$$t_{\text{Turner rebellion active}} \approx 2 \text{ days}, \quad t_{\text{Turner capture to execution}} = 11 \text{ November} - 30 \text{ October} = 12 \text{ days}$$

and Turner captured on 30 October 1831 and executed on 11 November 1831. Turner's extended pre-execution [Confessions][ref_turner_confessions_1831] dictated to attorney Thomas Ruffin Gray provide the most substantial documentary record of the rebellion and the religious framework that motivated it. David Walker's 1829 [Appeal in Four Articles to the Coloured Citizens of the World][ref_walker_appeal_1829], published at Boston and circulated clandestinely in the South, and Henry Highland Garnet's 1843 [Address to the Slaves of the United States][ref_garnet_address_to_the_slaves_1843] delivered at the National Negro Convention at Buffalo, provided the parallel Black abolitionist textual tradition invoking religious sanction for slave resistance. Turner's 28 February 1831 solar eclipse interpretation and August 1831 atmospheric phenomenon interpretation provided the religious signals from which he drew the timing of the rebellion. The [Greenberg 2003][book_greenberg_2003] and [Turner 1831][book_turner_confessions_1831] Confessions treatments provide the comprehensive treatments.

The 1831 aftermath of Nat Turner's rebellion produced substantial legal and religious consequences across the Southern states. Southern state legislatures enacted restrictions on the religious education of enslaved persons, prohibitions on Black religious meetings without white supervision, and restrictions on literacy instruction for enslaved persons. The 1832 Virginia General Assembly debate on the gradual abolition of slavery, provoked by the rebellion, marked the last serious Southern legislative consideration of emancipation and its defeat by a narrow margin established the Southern commitment to slavery expansion that would produce the sectional conflict of the subsequent three decades.

Additional smaller-scale enslaved resistance including individual escape, work slowdown, tool sabotage, poisoning, and arson continued throughout the antebellum period without producing large-scale rebellions comparable to Vesey and Turner. The pattern of enslaved resistance is treated in [Aptheker 1943][book_aptheker_1943] American Negro Slave Revolts and [Kolchin 1993][book_kolchin_1993] American Slavery.

## Free Black Church Expansion in the North

Free African American religious institutional expansion in the Northern states accelerated through the antebellum period, producing the institutional foundation that would shape the postbellum African American religious landscape. The [Newman 2008][book_newman_2008] Freedom's Prophet (already cited), [Nash 1988][book_nash_1988] Forging Freedom, [Lincoln and Mamiya 1990][book_lincoln_mamiya_1990] The Black Church in the African American Experience (already cited), [Payne 1891][book_payne_1891] History of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, [Sernett 2007][book_sernett_2007] Bound for the Promised Land, [Winch 2002][book_winch_2002] A Gentleman of Color, and [Dunbar 2008][book_dunbar_2008] A Fragile Freedom provide the comprehensive treatments.

African Methodist Episcopal Church expansion under Richard Allen (1760-1831), Daniel Payne (1811-1893), and subsequent leadership provided the most substantial free Black denominational growth. The 1816 formal organization at Philadelphia (treated in [A268][related_post_a268_awakening_and_founding]) expanded through state conferences and missionary activity, reaching approximately

$$P_{\text{AME adherents}}(1860) \approx 2 \times 10^4, \quad P_{\text{AME adherents}}(1870) \approx 4 \times 10^5$$

with the substantial 1870 growth reflecting the dramatic expansion into the former Confederate states following emancipation. Daniel Payne's 1852 election as the fourth AME bishop and decades of educational and institutional leadership shaped the denominational formation. The [Newman 2008][book_newman_2008] and [Payne 1891][book_payne_1891] treatments provide the comprehensive coverage.

African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church expansion under James Varick and successor bishops provided the second major free Black denominational structure. The 1821 formal organization at New York expanded through missionary activity, reaching approximately

$$P_{\text{AME Zion adherents}}(1860) \approx 5 \times 10^4$$

with substantial Underground Railroad and abolitionist activity. The AME Zion designation as "the Freedom Church" reflected the denominational commitment to abolitionist activity, with bishops including Christopher Rush and leaders including Harriet Tubman, Sojourner Truth, and Frederick Douglass involved with the denomination. The [Walls 1974][book_walls_1974] The African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church treats the history.

Free Black Baptist congregations grew through the period, with the 1840 formation of the American Baptist Missionary Convention providing the first national free Black Baptist organization. The 1866 formation of the Consolidated American Baptist Missionary Convention would provide the postbellum unified organization, and the 1895 formation of the National Baptist Convention would provide the largest African American denomination through the twentieth century. The [Washington 1985][book_washington_1985] Frustrated Fellowship treats the antebellum free Black Baptist history.

Sojourner Truth (approximately 1797-1883), formerly enslaved in New York (freed under New York gradual emancipation in 1827), provided the most prominent nineteenth-century Black woman religious leader alongside abolitionist and women's rights activity. Her 1851 Akron, Ohio Women's Rights Convention speech, popularly known as [Ain't I a Woman][ref_truth_aint_i_a_woman_1851] and subsequently reconstructed in the popular version through Frances Dana Barker Gage's 1863 rendering (with the historical accuracy of that reconstruction contested), provided the canonical statement combining Black woman's religious authority with abolitionist and women's rights advocacy. The [Painter 1996][book_painter_1996] Sojourner Truth provides the biography.

## Mexican Catholic Incorporation via the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo

The 1846-1848 Mexican-American War and the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo produced the American incorporation of substantial territories that had been Mexican Catholic populations, reshaping the American ethnoreligious landscape. The [Griswold del Castillo 1990][book_griswold_del_castillo_1990] The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, [Weber 1973][book_weber_1973] Foreigners in Their Native Land, [Weber 1982][book_weber_1982] The Mexican Frontier, [Deverell 2004][book_deverell_2004] Whitewashed Adobe, [Menchaca 2001][book_menchaca_2001] Recovering History, [Sánchez 1993][book_sanchez_1993] Becoming Mexican American, [Gutiérrez 1995][book_gutierrez_1995] Walls and Mirrors, and [Matovina 2011][book_matovina_2011] Latino Catholicism provide the comprehensive treatments.

The 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, ratified 30 May 1848, ceded to the United States approximately

$$A_{\text{Mexican Cession territory}} \approx 1.36 \times 10^6 \text{ km}^2$$

comprising approximately fifty-five percent of Mexico's pre-war territory, including present-day California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and portions of Colorado and Wyoming. The Mexican population within the ceded territory numbered approximately

$$P_{\text{Mexican population, Cession territory}}(1848) \approx 8 \times 10^4$$

with approximately eighty percent concentrated in the New Mexico Territory (approximately sixty thousand) and substantial California (approximately fifteen thousand) and Texas (already annexed 1845, approximately five thousand) populations.

Article IX of the Treaty guaranteed that Mexican inhabitants would be "admitted at the proper time (to be judged of by the Congress of the United States) to the enjoyment of all the rights of citizens of the United States according to the principles of the Constitution" and would in the meantime be "maintained and protected in the free enjoyment of their liberty and property, and secured in the free exercise of their religion without restriction." The religion protection reflected the Catholic identity of the Mexican population and the concern that Anglo-Protestant American governance would threaten Catholic practice. The subsequent American implementation failed to honor the property protections while broadly maintaining the religious accommodation.

Californio population (Spanish-descent and Mexican inhabitants of California), numbering approximately fifteen thousand in 1848, experienced rapid marginalization following the 1849 Gold Rush that brought approximately three hundred thousand American and international arrivals within two years. The 1851 California Land Act required Mexican-era landholders to prove their titles before federal commissions, producing substantial loss of Californio landholdings through legal costs, fraud, and ultimate confirmation-then-taxation dispossession. Approximately

$$f_{\text{Californio landholdings lost by 1870}} \approx 0.75$$

per reconstruction in [Pitt 1966][book_pitt_1966] The Decline of the Californios and [Camarillo 1979][book_camarillo_1979] Chicanos in a Changing Society.

Nuevomexicano population (Spanish-descent and Mexican inhabitants of New Mexico), numbering approximately sixty thousand in 1848, experienced slower marginalization due to the longer American statehood delay (New Mexico did not become a state until 1912). The Hispano community in northern New Mexico retained land tenure, Catholic religious practice, Spanish language, and cultural identity through the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The Penitentes (Los Hermanos Penitentes de Nuestro Padre Jesús Nazareno), the Catholic lay confraternity of northern New Mexico with Holy Week processional and mutual-aid functions, provided the religious infrastructure that maintained New Mexican Catholic identity through the American period without formal clerical oversight. The [Weigle 1976][book_weigle_1976] Brothers of Light Brothers of Blood treats the Penitente formation.

Tejano population in Texas (Spanish-descent and Mexican inhabitants of Texas), numbering approximately five thousand in 1848 after the earlier 1836 Texas Revolution had produced Tejano displacement, experienced substantial marginalization through the antebellum decades. The 1855 Cart War in south Texas, in which Anglo Texans attacked Tejano teamsters transporting goods between San Antonio and Gulf ports, marked the extension of anti-Mexican violence into the American statehood period. The [De León 1983][book_de_leon_1983] They Called Them Greasers treats the pattern.

American Catholic ecclesiastical incorporation of the Mexican Cession Catholic population proceeded through new diocese creation. The 1850 creation of the Vicariate Apostolic of New Mexico (subsequently Archdiocese of Santa Fe in 1875) under Jean-Baptiste Lamy (1814-1888) provided the American Catholic institutional accommodation. Lamy's French-descent and Roman-oriented approach to New Mexican Catholic reform conflicted with the Hispanic clergy including Antonio José Martínez of Taos, producing ecclesiastical tensions that Willa Cather's 1927 [Cather 1927][book_cather_1927] Death Comes for the Archbishop treats as the fictional narrative. The [Cassidy 2011][book_cassidy_2011] Mexican American Religions treats the religious formation.

The 1854 Gadsden Purchase, adding approximately seventy-seven thousand square kilometers of present-day southern Arizona and southern New Mexico to the United States, incorporated additional Mexican Catholic populations. The total American incorporation from the 1836 Texas independence through the 1854 Gadsden Purchase produced the foundation of the Mexican American population that would expand through subsequent decades and shape the contemporary American religious landscape through the continued Mexican and broader Latino migration treated in later articles.

## Transcendentalism and American Religious Liberalism

Transcendentalism, the antebellum New England literary-philosophical-religious movement, produced the most distinctive American religious liberalism of the period and provided the bridge from Unitarian religious rationalism to broader modern religious liberalism. The [Buell 2003][book_buell_2003] Emerson, [Packer 2007][book_packer_2007] The Transcendentalists, [Gura 2007][book_gura_2007] American Transcendentalism, [Robinson 2004][book_robinson_2004] The Unitarians and the Universalists (already cited), [Rose 1981][book_rose_1981] Transcendentalism as a Social Movement, [Miller 1950][book_miller_1950] The Transcendentalists An Anthology, and [Packer 1982][book_packer_1982] Emerson's Fall provide the comprehensive treatments.

Ralph Waldo Emerson (1803-1882), formerly Unitarian minister at the Second Church of Boston, provided the most influential Transcendentalist voice. His 1836 [Emerson 1836][book_emerson_1836] Nature manifesto, 1837 American Scholar address at Harvard, 1838 Divinity School Address at Harvard Divinity School (which scandalized Unitarian orthodoxy and produced Emerson's decades-long exclusion from Harvard), and extensive essays and lectures across the 1840s and 1850s reshaped American religious and philosophical thought. The [Richardson 1995][book_richardson_1995] Emerson The Mind on Fire provides the biography.

Henry David Thoreau (1817-1862) extended the Emersonian religious-philosophical program through the empirical natural-history direction. His 1854 [Thoreau 1854][book_thoreau_1854] Walden and 1849 [Thoreau 1849][book_thoreau_1849] Resistance to Civil Government (Civil Disobedience) provided the most influential Transcendentalist texts. The Walden experiment (July 1845 through September 1847) provided the practical exemplification of the Transcendentalist commitment to individual religious-philosophical self-reliance. The [Richardson 1986][book_richardson_1986] Henry Thoreau A Life of the Mind provides the biography.

Margaret Fuller (1810-1850), first female editor of the Transcendentalist journal The Dial (1840-1844) and first female foreign correspondent for the New-York Tribune, produced the most substantial Transcendentalist feminist contribution. Her 1845 [Fuller 1845][book_fuller_1845] Woman in the Nineteenth Century provided the canonical American feminist text and anticipated the 1848 Seneca Falls Convention treated below. The [Marshall 2013][book_marshall_2013] Margaret Fuller A New American Life provides the biography.

The Brook Farm community (1841-1847) under George Ripley (1802-1880) at West Roxbury, Massachusetts, provided the most substantial Transcendentalist communal experiment. The community combining manual labor, intellectual life, and religious-philosophical formation attracted participation from Nathaniel Hawthorne, Elizabeth Peabody, and other Transcendentalist-adjacent figures before its 1846 fire and 1847 dissolution. The [Delano 2004][book_delano_2004] Brook Farm provides the comprehensive treatment. The Fruitlands community (1843) under Bronson Alcott and Charles Lane provided the much smaller and shorter-lived complementary experiment.

Theodore Parker (1810-1860), Unitarian minister at the 28th Congregational Society of Boston, provided the most substantial Transcendentalist theological development combining higher-criticism Biblical scholarship with antislavery activism. His 1841 [Parker 1841][book_parker_1841] The Transient and Permanent in Christianity provided the systematic argument that traditional Christian doctrines were transient historical formulations of permanent religious truths. Parker's ministry attracted the broader Boston reformist community and advanced the Unitarian religious liberalization that would produce the late-nineteenth-century Unitarian formation treated in later articles. The [Grodzins 2002][book_grodzins_2002] American Heretic treats the Parker biography.

## Antebellum Social Reform Movements

The antebellum social reform movements reflected the religious energies of the Second Great Awakening applied to social problems. The [Walters 1978][book_walters_1978] American Reformers 1815-1860, [Hewitt 1984][book_hewitt_1984] Women's Activism and Social Change, [Ginzberg 1990][book_ginzberg_1990] Women and the Work of Benevolence, [Ryan 1981][book_ryan_1981] Cradle of the Middle Class, [Boylan 1988][book_boylan_1988] Sunday School, and [Cott 1977][book_cott_1977] The Bonds of Womanhood provide the comprehensive treatments.

The temperance movement, mobilizing against consumption of alcoholic beverages, emerged through the 1826 American Temperance Society preceded by Lyman Beecher's 1826 [Six Sermons on the Nature, Occasions, Signs, Evils, and Remedy of Intemperance][ref_beecher_six_sermons_on_intemperance_1826], expanded through the 1836 American Temperance Union, and produced substantial state prohibition legislation including the 1851 [Maine Law][ref_maine_law_1851] prohibiting the manufacture and sale of alcoholic beverages in Maine. Approximately

$$N_{\text{state prohibition laws by 1855}} = 13$$

Northern states had enacted prohibition or restriction laws following the Maine Law model, though most were subsequently repealed or amended. The temperance mobilization was religious in framing, drawing on the Second Great Awakening perfectionism and the voluntary-society infrastructure. The [Tyrrell 1979][book_tyrrell_1979] Sobering Up and [Blocker 1989][book_blocker_1989] American Temperance Movements treat the movement.

The women's rights movement mobilized in the 1840s combining abolitionist experience of women activists with broader concerns about women's legal, economic, and political rights. The 1848 Seneca Falls Convention under Elizabeth Cady Stanton (1815-1902) and Lucretia Mott (1793-1880) produced the [Declaration of Sentiments][ref_seneca_falls_declaration_1848] modeled on the Declaration of Independence with eighteen resolutions demanding women's rights, accompanied by Stanton's [Address on Woman's Rights][ref_stanton_address_womans_rights_1848]. Catharine Beecher's 1841 [Treatise on Domestic Economy][ref_beecher_treatise_on_domestic_economy_1841] provided the influential contemporary complementary framework centering female authority in the domestic sphere. The subsequent 1850 Worcester National Women's Rights Convention and decades of organizing established the antebellum women's rights movement. The [DuBois 1978][book_dubois_1978] Feminism and Suffrage and [Wellman 2004][book_wellman_2004] The Road to Seneca Falls provide the comprehensive treatments.

Prison reform under Dorothea Dix (1802-1887) produced substantial improvement in American treatment of specifically incarcerated and mentally ill persons. Dix's 1841-1843 investigation of Massachusetts jails and almshouses documented systematic mistreatment of mentally ill persons and produced her 1843 Memorial to the Massachusetts General Court that shaped subsequent state mental health institutional reform. Her extensive advocacy across twenty American states and European countries reshaped mental health treatment. The [Brown 1998][book_brown_1998] Dorothea Dix treats the biography.

Peace movement mobilization through the American Peace Society (founded 1828 by William Ladd) and subsequent organizations produced substantial antebellum peace advocacy. The movement divided during the Civil War over the question of whether violent action against slavery was justified, with substantial abolitionist peace advocates including Elihu Burritt supporting the Union cause despite pacifist commitments. The [Curti 1929][book_curti_1929] The American Peace Crusade treats the movement.

Education reform under Horace Mann (1796-1859) in Massachusetts produced the first American state education system through his 1837-1848 service as secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education. His 1848 [Twelfth Annual Report][ref_mann_twelfth_annual_report_1848] provided the most influential systematic articulation of the common school philosophy. The common school movement spread across Northern states, producing the antebellum foundation for the American public education system. The religious dimensions of the common school controversy, particularly the Catholic objection to Protestant Bible reading and Protestant hymn singing in public schools, would produce the Catholic parochial school system as the institutional alternative. The [Cremin 1980][book_cremin_1980] American Education The National Experience treats the developments.

## The American Colonization Society and Liberia

The American Colonization Society (ACS), founded 1816 at Washington by Robert Finley and supporters including Henry Clay, John Randolph, and Bushrod Washington, provided the alternative Protestant religious response to slavery that combined gradualist antislavery with colonization of free African Americans to Africa. The [Burin 2005][book_burin_2005] Slavery and the Peculiar Solution, [Tyler-McGraw 2007][book_tyler_mcgraw_2007] An African Republic, and [Everill 2013][book_everill_2013] Abolition and Empire in Sierra Leone and Liberia provide the comprehensive treatments.

The ACS's 1822 establishment of Cape Mesurado (subsequent Monrovia) provided the initial Liberian settlement. Approximately

$$N_{\text{African American emigrants to Liberia, 1822 to 1861}} \approx 1.3 \times 10^4$$

emigrated across the antebellum period, less than the ACS's ambitions but providing the institutional foundation for the 1847 Liberian independence and subsequent history. The ACS drew substantial support from Northern moderates seeking gradualist solution to slavery and from Southern slaveholders seeking removal of free Black population perceived as threat to slavery. The radical abolitionist tradition treated above rejected the ACS as complicit with slavery. Free Black communities divided over the ACS, with the 1817 Philadelphia meeting under James Forten rejecting colonization while other free Black leaders including Paul Cuffee (whose 1815 voyage to Sierra Leone predated the ACS) and later John Russwurm supporting emigration. The [Sinha 2016][book_sinha_2016] and [Nash 1988][book_nash_1988] (already cited) treatments cover the free Black colonization debate.

## Scandinavian Lutheran Migration

Scandinavian migration to the United States began in the 1840s and accelerated through the antebellum and post-Civil War decades, producing the Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish Lutheran presence in the American Midwest. The [Lovoll 1984][book_lovoll_1984] The Promise of America, [Blegen 1931][book_blegen_1931] Norwegian Migration to America, [Barton 1994][book_barton_1994] A Folk Divided, and [Ostergren 1988][book_ostergren_1988] A Community Transplanted provide the comprehensive treatments.

Norwegian migration began with the 1825 arrival of the Sloopers aboard the Restauration at New York, though sustained migration accelerated only from the 1836 forward. Total Norwegian arrivals through 1861 reached approximately

$$N_{\text{Norwegian arrivals, US, 1825 to 1861}} \approx 4 \times 10^4$$

concentrated in the Fox River Valley of Illinois, southern Wisconsin, and Minnesota settlements. The Norwegian Lutheran denominational formation produced multiple competing synods reflecting pietist and confessional tendencies. The 1853 formation of the Norwegian Synod under Herman Amberg Preus provided the most substantial initial Norwegian Lutheran denominational structure.

Swedish migration began in the 1840s under Erik Jansson's 1846 Bishop Hill communal settlement in Illinois (the Jansonist religious movement) and the broader Lutheran migration. Total Swedish arrivals through 1861 reached approximately

$$N_{\text{Swedish arrivals, US, 1840 to 1861}} \approx 2 \times 10^4$$

with substantial subsequent expansion post-1865 producing the Augustana Synod (formed 1860). The [Bishop Hill][book_bishop_hill_beijbom] Beijbom treatment covers the Jansonist settlement.

Danish migration to the United States produced smaller presence than Norwegian or Swedish through the antebellum period but substantial subsequent expansion post-1865. Danish Baptist migration from the 1850s under Peter Rasmussen and others produced the Danish Baptist General Conference (formed 1910) and the broader Danish American religious formation.

Scandinavian Lutheran institutional consolidation produced substantial denominational diversity. By 1900 approximately

$$N_{\text{Scandinavian Lutheran denominations, US}} \approx 15$$

separate denominational bodies existed, reflecting national origin, pietist versus confessional tendencies, and theological positions. Subsequent mergers through the twentieth century treated in later articles consolidated the Scandinavian Lutheran presence into the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America (1988).

## Applying the Six-Axis Framework to the Antebellum Substrates

The six-axis analytical framework introduced in [A266][related_post_a266_framing] and applied to colonial substrates in [A267][related_post_a267_colonial_folkways] and Revolutionary substrates in [A268][related_post_a268_awakening_and_founding] admits systematic application to the antebellum substrates treated in this article.

The signal axis characterizes the training information each substrate received. Second Great Awakening evangelical formation combined intensive camp-meeting emotional experience with sustained voluntary-society participation, producing the high-intensity, sustained pattern. Mormon formation combined authoritative revelatory signal through Joseph Smith's continuing revelations with sustained community discipline, producing the most distinctive antebellum religious signal pattern. Irish Catholic immigrant formation combined sacramental parish participation with substantial parochial education, producing the dense institutional signal. Enslaved African American religious formation operated under slaveholder-authorized signal with autonomous hush harbor supplementation, producing the dual-signal pattern combining accommodation and resistance. Native peoples' religious formation combined traditional practices with missionary Christian instruction under substantial coercive pressure, producing the asymmetric syncretic pattern.

The objective axis characterizes what each substrate sought to optimize. Second Great Awakening evangelicals optimized for the individual conversion combined with millennial expectation of Christ's imminent return and consequent reform mobilization. Mormons optimized for the building of Zion combined with gathering to the Kingdom of God. Catholic immigrants optimized for the parish community sustaining ethnic and religious identity under hostile Protestant majority. Southern proslavery clergy optimized for the defense of slaveholder society through the biblical hermeneutical framework. Abolitionists optimized for the immediate emancipation grounded in evangelical perfectionism.

The structure axis characterizes ecclesiastical form. Methodist and Baptist evangelical structures spread through circuit-rider and associational patterns permitting rapid expansion. Mormon structure combined priesthood hierarchy with communal settlement patterns. Catholic structure retained hierarchical episcopal governance under Roman jurisdiction. Denominational splits along North-South lines produced the institutional foreshadowing of political division. Native and African American formations retained substantial autonomous structural elements under substantial external constraint.

The model axis characterizes theological content. Second Great Awakening formation modified strict Calvinist predestinarianism through New Divinity, New Haven Theology, and Arminian evangelical variants toward human-agency emphasis. Mormonism developed distinctive theological framework treated above. Southern proslavery theology developed biblical hermeneutics defending slavery. Abolitionist theology developed higher-law framework grounded in perfectionist evangelical commitments. Transcendentalism developed naturalistic religious framework departing from traditional Christian formulations.

The interaction axis characterizes external relationships. Northern evangelical formations engaged substantial voluntary-society reform activity extending to political action. Southern evangelical formations aligned with slaveholder political interests through denominational splits treated above. Catholic populations operated outside mainstream Protestant civic engagement while maintaining parish-based social provision. Mormon populations experienced severe conflict producing the 1846-1847 Utah migration. Native populations experienced substantial displacement through removal treated above. Enslaved and free Black populations experienced increasing constraint through the earlier [Fugitive Slave Act of 1793][ref_fugitive_slave_act_1793], the [Missouri Compromise of 1820][ref_missouri_compromise_1820], the [Compromise of 1850][ref_compromise_of_1850] and its [Fugitive Slave Act][ref_fugitive_slave_act_1850], the [Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854][ref_kansas_nebraska_act_1854], the [1857 Dred Scott v. Sandford decision][ref_dred_scott_v_sandford_1857], and expanding Southern proslavery consensus.

The adaptation axis characterizes response to novel circumstances. The antebellum period saw substantial adaptation across all substrates through institutional innovation, denominational reorganization, and ideological development. The denominational splits of the 1837-1861 period marked the most substantial institutional adaptation as substantial denominations reorganized along North-South lines preceding the political split.

## Terminological Note

This article follows the terminological conventions established in [A266][related_post_a266_framing]. antebellum usage includes the following. Free states and slave states refer to the American political geography developed through the Missouri Compromise (1820), Compromise of 1850, and subsequent legislation. Free Black and free colored refer to the African-descent population not held in bondage in either the Northern or Southern states. Immigrant refers to voluntary migrants to the United States, while enslaved persons refers to African-descent persons held in bondage. Native, indigenous, and Indian are used approximately interchangeably following the practice of the primary scholarship cited, with tribal names where appropriate. Denominational names (Methodist, Baptist, Presbyterian, Catholic, and others) are used in their antebellum meanings.

## Load-Bearing Open Questions

- What is the correct empirical characterization of the Second Great Awakening as either a coherent multi-decade movement or as a series of regional and denominational revivals subsequently unified by historiographical construction?
- What is the correct assessment of the religious causation in the 1830s-1850s abolitionist mobilization relative to the economic, political, and broader ideological factors?
- What is the correct empirical characterization of the Trail of Tears mortality including attribution to removal conditions, pre-existing disease, and subsequent Indian Territory conditions?
- What is the correct assessment of the relative contribution of the 1830s Mormon persecutions to the 1846-1847 Utah migration and the subsequent Mormon distinctive religious formation?
- What is the correct empirical characterization of the religious composition of the antebellum immigrant streams given the documentary limitations of the pre-1850 immigration records?
- What is the correct assessment of the religious dimensions of the denominational splits of 1837, 1844, 1845, and 1861 as antecedents to the political split of 1861?
- What is the correct assessment of the extent and persistence of Mexican Catholic religious practice in the post-1848 Cession territories against the pressures of Anglo-Protestant migration and American Catholic institutional reform?
- What is the correct empirical characterization of the enslaved Christian religious life given the documentary limitations and the interpretive contest between the accommodationist and resistance readings?
