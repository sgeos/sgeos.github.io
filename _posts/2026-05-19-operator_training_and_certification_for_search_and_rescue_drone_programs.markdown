per year

The Tier 4 programme
spends

The Tier 1 and Tier 2 programmes

## Why Training Dominates the Cost

Three structural facts
make operator training
the dominant programme cost.

**Personnel cost scales with operator count.**
The Tier 3 programme
described in A146
supported
five to ten qualified operators
on multiple platforms.
The Tier 4 programme
supported
fifteen to forty qualified operators.
Each operator
carries
an initial training cost
and an annual recurrency cost.
The total training cost
across the operator pool
scales linearly
with the count.

**Currency requirements
accumulate.**
An operator
qualified on
one platform type
who must maintain currency
on two or three platform types
spends two or three times
the recurrency budget.
A programme operating both
fixed-wing and multicopter platforms
typically supports
operators qualified on both,
which doubles the recurrency cost
per operator.

**Turnover replaces operators.**
Operator turnover
in a public safety drone programme
runs
fifteen to twenty-five percent
per year
under typical conditions.
A ten-operator programme
loses
one to three operators per year
and must replace them
from
the beginning of the training stack.
The replacement cost
is comparable to
the initial training cost
of a new operator
and adds substantially to the annual budget.

These three facts
combine
to produce
an annual training cost
that equals or exceeds
the annual platform upkeep cost
in most programmes.
A146's TCO table
reflected
this structural cost
through
the cumulative operating cost
that approached
the acquisition cost
over five years.
A149's article
disaggregates
the training portion
of that cost
so that
the budgeted entity
can plan the human-capital investment
with the same precision
it plans the platform investment.

## The Five Layers of the Training Stack

The training a search and rescue
drone operator
must complete
spans five layers
that
each address
a different aspect
of the operator's job.

**Layer 1, the regulatory minimum.**
[The Federal Aviation Administration
Part 107 remote pilot certificate][ref_faa_part_107]
qualifies
the operator
for commercial small unmanned aircraft operations
under
the standard rules.
This is the legal minimum
for commercial flight,
including
public safety flight
under
the
[Part 107 public safety provisions][ref_faa_public_safety].

**Layer 2, manufacturer training.**
The platform vendor
operates
a training programme
that
qualifies the operator
on
the platform's
flight characteristics,
mission planning interface,
ground control station,
and payload integration.
[The DJI Enterprise Learning Center][ref_dji_academy],
[Skydio Academy][ref_skydio_academy],
[the Parrot training programme][ref_parrot_training],
[the AeroVironment training programmes][ref_aerovironment_training],
and equivalent vendor offerings
deliver
this layer.

**Layer 3, SAR operational training.**
The
[DRONERESPONDERS UNITE programme][ref_droneresponders_unite],
[the National Association for Search and Rescue][ref_nasar],
[the Texas A&M Engineering Extension Service UAS programmes][ref_teex_uas],
and similar SAR-focused training providers
qualify the operator
in
search pattern execution,
sensor operator skills,
crew coordination,
and the integration with
the broader incident response.

**Layer 4, NIMS and Incident Command System integration.**
The
[Federal Emergency Management Agency
National Incident Management System][ref_nims_framework]
training catalogue
includes
[IS-100][ref_is_100]
through
[IS-800][ref_is_800],
[ICS-300][ref_ics_300],
and
[ICS-400][ref_ics_400]
courses
that qualify
the operator
to integrate with
the broader incident response.
A UAS Group Supervisor
in the Incident Command System
needs
the intermediate and advanced courses
to lead
the UAS team
under
the Operations Section Chief.

**Layer 5, specialised operations training.**
Particular missions
require
particular training
beyond
the four general layers.
Night operations,
operations over people,
beyond visual line of sight operations,
wildfire-specific operations
under
[National Wildfire Coordinating Group][ref_nwcg]
qualifications,
and water rescue operations
each have
specialised training requirements
that
the programme adopts
based on
its operational profile.

The five layers
operate in sequence
through the operator's first year
and continue
in parallel
through
the operator's career
as recurrency
maintains qualification.

## Layer 1, the Regulatory Minimum

The
[Federal Aviation Administration
Part 107 remote pilot certificate][ref_faa_part_107]
is the legal qualification
for commercial operation
of small unmanned aircraft
weighing less than fifty-five pounds.
Public safety operators
holding
the certificate
operate under
the Part 107 rules
unless
the operation
qualifies for a
[Certificate of Waiver or Authorization][ref_faa_coa]
or a
[Section 44807 exemption][ref_section_44807]
that
admits operation
outside
the standard rules.

The initial certificate
requires
the operator
to pass
an aeronautical knowledge test
covering
airspace classification,
weather effects,
loading and performance,
operations,
regulations,
and emergency procedures.
The test
is administered
at
[FAA-approved knowledge test centres][ref_faa_knowledge_test_centers]
operated by
third-party providers
such as PSI Services.
The test fee
in 2026
is one hundred seventy-five US dollars.
Self-study materials
are free,
including
[the official Part 107 Study Guide][ref_faa_study_guide]
that
the FAA publishes
on its website.
Commercial prep courses
from
[Pilot Institute][ref_pilot_institute],
[King Schools][ref_king_schools],
and similar providers
cost
one hundred to five hundred dollars
and substantially improve
first-attempt pass rates.

The certificate
is valid for twenty-four months.
Recurrency
requires
the operator
to complete
the
[ALC-451 online recurrent course][ref_faa_recurrent]
through the
FAA Safety Team learning portal.
The recurrent course
is free
and takes
approximately two hours
to complete.
The completion
extends the certificate
for another
twenty-four months.

For operations outside
the standard Part 107 rules,
namely
operations over people in Categories 2 or 3,
night operations
without
the night operations endorsement,
beyond visual line of sight operations,
or operations of platforms
above fifty-five pounds,
the operator or the organisation
applies for
a specific
[waiver or exemption][ref_faa_waivers].
The waiver application process
is administrative
rather than training-based,
though
many waivers
require
operator demonstration
of the proposed operation's safety
through
a written concept of operations
that
the operator must develop
and that
the FAA reviews.

A Government agency
operating drones
for public safety missions
can alternatively operate under
[a Public Aircraft Operations
exemption][ref_public_aircraft]
that
admits
public agency flight
outside
the standard commercial rules.
The Public Aircraft Operations pathway
exists
for government operators
performing missions
in the public interest,
including
search and rescue.

**Layer 1 cost per operator.**
The Layer 1 cost
runs
approximately
one hundred seventy-five dollars
for the test
plus
zero to five hundred dollars
for prep materials
and study time,
totalling
approximately
two hundred to seven hundred dollars
per operator
for the initial certificate.
The recurrency cost
runs
the operator's time only,
plus
optional refresher materials,
totalling
approximately
fifty to two hundred dollars
per operator
every twenty-four months.

**Layer 1 timeline.**
A new operator
typically completes
Layer 1
in
two to six weeks
from
the decision to pursue the certificate
through
the test passage.

## Layer 2, Manufacturer Training

The platform vendor's training programme
qualifies the operator
on
the platform.
This layer
is administratively independent of
Layer 1
but
operationally essential.
A Part-107-certified operator
without manufacturer training
on
a platform
will
struggle with
the platform's
mission planning interface,
the ground control station,
the payload integration,
and the platform-specific
emergency procedures.

**DJI Enterprise Learning Center.**
[The DJI Enterprise Learning Center][ref_dji_academy]
offers
self-paced online modules
free of charge
covering
the major DJI Enterprise platforms
including
the Matrice 30 and 350 series,
the Matrice 4 series,
the Mavic 3 Enterprise series,
and the legacy Phantom 4 Enterprise.
Instructor-led courses
delivered in person
at DJI Enterprise training facilities
or at customer sites
cost
two thousand to five thousand dollars
per student
for a five-day course
that
includes
hands-on flight time
and the platform-specific
qualification.
A budgeted entity
operating
the DJI line
should
plan
the instructor-led course
for
the first operators
and the self-paced courses
for
subsequent operators
who follow
the experienced operators
through
mentored flight.

**Skydio Academy.**
[Skydio Academy][ref_skydio_academy]
operates
under
the
[Skydio public safety programme][ref_skydio_public_safety]
and
typically includes
training for
the first operators
in the platform price.
Additional operators
or specialised courses
cost
one thousand to three thousand dollars
per student.
The Skydio courses
emphasise
the autonomous flight features
that distinguish
the Skydio platforms
from
the manually-piloted DJI line.

**Parrot training.**
[Parrot's training resources][ref_parrot_training]
for the ANAFI USA, ANAFI USA GOV,
and ANAFI Ai platforms
include
free online documentation,
self-paced modules,
and instructor-led courses
delivered through
authorised Parrot training partners.
The instructor-led course
costs
typically run
two thousand to four thousand dollars per student.

**AeroVironment training.**
[AeroVironment's training programmes][ref_aerovironment_training]
for the Puma 3 AE
and similar
small fixed-wing platforms
cost
five thousand to fifteen thousand dollars
per student
and require
one to two weeks
of in-person attendance
at the AeroVironment training facility
in California
or at the customer's site
through an on-site contract.
The cost difference
relative to the multicopter training
reflects
the additional complexity
of fixed-wing operation,
the launch and recovery training,
and the platform-specific certification
that
the Puma's federal customers
require.

**Quantum Systems training.**
[Quantum Systems training][ref_quantum_training]
for the Trinity F90+
and Vector platforms
costs
three thousand to ten thousand dollars
per student
and follows
a similar in-person model
to AeroVironment training.

**Wingtra training.**
[Wingtra training][ref_wingtra_training]
for the WingtraOne Gen II
includes
online resources
and an optional in-person course
costing
one thousand five hundred to four thousand dollars per student.

**Layer 2 cost per operator.**
The Layer 2 cost
varies substantially
by platform type.
A multicopter operator
on a DJI or Skydio platform
spends
zero to five thousand dollars
on Layer 2.
A fixed-wing operator
on an AeroVironment Puma
or
Edge Autonomy Penguin C
spends
five thousand to fifteen thousand dollars
on Layer 2.
A hybrid VTOL operator
on a Quantum Systems Trinity F90+
spends
three thousand to ten thousand dollars.

**Layer 2 timeline.**
Multicopter Layer 2
typically completes
in
one to two weeks
including
the self-paced and instructor-led courses.
Fixed-wing Layer 2
typically completes
in
two to four weeks.

**Layer 2 recurrency.**
The manufacturer training
does not have
a formal recurrency requirement
in most cases,
but
the platform vendor
publishes
firmware updates and procedural updates
that
the operator
must
absorb
through
the vendor's update channels.
A programme typically budgets
five hundred to two thousand dollars
per operator per year
for refresher attendance
at vendor user conferences,
update courses,
and platform-specific
recurrency.

## Layer 3, Search and Rescue Operational Training

The third training layer
qualifies the operator
in
the operational procedures
that
search and rescue missions require,
namely
search pattern execution,
sensor operator skills,
crew coordination,
incident integration,
and the platform-agnostic
operational competence
that
distinguishes
a SAR drone operator
from
a generic Part 107 holder.

**DRONERESPONDERS UNITE programme.**
[DRONERESPONDERS][ref_droneresponders]
is the national non-profit
that
serves
the public safety unmanned aircraft community.
[The UNITE programme][ref_droneresponders_unite]
delivers
a standardised
training pathway
for public safety drone operators
at four to five
proficiency levels.
The UNITE programme
incorporates
the NIST Standard Test Methods
as a proficiency benchmark
and
provides
a recognised certification
that
agencies use
for operator qualification.
The cost
runs
five hundred to two thousand dollars
per operator
for the initial certification
and
two hundred to five hundred dollars
per operator
for recurrency.

**Texas A&M Engineering Extension Service.**
[TEEX UAS Programs][ref_teex_uas]
deliver
public safety drone training
through
courses ranging from
a few days
to multi-week academies.
TEEX's UASO (UAS Operator),
UASR (UAS Responder),
and related curricula
provide
a recognised pathway
that
many state and local agencies adopt.
TEEX is also
a National Institute of Standards and Technology-aligned evaluator
for
[the Standard Test Methods for Small Unmanned Aircraft Systems][ref_nist_tests].
NIST does not license evaluators directly,
but
the standards-development organisations
including the
[American Society for Testing and Materials Committee E54.09][ref_astm_e54_09]
and
the Airborne Public Safety Association
adopt and credential proctors
who administer the tests.
TEEX-administered tests
carry
the federal recognition
that the NIST programme provides
through the adopting organisations.

**The National Association for Search and Rescue.**
[NASAR][ref_nasar]
provides
SAR-specific training curricula
that
the drone operator
augments with
the platform-specific training.
NASAR's
[SARTech][ref_nasar_sartech]
certification series
qualifies
the operator
in
search theory,
crew coordination,
and the incident response framework
that
SAR missions follow.
A drone operator
who holds
SARTech II or higher
inherits
the ground SAR competence
that
the drone team
must coordinate with
during the mission.

**Other public safety training providers.**
[North Dakota State University's
Northern Plains UAS Test Site
operator training][ref_ndsu_uas],
[Sinclair Community College's UAS Training Center][ref_sinclair_uas],
and
[Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University's
UAS training programmes][ref_erau_uas]
each
provide
SAR-relevant operational training
at various price points
and delivery formats.
A programme manager
selecting
a Layer 3 provider
should
align the provider's curriculum
with
the programme's mission profile
and geographic setting.

**Layer 3 cost per operator.**
The Layer 3 cost
runs
five hundred to three thousand dollars
per operator
for the initial certification
and
two hundred to one thousand dollars
per operator per year
for recurrency.

**Layer 3 timeline.**
Layer 3
typically completes
in
two to eight weeks
spread across
the operator's first six months,
because
the operator
needs
flight hours
on
the actual platforms
to integrate
the operational training
with the platform-specific skills.

## Layer 4, NIMS and Incident Command System Integration

The fourth training layer
qualifies the operator
to integrate
with
the broader incident response
under
[the National Incident Management System][ref_nims_framework]
and
the
[Incident Command System][ref_ics_framework].
A drone operator
who arrives at an incident
without
the NIMS and ICS training
will fail to integrate
with the incident command structure,
which will exclude
the drone capability
from
the operational decision-making
where it would provide
the greatest value.

**FEMA Independent Study courses.**
[The Federal Emergency Management Agency
Independent Study programme][ref_fema_is]
delivers
free online courses
that
public safety responders
complete
to qualify for
incident response roles.
The relevant courses for
drone operators
are
[IS-100, Introduction to the Incident Command System][ref_is_100],
[IS-200, Basic ICS for Initial Response][ref_is_200],
[IS-700, National Incident Management System Introduction][ref_is_700],
and
[IS-800, National Response Framework][ref_is_800].
Each course
takes
two to four hours
to complete
and is free.
Operators
typically complete
all four courses
in
the first week of programme onboarding.

**Instructor-led ICS courses.**
The
[ICS-300, Intermediate ICS for Expanding Incidents][ref_ics_300]
and
[ICS-400, Advanced ICS for Command and General Staff][ref_ics_400]
courses
are instructor-led
and required
for operators
who serve in
intermediate or senior roles
within
the incident command structure.
A UAS Group Supervisor
in the Operations Section
typically holds
ICS-300 at minimum,
and a Group Supervisor
on
complex incidents
typically holds
ICS-400.
The courses
are delivered through
state and local emergency management training centres
at no cost
or
through commercial providers
at three hundred to one thousand dollars per student.

**NWCG-aligned ICS for wildfire-adjacent programmes.**
A programme
whose mission profile
includes
wildfire response
should
align its ICS training
with
[the National Wildfire Coordinating Group][ref_nwcg]
position qualifications.
The NWCG
maintains
the position qualification system
that
the wildfire response community
recognises
for the unique requirements of
wildland fire incidents.

**Layer 4 cost per operator.**
The Layer 4 cost
runs
zero to two thousand dollars
per operator
for the initial NIMS and ICS qualifications,
with
the bulk of the cost
borne in operator time
rather than in training fees.
The recurrency
typically runs
zero to two hundred dollars
per operator
per year.

**Layer 4 timeline.**
Layer 4
typically completes
in
one to four weeks
of effort
spread across
the operator's first month,
because
the IS-series courses
are short enough
to fit
in evening or weekend study.
The instructor-led ICS-300 and ICS-400 courses
require
several days
of in-person attendance.

## Layer 5, Specialised Operations Training

The fifth training layer
addresses
the operations
that
particular missions require
beyond
the four general layers.

**Night operations.**
The
[FAA Part 107 night operations rule][ref_part_107_night]
admits
night flight
under Part 107
after
the operator completes
[the night operations training][ref_faa_night_training]
that
is incorporated
into the initial knowledge test
and the recurrent training
after the 2021 rule change.
A programme
that operates
at night
should plan
operator-specific night-flight training
on
the actual platforms
because
the platform-specific
night-flight handling
differs substantially
from day-flight handling.
Night flight training
costs
five hundred to two thousand dollars
per operator
for initial qualification
and
typically does not require
formal recurrency
beyond
the standard Part 107 recurrency.

**Operations over people.**
The
[Operations Over People rule][ref_oop_final_rule]
admits
operations over people
under
the Category 2, Category 3, or Category 4 frameworks.
Categories 2 and 3
require
[the ASTM F3322 parachute standard][ref_astm_f3322].
A programme operating
at A148's Level 1 or Level 2
typically operates
under Category 2 or Category 3.
The operator
must be trained on
the parachute system's
pre-flight check,
the deployment procedure,
and the post-flight inspection.
The training
typically costs
five hundred to two thousand dollars per operator
for the initial qualification
and
two hundred to five hundred dollars per operator per year
for recurrency.

**Beyond visual line of sight operations.**
A programme operating
beyond visual line of sight
under
[a Section 44807 exemption][ref_section_44807],
the
[Centres of Excellence pathway][ref_faa_coe_uas],
or eventually
[the Part 108 final rule][ref_part_108]
must train operators
in
the BVLOS procedures
that the authorisation requires.
The training
typically costs
two thousand to ten thousand dollars per operator
for initial qualification
and
five hundred to two thousand dollars per operator per year
for recurrency.

**Wildfire-specific operations under NWCG qualifications.**
A programme
whose mission profile
includes
wildfire response
qualifies operators
through
[NWCG Next Generation Position Task Books][ref_nwcg_taskbooks]
for the
[Unmanned Aircraft Systems Pilot (UASP)][ref_nwcg_uasp],
[Unmanned Aircraft Systems Manager (UASM)][ref_nwcg_uasm],
and
[Unmanned Aircraft Systems Module Leader (UASL)][ref_nwcg_uasl]
positions.
The taskbook qualification
requires
demonstrated performance
on actual wildfire incidents
in addition to
classroom training,
which makes
the qualification timeline
multi-season
rather than
short-term.

**Other specialised operations.**
Water rescue operations,
alpine operations,
confined-space operations,
and
multi-aircraft swarm operations
each have
specialised training requirements
that
a programme
adopts
based on
its operational profile.

**Layer 5 cost per operator.**
The Layer 5 cost
varies substantially
by mission profile.
A programme
operating
only standard Part 107 daylight operations
in uncontrolled airspace
spends
zero on Layer 5.
A programme
operating
across all four specialised categories
spends
five thousand to fifteen thousand dollars
per operator
across all specialisations.

**Layer 5 timeline.**
Layer 5
typically completes
in parallel with
Layer 3 and Layer 4
across
the operator's first year
and continues
through
the operator's career
as new specialisations
become operationally relevant.

## Recurrency and Currency Maintenance

The training stack
is not complete
once the operator
finishes the initial training.
The operator
must
maintain currency
on
each layer's qualifications
through
the recurrent training cycle
and
through
flight-hour requirements
that
the programme establishes.

**Regulatory recurrency.**
Part 107
requires
the operator
to complete
the
[ALC-451 online recurrent course][ref_faa_recurrent]
every twenty-four months.
The course is free
and takes approximately two hours.

**Manufacturer recurrency.**
The platform vendor
publishes
firmware updates and procedural updates
that
the operator must absorb
through
the vendor's update channels.
A programme typically budgets
two to five days per year
per operator
for update courses,
vendor user conferences,
and platform-specific
refresher training.

**Operational recurrency.**
The
DRONERESPONDERS UNITE programme,
TEEX programmes,
and similar Layer 3 providers
publish
recurrency requirements
that
maintain
the operator's certification.
A programme typically budgets
one to three days per year
per operator
for operational refresher training.

**NIMS and ICS recurrency.**
The FEMA Independent Study courses
do not have
a formal recurrency requirement,
but
the operator's skills
decay over time
and
a programme that
does not refresh
the ICS training
will find
its operators
ill-prepared
for major incidents.
A programme typically budgets
one day per year
per operator
for ICS refresher.

**Flight hour requirements.**
A programme typically requires
each operator
to log
twenty to sixty flight hours per year
on
each platform type
to maintain
currency.
The flight hours
combine
training flights,
demonstration flights,
and actual mission flights.
A programme operating
multiple platform types
must support
the flight hour requirement
across
all platforms,
which
multiplies
the training-flight budget.

The cumulative annual recurrency cost
across all five layers
runs
approximately
fifteen hundred to four thousand dollars
per operator
for the multicopter-only programme
and
three thousand to seven thousand dollars
per operator
for the multi-platform programme.

## Crew Roles and Training Pathways

A search and rescue
drone mission
runs
with a crew
of three to five people
in roles
that
each
have
training requirements.

**Remote Pilot in Command.**
The
[Remote Pilot in Command][ref_part_107_rpic]
is
the Part-107-certified operator
who manipulates the controls
of the unmanned aircraft.
The RPIC
holds
all five layers
of the training stack
for the platform being operated.
The RPIC
typically takes
six to twelve months
to qualify
from
a standing start.

**Visual Observer.**
The
[Visual Observer][ref_visual_observer]
maintains
visual line of sight
on the aircraft
and
serves
as the RPIC's
external situational awareness.
The Visual Observer
does not require
a Part 107 certificate,
but
the programme
typically requires
the Visual Observer
to complete
Layer 4 NIMS and ICS training
and
Layer 3 operational training
without
the platform-specific Layer 2
that
the RPIC carries.
The Visual Observer
typically qualifies
in
two to four months
from
a standing start.

**Sensor Operator.**
The Sensor Operator
runs
the thermal,
electro-optical,
or other payload
during the mission.
The Sensor Operator
typically holds
the Visual Observer qualifications
plus
sensor-specific training
on
the payload models
the programme operates.
The Sensor Operator
typically qualifies
in
three to six months.

**Search Team Coordinator.**
The Search Team Coordinator
manages
the search pattern execution
and coordinates
with
ground teams
and
other aircraft.
The Coordinator
typically holds
Layer 3 and Layer 4 training
at a higher level
than the RPIC,
including
ICS-300
for the role.
The Coordinator
typically qualifies
in
nine to twelve months.

**UAS Team Leader.**
The
UAS Team Leader
serves
as the operations leader
for the UAS team
during
major incidents.
The role corresponds approximately
to the
NWCG
[Unmanned Aircraft Systems Manager (UASM)][ref_nwcg_uasm]
position
in wildfire response
and to
the
[Remote Pilot in Command][ref_part_107_rpic]
position
in the
[Federal Emergency Management Agency
sUAS Team typing under NIMS-509][ref_fema_rtlt_suas],
though the
NIMS-509 catalogue
does not include
a dedicated
UAS Group Supervisor designator.
The Team Leader
holds
ICS-300 and ICS-400
plus
all five training layers
on at least one platform type,
plus
the Layer 3 senior-level certifications
that
the SAR providers offer.
The Team Leader
typically qualifies
in
eighteen to thirty-six months.

A programme building
the crew complement
should plan
the training pathway
for each role
so that
new operators
enter
the Visual Observer or Sensor Operator role
while
they complete
the RPIC training,
which spreads
the training cost
and the operator availability
across multiple roles
rather than
concentrating it
in the RPIC role
where
all five layers
are required.

| Role | Initial training time | Initial training cost | Layers required |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visual Observer | 2 to 4 months | $1,000 to $3,000 | 3, 4 (no 1, no 2 platform-specific) |
| Sensor Operator | 3 to 6 months | $2,000 to $5,000 | 3, 4 plus sensor-specific |
| Remote Pilot in Command | 6 to 12 months | $5,000 to $20,000 | 1, 2, 3, 4 |
| Search Team Coordinator | 9 to 12 months | $7,000 to $20,000 | 1, 2, 3, 4 plus ICS-300 |
| UAS Team Leader | 18 to 36 months | $15,000 to $40,000 | All 5 plus senior 3, ICS-400 |

The role-based training cost
combines
to produce
the operator pool cost
that
the programme bears.

## Building the Operator Pool

A search and rescue
drone programme
maintains
an operator pool
sized
to support
the operational tempo
under
weather, illness,
and turnover resilience.
A146 noted
the typical operator population
of three to five operators
per platform type.
The composition
depends on
the programme size
and
the mission profile.

**Selection criteria.**
A programme selecting
operators
from
within an existing public safety agency
typically looks for
candidates with
prior flight experience,
strong spatial awareness,
the ability to work
under time pressure,
and willingness to commit
to the multi-year training pathway.
Public safety agencies
often draw candidates from
the aviation-experienced subset
of the agency,
including
former military pilots,
private pilots,
and recreational drone enthusiasts
who have transitioned
to professional certification.

**Volunteer versus paid operators.**
Volunteer programmes,
including
many search and rescue teams,
state aviation auxiliary services,
and county-level
emergency response volunteer organisations,
operate with
unpaid
operators
who absorb the training cost
through
employer support,
self-funding,
or
state-level scholarship programmes.
Paid programmes
within
government public safety agencies
absorb the training cost
through
the agency's training budget.
A hybrid programme
mixes
paid full-time operators
with
volunteer surge capacity
that
deploys
during major incidents.

**Retention strategies.**
Operator turnover
in
public safety drone programmes
runs
fifteen to twenty-five percent
per year
under typical conditions
because
the operator population
includes
substantial volunteer participation,
because
the public safety agency
loses operators
to higher-paying roles
in adjacent agencies
or in commercial drone operation,
and because
the training pathway
qualifies operators
for commercial roles
that
pay better
than
the public safety position.
Retention strategies
include
competitive compensation,
clear career advancement paths
within the drone programme,
recognition
for senior roles
such as UAS Group Supervisor,
and
participation in
research and development work
that
extends
the operator's professional development.

**Multi-platform qualification.**
A programme operating
multiple platform types
must decide whether
each operator
qualifies on
one platform type only
or on
multiple platforms.
Single-platform qualification
reduces
the per-operator training cost
but
restricts
operational flexibility
because
operators
cannot substitute
across platforms
when staff or aircraft
are unavailable.
Multi-platform qualification
increases
the per-operator training cost
by approximately
fifty to one hundred percent
but
provides
the operational flexibility
that
larger programmes
require.
Most Tier 3 and Tier 4 programmes
qualify
the senior operators
on multiple platforms
while
the junior operators
qualify on
a single platform.

## Training Budget by Program Size

The training budget
varies substantially
with
the programme size
and tier
that A146 described.

**Tier 0, Evaluation and Proficiency.**
A programme
operating
the proof-of-concept tier
trains
one to two operators
on consumer-grade platforms.
The training budget
runs
five hundred to two thousand dollars
in the first year
across Layer 1
and limited Layer 3
elements.
Layer 2 manufacturer training
is typically self-paced and free
on consumer platforms.
Layer 4 NIMS training
is free
through FEMA.

**Tier 1, Volunteer.**
A volunteer programme
trains
one to three operators
on
prosumer multicopters.
The first-year training budget
runs
two thousand to seven thousand dollars
across the operator pool.
Annual recurrency
runs
one thousand to three thousand dollars per year.

**Tier 2, Small Professional.**
A small professional programme
trains
two to four operators
on
professional multicopters
plus
a Visual Observer pool
of three to six people.
The first-year training budget
runs
fifteen to thirty-five thousand dollars
across the operator pool.
Annual recurrency
runs
five to fifteen thousand dollars per year.

**Tier 3, Medium Professional.**
A medium professional programme
trains
five to ten operators
on
a multi-platform fleet
plus
a Visual Observer pool
of ten to twenty people.
The first-year training budget
runs
fifty to one hundred fifty thousand dollars
across the operator pool.
Annual recurrency
runs
fifteen to forty thousand dollars per year.

**Tier 4, Large Program or Federal Agency.**
A large programme
trains
fifteen to forty operators
on a substantial fleet
plus
a Visual Observer and Sensor Operator pool
of thirty to one hundred people.
The first-year training budget
runs
two hundred to seven hundred thousand dollars
across the operator pool.
Annual recurrency
runs
fifty to two hundred thousand dollars per year.

| Tier | Operators | First-year training | Annual recurrency |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 to 2 | $500 to $2,000 | $200 to $500 |
| 1 | 1 to 3 | $2,000 to $7,000 | $1,000 to $3,000 |
| 2 | 2 to 4 + observers | $15,000 to $35,000 | $5,000 to $15,000 |
| 3 | 5 to 10 + observers | $50,000 to $150,000 | $15,000 to $40,000 |
| 4 | 15 to 40 + observers | $200,000 to $700,000 | $50,000 to $200,000 |

The training budget
adds to
the platform acquisition cost
and the platform upkeep cost
that A146 described,
which together produce
the programme TCO.
A budgeted entity
planning the investment
should treat
the training budget
as
a separate
budget line
that
scales with
the operator pool
rather than with
the platform count.

## Out of Scope

This article
restricts itself
to the training stack
for a US-based
search and rescue
drone operator population
in 2026.
Four substantive topics
are deliberately deferred.

**Sensor and payload selection training.**
The selection and operational use
of
thermal, electro-optical, LiDAR,
multispectral,
and other payloads
warrants
its own treatment
in
a future article
in this series.
This article
covers
the operator's qualification
to fly the platforms
without
the depth on payload selection
that
a programme planning
the sensor investment
requires.

**Maintenance and lifecycle training.**
A maintenance technician
who manages
the platform fleet's
inspection, repair,
and lifecycle
holds
different qualifications
from
the operator
who flies it.
A future article
in this series
will address
the maintenance programme
and the associated training requirements.

**State-by-state variation.**
State and local
regulatory environments
overlay
the federal framework
in ways
that
this article cannot address
across all fifty states.
A programme manager
should consult
state-specific
training and certification requirements
that may
supplement
the federal stack.

**International training frameworks.**
The European Union Aviation Safety Agency
[Open, Specific, and Certified categories][ref_easa_uas]
have
their own training and certification requirements
distinct from
the FAA Part 107 stack.
A programme operating
outside the United States
should
consult
the jurisdiction-specific
requirements.

## Conclusion

A search and rescue
drone programme
in 2026
maintains
an operator pool
qualified through
a five-layer training stack
that
covers
the regulatory minimum,
the manufacturer training,
the SAR-specific operational training,
the National Incident Management System integration,
and
the specialised operations training
that
particular missions require.
The training cost
across the operator pool
equals or exceeds
the platform acquisition cost
over
the programme's first five years
and
dominates
the operating cost
through
the programme lifetime.

A budgeted entity
planning the investment
treats
the training budget
as
a separate budget line
that
scales with
the operator pool
rather than with
the platform count.
The first-year training budget
runs
from
a few thousand dollars
at the Tier 0 proof-of-concept tier
to
several hundred thousand dollars
at the Tier 4 federal-agency tier.
The annual recurrency
runs
ten to thirty percent
of the first-year cost
across all tiers
under
typical conditions.

The crew complement
varies
by role,
with
the Remote Pilot in Command,
the Visual Observer,
the Sensor Operator,
the Search Team Coordinator,
and
the UAS Group Supervisor
each
holding
a different subset
of the training stack.
A programme building
the crew complement
plans
the training pathway
across multiple roles
so that
new operators
enter
the Visual Observer or Sensor Operator role
while
they complete
the longer Remote Pilot in Command training,
which spreads
the cost
and
the operator availability
across multiple roles.

The articles
in this series
that follow A149
will address
sensor and payload selection,
maintenance and lifecycle management,
and
the data management
and chain of custody
that
the operating programme requires.
Together
the articles
provide
a working reference
for
a budgeted entity
planning
the multi-year
investment
in a SAR drone capability.
