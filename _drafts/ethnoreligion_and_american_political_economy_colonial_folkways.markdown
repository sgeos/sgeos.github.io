This second article of the series treats the encounter conditions of 1607 through 1725 during which the three pre-1607 ethnoreligious substrates identified in [A266][related_post_a266_framing] met and combined under the conditions of European colonial settlement, indigenous resistance and accommodation, and African forced migration. Coverage includes the four British folkways treated by Fischer (Puritan New England, Cavalier Virginia, Quaker Delaware Valley, and the Scots-Irish backcountry formation that would culminate in the eighteenth century), the Rhode Island Baptist and dissenter formation under Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson, the parallel European Continental streams (Dutch Reformed New Netherland, Swedish Lutheran New Sweden, Catholic Maryland, Sephardic Jewish communities at Newport and New Amsterdam and Charleston, Huguenot settlements, and German Pietist and Anabaptist migration), the Spanish colonial religious formation in the Southwest and Florida with its patterns of accommodation and revolt against the Pueblo and Muskogean peoples, the French colonial religious formation in Louisiana with its Catholic-Native and Catholic-African encounters, colonial higher education and print culture as ethnoreligious institutional infrastructure, the indigenous-European interactions and diffusion including the New England praying towns and King Philip's War, the Chesapeake Anglo-Powhatan conflict and agricultural exchange, Iroquois diplomatic sophistication and Quaker peace policy, the initial arrivals and religious formations among Africans forcibly transported to North America, and the systematic application of the six-axis analytical framework from [A266][related_post_a266_framing] to the colonial substrates. The article traces the causal patterns each stream established that later articles will trace through subsequent centuries.

## Contact and Colonial Formation 1607-1725

The period 1607 through 1725 defines the initial century of Anglo-American colonial formation in the territory that would become the United States. The starting date reflects the founding of Jamestown as the first permanent English settlement under the [Virginia Charter of 1606][ref_virginia_charter_1606]. The endpoint reflects the completion of the initial thirteen-colony territorial framework by approximately 1725 through the founding of Georgia in 1732 and the maturation of the earlier colonial institutions. The period frames the encounter conditions among the three ethnoreligious substrates that [A266][related_post_a266_framing] identified.

Colonial population and religious composition changed substantially across the period. The English colonial population followed the approximate trajectory

$$P_{\text{Anglo}}(1620) \approx 5 \times 10^3, \quad P_{\text{Anglo}}(1700) \approx 2.5 \times 10^5, \quad P_{\text{Anglo}}(1725) \approx 6 \times 10^5$$

with intermediate values roughly on the exponential trajectory

$$P_{\text{Anglo}}(t) = P_0 \, \exp(\lambda (t - t_0)), \quad \lambda \approx 0.031 \text{ per year}$$

where $\lambda$ combines approximately equal contributions from natural increase (very high fertility rates approaching Malthusian ceilings under abundant land conditions) and migration inflow. The proportion of that population held in bondage as enslaved Africans grew across the period from

$$f_{\text{enslaved}}(1620) \approx 0.00, \quad f_{\text{enslaved}}(1700) \approx 0.10, \quad f_{\text{enslaved}}(1725) \approx 0.15$$

The [Gemery 1980][research_gemery_1980] and [McCusker and Menard 1985][book_mccusker_menard_1985] quantitative reconstructions consolidate the demographic evidence. The [Bailyn 1986a][book_bailyn_1986a] The Peopling of British North America and [Bailyn 1986b][book_bailyn_1986b] Voyagers to the West provide the interpretive framework relating migration flows, colonial development, and Atlantic-world integration. The [Nash 2018][book_nash_2018] Red, White, and Black textbook synthesizes the tripartite demographic history from the colonial period forward. The [Greene 1988][book_greene_1988] Pursuits of Happiness argues for greater regional continuity across the colonial-Revolutionary transition than the Fischer framework proposes, providing the alternative comparative treatment.

The indigenous population of the same territory declined substantially across the same period through introduced epidemic disease, warfare, and displacement. The approximate demographic ratio inverted across the period,

$$\frac{P_{\text{Anglo}}}{P_{\text{indigenous, east of Mississippi}}}(1620) \approx 0.005, \quad \frac{P_{\text{Anglo}}}{P_{\text{indigenous, east of Mississippi}}}(1725) \approx 3$$

with the indigenous population east of the Mississippi declining from approximately one million in 1620 to approximately two hundred thousand by 1725, and the Anglo-American population growing from approximately five thousand to approximately six hundred thousand across the same interval. The [Thornton 1987][book_thornton_1987] and [Kelton 2015][book_kelton_2015] treatments document the demographic collapse patterns.

The African population transported directly to North America across the period totaled

$$N_{\text{African, direct arrivals}}(1607 \text{ to } 1725) \approx 5 \times 10^4 \text{ people}$$

with substantial subsequent growth of the resident African-descent colonial population through both continuing transatlantic imports and natural increase to reach

$$P_{\text{African, colonial}}(1725) \approx 1 \times 10^5$$

The North American pattern was distinctive because it produced natural increase in the enslaved population rather than the natural decrease that characterized Caribbean and Brazilian slave economies. The differential can be characterized approximately as

$$r_{\text{natural, NA}} > 0, \quad r_{\text{natural, Caribbean}} < 0$$

with the North American reproduction rate exceeding mortality by the eighteenth century, in contrast to the Caribbean and Brazilian pattern where enslaved deaths substantially exceeded births and continuing imports were required to maintain populations. The [Voyages Slave Trade Database 2019][ref_voyages_database] provides the quantitative record.

## The Four British Folkways

The [Fischer 1989][book_fischer_1989] Albion's Seed provided the influential thesis that four distinct English regional cultures produced four corresponding American colonial folkways whose institutional and cultural imprints remain identifiable in contemporary American regional variation. The four folkways and their approximate migration windows are

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Puritan (East Anglia to Massachusetts Bay)} &: [1629, 1640] \\
\text{Cavalier (Southern/Western England to Chesapeake)} &: [1642, 1675] \\
\text{Quaker (North Midlands and Wales to Delaware Valley)} &: [1675, 1725] \\
\text{Borderlands (Scots-Irish to backcountry)} &: [1717, 1775]
\end{aligned}$$

The four folkway thesis has been substantially contested since publication. The [Butler 1990][book_butler_1990] Awash in a Sea of Faith argued that the four-folkway framework substantially oversimplifies colonial religious diversity and undercounts the Continental European, African, and syncretic streams. The [Hodgson 1996][research_hodgson_1996] review argued that the regional cultural distinctions Fischer identifies were less sharp than the framework suggests. Recent quantitative work using demographic and genetic data by [Han et al 2017][research_han_et_al_2017] partially confirms the folkway distinctions while identifying substantial subsequent mixing. The present treatment adopts Fischer's four folkway framework as a first-order approximation while noting explicitly the substantial Continental European, African, and syncretic streams that the framework does not capture.

## Puritan New England and Congregationalist Governance

The Puritan migration to Massachusetts Bay under the [Winthrop 1630][book_winthrop_1630] leadership and the [Massachusetts Bay Charter of 1629][ref_massachusetts_bay_charter_1629] established the institutional formation that would substantially shape New England ethnoreligious development for the following two centuries. The [Winthrop 1630 A Model of Christian Charity Hanover text][ref_winthrop_model_christian_charity_hanover] provides an alternative primary transcription of the shipboard sermon that grounded the colonial covenantal vision. The [Winthrop 1630 Journal][book_winthrop_journal] provides the primary-source account of the colony's first years from its leader's perspective. The [Miller 1953][book_miller_1953] The New England Mind, [Morgan 1958][book_morgan_1958] The Puritan Dilemma, [Miller 1956][book_miller_1956] Errand into the Wilderness, [Bailyn 1955][book_bailyn_1955] The New England Merchants, [Foster 1971][book_foster_1971] Their Solitary Way, [Bushman 1967][book_bushman_1967] From Puritan to Yankee, [Middlekauff 1971][book_middlekauff_1971] The Mathers, [Hall 1989][book_hall_1989] Worlds of Wonder, Days of Judgment, [Innes 1995][book_innes_1995] Creating the Commonwealth, [Lockridge 1970][book_lockridge_1970] A New England Town, and [Breen 1970][book_breen_1970] The Character of the Good Ruler provide the classical and modern comprehensive treatments. The [Mather 1702][book_mather_1702] Magnalia Christi Americana provides the foundational primary Puritan self-historical treatment covering the colonial period through the turn of the eighteenth century.

Congregationalist ecclesiology, transferred from the English Puritan movement to the New England colonies, provided the institutional foundation for New England town governance. Each town constituted a covenanted community whose members entered into voluntary religious and civil association. The earlier [Mayflower Compact of 1620][ref_mayflower_compact_1620] provided the archetypal covenantal-civil form later extended across New England. The town meeting, at which male church members voted on local matters, provided the local democratic institution documented in [Zuckerman 1970][book_zuckerman_1970] Peaceable Kingdoms and [Anderson 1991][book_anderson_1991] New England's Generation. The [Cambridge Platform 1648][book_cambridge_platform_1648] formalized the Congregationalist ecclesiology across the Massachusetts churches and provided the institutional model for subsequent Connecticut and Plymouth arrangements. John Cotton's earlier defense of the New England ecclesiology in the [Cotton Way of the Churches of Christ in New England 1645][ref_cotton_way_congregational_1648] provided the systematic ministerial exposition that fed the Cambridge Platform. The [Fundamental Orders of Connecticut 1639][ref_fundamental_orders_connecticut_1639] provided the parallel civil-covenantal framework for the Connecticut settlements. The [Massachusetts Body of Liberties 1641][ref_massachusetts_body_of_liberties_1641] provided the earliest colonial codification of rights and legal procedure, combining Reformed political theory with English common-law inheritance. Following the 1684 revocation of the initial charter, the [Massachusetts Bay Charter of 1691][ref_massachusetts_bay_charter_1691] reestablished the province under royal governance while extending liberty of conscience to Trinitarian Protestants.

The Half-Way Covenant of 1662 and subsequent adjustments to church membership requirements reflected the demographic problem that second and third generation New Englanders often could not report the conversion experiences their parents had reported and thus could not attain full church membership under strict Congregationalist requirements. The [Pope 1969][book_pope_1969] The Half-Way Covenant documents the adjustment and its consequences. The tension between covenantal purity and inclusive membership produced ongoing controversy through the seventeenth century and contributed to the religious energies that the First Great Awakening would later channel.

New England town governance combined Reformed covenantal ecclesiology with English common-law inheritance to produce a distinctive local political-economic form. The institutional pattern included town proprietors distributing land in proportions to household heads, common lands managed by town meeting, universal (male household head) participation in town governance, mandatory town-supported ministry, and mandatory town schools from 1647 forward under the Old Deluder Satan Law. The pattern produced high per-capita literacy rates documented by [Lockridge 1974][book_lockridge_1974] Literacy in Colonial New England, approaching

$$L_{\text{male, NE}}(1690) \approx 0.65 \text{ to } 0.90$$

depending on the measure and colony, substantially exceeding contemporary English rates and providing the base for subsequent New England intellectual and commercial development. Female literacy rates lagged male rates but also substantially exceeded contemporary European rates.

The Salem witch trials of 1692 provide the canonical crisis of Puritan New England, documented in [Boyer and Nissenbaum 1974][book_boyer_nissenbaum_1974] Salem Possessed and [Norton 2002][book_norton_2002] In the Devil's Snare. The primary court records survive in the [Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive and Transcription Project][ref_salem_witch_trials_papers] hosted by the University of Virginia. The contemporary Puritan clerical responses appear in the [Cotton Mather 1693 Wonders of the Invisible World][ref_mather_wonders_1693] defense of the proceedings and the parallel [Increase Mather 1693 Cases of Conscience Concerning Evil Spirits][ref_increase_mather_cases_of_conscience_1693] argument against the admissibility of spectral evidence. The episode reflected accumulating tensions including the French and Native raids on Maine and New Hampshire that displaced substantial populations into Essex County, factional disputes within Salem Village, and the theological crisis of accepting spectral evidence in judicial proceedings. The recorded casualties totaled

$$N_{\text{Salem, executed}} = 19, \quad N_{\text{Salem, died in prison}} = 5, \quad N_{\text{Salem, total deaths}} = 24$$

before Governor Phips halted the trials in October 1692. The subsequent public apologies (by Judge Samuel Sewall in 1697 and by the jurors collectively) marked the transition from the earlier Puritan certainty toward the more tempered Enlightenment religious sensibility documented in the eighteenth century.

Indigenous-Puritan encounters in New England followed the pattern established by [Salisbury 1982][book_salisbury_1982] Manitou and Providence. The initial Pilgrim-Wampanoag alliance of 1621 under Massasoit and Tisquantum provided the early cooperative pattern documented in [Bradford 1856][book_bradford_1856] Of Plymouth Plantation. The Pequot War of 1636-1638 documented in [Cave 1996][book_cave_1996] The Pequot War and [Karr 1998][research_karr_1998] Why Should You Be So Furious? established the pattern of English military conquest that would recur repeatedly. King Philip's War of 1675-1676, treated in [Lepore 1998][book_lepore_1998] The Name of War, [Drake 1999][book_drake_1999] King Philip's War, and [Pulsipher 2005][book_pulsipher_2005] Subjects unto the Same King, marked the violent breakdown of Puritan-Native coexistence in New England. The casualty ratios were substantial on both sides,

$$\frac{N_{\text{Anglo, dead}}}{P_{\text{Anglo, NE}}} \approx 0.05, \quad \frac{N_{\text{Native, dead}}}{P_{\text{Native, NE region}}} \approx 0.40$$

killing approximately five percent of the New England colonial population and approximately forty percent of the surviving Native population in the region. The war ended with the destruction of the Wampanoag as a political entity, the enslavement or displacement of surviving Natives, and the Puritan self-understanding of the war as divine chastisement documented in the [Increase Mather 1676 Brief History of the War with the Indians in New-England][ref_increase_mather_brief_history_1676]. The [Rowlandson 1682 Captivity Narrative][ref_rowlandson_captivity_narrative_1682] contemporary account of Mary Rowlandson's captivity at the hands of Narragansett captors provides the earliest widely-circulated colonial captivity narrative and the primary vernacular source through which subsequent generations remembered the war.

The praying-town system established by John Eliot from 1651 forward at Natick and thirteen subsequent villages provided the missionary alternative to conquest. Eliot's Massachusett-language translation of the Bible in 1663 (Mamusse Wunneetupanatamwe Up-Biblum God) constituted the first Bible printed in the Americas. Praying-town populations reached the peak

$$P_{\text{praying towns, peak}} \approx 1.1 \times 10^3 \text{ residents across 14 villages}$$

before King Philip's War substantially destroyed the system through both English mistrust of Christian Natives and Native rejection of the accommodations praying-town life required. The [Cogley 1999][book_cogley_1999] John Eliot's Mission to the Indians before King Philip's War treats the system and its collapse.

## Rhode Island, Anne Hutchinson, and the Baptist Tradition

Rhode Island's founding by refugees from Massachusetts Bay produced the colonial North American formation with the greatest religious pluralism and the earliest institutional guarantee of religious liberty. The colony's founding and development shaped subsequent American religious formations to a degree substantially exceeding its small colonial population would suggest. The [Gaustad 1991][book_gaustad_1991] Liberty of Conscience, [James 1975][book_james_1975] Colonial Rhode Island, [McLoughlin 1971][book_mcloughlin_1971] New England Dissent, [Byrd 2008][book_byrd_2008] The Challenges of Roger Williams, and [Barry 2012][book_barry_2012] Roger Williams and the Creation of the American Soul provide the comprehensive treatments.

Roger Williams (approximately 1603-1683), banished from Massachusetts Bay in 1636 for what the Bay authorities characterized as dangerous opinions concerning church-state relations and the invalidity of English Crown title to Native lands, founded Providence in 1636 on land purchased from the Narragansett. Williams's [Williams 1644][book_williams_1644] The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution developed the theological argument for religious liberty grounded in the fallenness of human political judgment and the priority of individual conscience under God. The argument would substantially influence subsequent English and American thought on religious accommodation.

The Antinomian Controversy of 1636-1638, treated in [Hall 1990][book_hall_1990] The Antinomian Controversy, [Winship 2002][book_winship_2002] Making Heretics, and [Lang 1987][book_lang_1987] Prophetic Woman, produced the second major dissenter migration from Massachusetts Bay to Rhode Island. Anne Hutchinson (1591-1643), whose home meetings for women had drawn substantial following, was tried and banished for teaching that immediate personal revelation could take precedence over church authority, that living saints could receive certainty of salvation without the covenantal signs the Bay orthodoxy required, and that leading Bay ministers were preaching a covenant of works rather than a covenant of grace. The controversy substantially reshaped Massachusetts Bay ecclesiology and produced the formalization of the Half-Way Covenant later treated. Hutchinson subsequently moved to Portsmouth (Aquidneck Island) and then to New Netherland, where she and most of her family were killed in Kieft's War of 1643.

The First Baptist Church in America, organized at Providence in 1638 under Williams, established the Baptist institutional presence in North America. The Baptist tradition, tracing to the 1609 formation in Amsterdam under John Smyth and the 1611 English organization under Thomas Helwys, combined believer's baptism, rejection of infant baptism, congregational polity, and the religious-liberty commitment. The [McLoughlin 1971][book_mcloughlin_1971] New England Dissent, [Brackney 2006][book_brackney_2006] Baptists in North America, and [Kidd and Hankins 2015][book_kidd_hankins_2015] Baptists in America provide the comprehensive treatments of the Baptist tradition through the colonial period. The confessional foundations appear in the [First London Baptist Confession 1644][ref_first_london_baptist_confession_1644] of the seven Particular Baptist congregations and its later expansion in the [Second London Baptist Confession 1689][ref_second_london_baptist_confession_1689] that subsequent Philadelphia and Charleston Baptist associations adopted with modifications.

The Rhode Island Charter of 1663, secured from Charles II through the efforts of John Clarke, provided the unprecedented royal guarantee of religious liberty,

$$t_{\text{RI religious liberty guarantee}} = 1663 \text{ CE}, \quad \Delta t_{\text{ahead of First Amendment}} = 1791 - 1663 = 128 \text{ years}$$

establishing the colonial precedent that the First Amendment religious clauses would substantially formalize approximately a century and a quarter later. The Charter permitted residents to hold, enjoy, and pursue their differing beliefs without interference by the colonial or metropolitan governments. The [Rhode Island Charter 1663][book_ri_charter_1663] provides the founding document.

Baptist expansion beyond Rhode Island proceeded slowly through the colonial period. The Regular Baptist tradition emerging from the middle colonies (Philadelphia Baptist Association, formed 1707) provided the institutional structure. The Particular Baptist stream from England contributed doctrinal Calvinist commitments distinct from the General Baptist Arminian stream. Baptist growth would accelerate substantially through the First Great Awakening treated in article three, producing the Separate Baptist movement that would substantially reshape the American religious landscape from the middle eighteenth century forward.

The Rhode Island religious pluralism attracted substantial Jewish, Quaker, and other minority religious populations. Newport's Sephardic community treated above began arriving in 1658 attracted by the religious accommodation. The Quaker community established substantial presence, and later Baptist, Congregationalist, Anglican, and other communities coexisted under the Charter's religious liberty framework. The [Bonomi 1986][book_bonomi_1986] Under the Cope of Heaven documents the colonial religious diversity across the middle Atlantic and Rhode Island.

## Cavalier Virginia and the Chesapeake

The Cavalier Virginia and Chesapeake formation, following the second folkway in Fischer's framework, established the slavery-based tobacco plantation economy that would substantially shape the American South. The [Morgan 1975][book_morgan_1975] American Slavery, American Freedom provides the classical treatment of the paradox in which Virginia's political freedoms developed in interdependence with its slave institution. The [Kulikoff 1986][book_kulikoff_1986] Tobacco and Slaves develops the demographic and economic analysis. The [Isaac 1982][book_isaac_1982] The Transformation of Virginia treats the religious and cultural formation. The [Breen 1980][book_breen_1980] Puritans and Adventurers develops the comparative treatment between the New England and Chesapeake patterns. The [Jordan 1968][book_jordan_1968] White over Black provides the foundational treatment of the emergence of specifically-racial slavery in the seventeenth-century Chesapeake. The [Brown 1996][book_brown_1996] Good Wives, Nasty Wenches, and Anxious Patriarchs treats the gendered and racial construction of Virginia society. The [Fredrickson 1981][book_fredrickson_1981] White Supremacy provides the comparative treatment of Southern racial ideology against South African development.

Anglican ecclesiology, established by law in Virginia from 1619 forward, provided the institutional foundation for Virginia's religious formation. The parish system, which combined a legally-established Church of England with substantial vestry autonomy, produced local governance patterns substantially different from the New England Congregationalist pattern. Parish vestries, elected by parish members who were initially all landowners and later restricted, managed local ecclesiastical affairs and levied taxes for church support. The [Bond 2000][book_bond_2000] Damned Souls in a Tobacco Colony and [Nelson 2001][book_nelson_2001] A Blessed Company treat the Anglican Virginia religious formation. The liturgical foundation of colonial Anglican worship rested on the [Book of Common Prayer 1662][ref_book_of_common_prayer_1662] adopted after the Restoration and used throughout the colonial parishes until the Revolutionary-era revisions.

The demographic conditions of the early Chesapeake produced substantially different religious dynamics from New England. The Chesapeake experienced substantial disease mortality (particularly from malaria in tidewater regions), skewed sex ratios, late marriage, and comparatively low natural increase. The seventeenth-century Chesapeake sex ratio approached

$$\text{Sex ratio (M:F, Chesapeake, mid-17th c.)} \gtrsim 2$$

and life expectancy at age twenty was substantially lower than the contemporary New England pattern,

$$e_{20, \text{Chesapeake}} \approx 25 \text{ years}, \quad e_{20, \text{New England}} \approx 45 \text{ years}$$

per the [Rutman and Rutman 1984][book_rutman_rutman_1984] A Place in Time reconstruction for Middlesex County and the comparable New England reconstructions. The mortality rate produced high orphaning and remarriage rates that shaped family structure. The Anglican religious infrastructure was substantially thinner than the New England Congregationalist infrastructure through the seventeenth century.

Bacon's Rebellion of 1676 documented in [Washburn 1957][book_washburn_1957] The Governor and the Rebel and [Rice 2012][book_rice_2012] Tales from a Revolution marked the inflection point after which the Virginia elite substantially shifted from the earlier reliance on indentured European labor toward the reliance on enslaved African labor. The rebellion's programmatic self-statement appears in [Bacon's Declaration in the Name of the People 1676][ref_bacon_declaration_1676]. The rebellion, led by Nathaniel Bacon against Governor Berkeley in the aftermath of frontier conflicts with Native peoples, combined class and racial dynamics whose interpretation remains contested. The subsequent Virginia slave codes of the 1680s and 1690s substantially formalized the racialized chattel slavery that would define the Southern political economy for the next two centuries, culminating in the comprehensive consolidation of the [Virginia Slave Code 1705][ref_virginia_slave_code_1705] An Act Concerning Servants and Slaves. The [Berlin 1998][book_berlin_1998] Many Thousands Gone and [Parent 2003][book_parent_2003] Foul Means treat the transition.

The tobacco economy shaped the Chesapeake religious and cultural formation in ways different from the Puritan agricultural economy. Tobacco cultivation required substantial labor inputs, produced substantial cash returns, and encouraged the dispersed plantation settlement pattern rather than the nucleated village pattern of New England. The settlement pattern produced weaker parish-based religious institutions, higher landowning household autonomy, and the gentry-centered cultural formation Fischer characterizes as Cavalier. The tobacco cultivation techniques themselves derived from indigenous Chesapeake practice adopted from Powhatan agriculture, documented in [Rountree 1990][book_rountree_1990] Pocahontas's People and [Kupperman 1980][book_kupperman_1980] Settling with the Indians.

Anglo-Powhatan relations followed the pattern of initial cooperation, escalating conflict, and eventual conquest documented in [Rountree 1990][book_rountree_1990]. The contemporary Virginia gentleman's account of the colony's first century, including its Native and religious history, appears in the [Beverley 1705 History and Present State of Virginia][ref_beverley_history_virginia_1705]. The Powhatan uprising of March 1622 killed

$$N_{\text{Powhatan uprising, English dead, 1622}} \approx 350$$

approximately one-third of the entire English colonial population at that date, and marked the end of the initial cooperative phase. The Second Anglo-Powhatan War of 1644 and its resolution through the 1646 Treaty of Powhatan substantially subordinated the surviving Native peoples of the tidewater to Virginia sovereignty. The dispossession of Powhatan lands proceeded through the second half of the seventeenth century.

## Quaker Delaware Valley

The Quaker migration to the Delaware Valley under William Penn's 1681 proprietorship established the religious and political formation that would substantially shape Pennsylvania and the middle colonies. The [Nash 1968][book_nash_1968] Quakers and Politics, [Barbour and Frost 1988][book_barbour_frost_1988] The Quakers, [Frost 1973][book_frost_1973] The Quaker Family in Colonial America, [Levy 1988][book_levy_1988] Quakers and the American Family, and [Marietta 1984][book_marietta_1984] The Reformation of American Quakerism provide the comprehensive treatments. The [Tolles 1948][book_tolles_1948] Meeting House and Counting House provides the foundational modern treatment of the Quaker Philadelphia commercial culture and its distinctive combination of religious inwardness with mercantile activity. The [James 1963][book_james_1963] A People Among Peoples treats the political history of Quakers in colonial America across the eighteenth century.

Religious Society of Friends ecclesiology, following George Fox's founding of the movement in the 1650s and its subsequent institutional consolidation, provided the consensus-governance, plain-dress, peace-testimony religious tradition that Penn transferred to Pennsylvania. The religious practice included silent worship punctuated by inspired vocal ministry, refusal of oaths and hat-honor, plain speech (thee-thou address, refusal of pagan-derived day and month names), plain dress, refusal of military service, and consensus decision-making through the meeting for business structure. The [Fox 1694][book_fox_1694] Journal provides the founder's own account, and the [Barclay 1678][book_barclay_1678] Apology for the True Christian Divinity provides the systematic theological statement.

Pennsylvania's founding legal framework, encoded in the 1682 Frame of Government and subsequent revisions, established religious toleration for all who acknowledged one God, universal male suffrage among Christians, prohibition of military establishment, and commitments to peaceful relations with Native peoples. The [Frame of Government 1682][book_frame_of_government_1682] provides the founding document, and the earlier [Pennsylvania Charter 1681][ref_pennsylvania_charter_1681] issued by Charles II to William Penn provided the proprietary grant of the province. The initial statutory codification appeared in the [Great Law of Pennsylvania 1682][ref_great_law_pennsylvania_1682] whose first act guaranteed liberty of conscience across Trinitarian Christian communions. The [Pennsylvania Charter of Privileges 1701][ref_charter_of_privileges_1701] later replaced the earlier frames and functioned as the effective Pennsylvania constitution until the American Revolution. The earlier proprietary [Concessions and Agreements of West New Jersey 1677][ref_concessions_west_jersey_1677] provided the parallel Quaker legal framework for the adjacent province and prefigured the Pennsylvania arrangements. William Penn's own reflective aphorisms on personal and civil ethics appear in [Penn 1682 Some Fruits of Solitude][ref_penn_some_fruits_of_solitude_1682]. The religious pluralism the framework enabled produced the rapid growth of German Pietist, Anabaptist, Sephardic Jewish, Anglican, Presbyterian, Reformed, and other communities in Pennsylvania across the following decades.

Quaker peace policy in relations with Native peoples produced the extended peace that distinguished Pennsylvania from other colonies through Penn's proprietorship. The 1682 Treaty of Shackamaxon (the documented existence of which is contested but whose spirit shaped subsequent policy) inaugurated a period of substantial Quaker-Lenape cooperation lasting approximately

$$t_{\text{Quaker-Lenape peace}} = 1737 - 1682 = 55 \text{ years}$$

The [Marshall 1967][book_marshall_1967] William Penn and the Founding of Pennsylvania and [Soderlund 2015][book_soderlund_2015] Lenape Country provide the treatments. The 1737 Walking Purchase, treated in [Grumet 2009][book_grumet_2009] The Munsee Indians, marked the end of the initial cooperative phase when Penn's sons substantially defrauded the Lenape of upper Delaware Valley lands through a fraudulent interpretation of an earlier land agreement.

The Quaker tradition produced substantial contributions to later American political and social development documented in later articles of this series. The antislavery tradition emerging from the [Germantown Quaker Petition Against Slavery 1688][ref_germantown_petition_1688] and the subsequent 1758 Philadelphia Yearly Meeting minute disowning slaveholding members produced the first organized abolitionist movement in the corpus of European-American ethnoreligious traditions. The [Soderlund 1985][book_soderlund_1985] Quakers and Slavery treats the formation. Quaker contributions to prison reform, mental health treatment, education (including women's higher education), and the peace movement recur throughout later articles.

## Scots-Irish Backcountry Formation

The Scots-Irish backcountry formation, following the fourth folkway in Fischer's framework, arrived through substantial migration from Ulster from approximately 1717 forward and settled principally in the Appalachian backcountry from Pennsylvania south through the Carolinas and Georgia. The [Fischer 1989][book_fischer_1989] Albion's Seed, [Griffin 2001][book_griffin_2001] The People with No Name, [Leyburn 1962][book_leyburn_1962] The Scotch-Irish, and [Ford 1915][book_ford_1915] The Scotch-Irish in America provide the comprehensive treatments. The [Kenny 2000][book_kenny_2000] The American Irish treats the broader Irish American formation from the colonial period forward including both the Scots-Irish Presbyterian and the later Catholic Irish streams. The [Cunningham 1987][book_cunningham_1987] Apples on the Flood documents the cultural transmission from Ulster to the American backcountry.

Presbyterian ecclesiology, transferred from Ulster and Scotland, provided the institutional foundation for Scots-Irish religious formation. The Presbyterian synod-based governance combined local congregational autonomy with representative regional and national governance through the hierarchy of session, presbytery, synod, and general assembly. The religious commitments included substantial biblicism, subscription to the [Westminster Confession of Faith 1647][ref_westminster_confession_1647] as the doctrinal standard, and the historically-driven distrust of Anglican and Catholic ecclesiastical hierarchy that Ulster experience had substantially reinforced. The [Westerkamp 1988][book_westerkamp_1988] Triumph of the Laity treats the Scots-Irish revivalist religious pattern that would substantially shape the First Great Awakening.

The Scots-Irish frontier cultural pattern combined honor-based interpersonal relations, clan-based extended family organization, hunter-farmer economic patterns, and religious commitments in ways that Fischer characterizes as the border culture. The pattern shaped the development of Appalachian and Southern rural culture across subsequent centuries. The Scots-Irish contribution to the American Revolution, particularly through the role of Presbyterian clergy in Revolutionary mobilization, is treated in article three.

Scots-Irish encounters with Cherokee, Shawnee, and other Southeastern indigenous peoples followed the frontier pattern combining conflict, trade, intermarriage, and religious syncretism. The documented cases of Scots-Irish men marrying into Cherokee matrilineal clans produced substantial mixed-descent populations documented by [Perdue 1998][book_perdue_1998] Cherokee Women and [McLoughlin 1984][book_mcloughlin_1984] The Cherokees and Christianity. The Cherokee acceptance of some Scots-Irish traders and settlers into clan structures reflected the matrilineal kinship system through which Cherokee identity was transmitted. The implications for later Cherokee political and religious formation are treated in article four.

## Dutch Reformed New Netherland

The Dutch Reformed New Netherland colony, established 1614 as a trading operation and formally colonized from 1624 forward under the West India Company, produced the ethnoreligious formation that would substantially shape New York after the 1664 English conquest. The [Rink 1986][book_rink_1986] Holland on the Hudson, [Jacobs 2009][book_jacobs_2009] The Colony of New Netherland, [Shorto 2004][book_shorto_2004] The Island at the Center of the World, and [Goodfriend 1992][book_goodfriend_1992] Before the Melting Pot provide the comprehensive modern treatments.

Dutch Reformed ecclesiology, established as the official religion of the colony, provided the institutional foundation for New Netherland religious formation. The ecclesiology combined Calvinist doctrine with the presbyterian-synodal governance of the Dutch Reformed Church, which had emerged from the Dutch Revolt (1568-1648) as the state church of the Dutch Republic. The [Fabend 2000][book_fabend_2000] Zion on the Hudson treats the New Netherland Dutch Reformed formation and its persistence after the English conquest.

The Dutch pattern of religious pluralism, established in Amsterdam and other Dutch cities across the seventeenth century, produced the de facto religious toleration in New Netherland despite the Reformed establishment. The colony's population included substantial Lutheran, Anabaptist (Mennonite), Catholic, Quaker, and Jewish minorities alongside the Dutch Reformed majority. Peter Stuyvesant's attempt to expel the twenty-three Sephardic Jews who arrived at New Amsterdam in 1654 from Recife was overruled by the West India Company (which had substantial Sephardic investors), establishing the precedent for continuing Jewish presence in New Amsterdam and later New York. The religious pluralism produced the New York character that distinguished it from more religiously uniform colonies.

The Dutch colonial pattern also included substantial Dutch-Native trade and diplomatic relations, particularly with the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy through the Fort Orange (later Albany) trading post. The [Merwick 1990][book_merwick_1990] Possessing Albany treats the Dutch-Iroquois trading relationship. The Dutch trading pattern differed from the English settler-agricultural pattern in ways that produced substantially different indigenous outcomes. Dutch New Netherland also included substantial enslaved African populations, imported through Dutch Atlantic trade networks in numbers proportional to the Dutch colonial population greater than in most contemporary English colonies. The [Foote 2004][book_foote_2004] Black and White Manhattan documents the New Amsterdam African community.

The 1664 English conquest of New Netherland and its rename as New York produced substantial institutional continuity through the negotiated surrender terms guaranteeing property rights, religious practice, and continued Dutch civic participation. Dutch Reformed churches continued to conduct services in Dutch through the eighteenth century, and Dutch-descent families remained politically prominent in New York through the American Revolution and beyond. The persistence produced the New York pluralistic ethnoreligious formation that distinguished the colony and later state from more homogeneous alternatives.

## New Sweden and Middle Colony Lutheran Presence

The New Sweden colony on the Delaware Bay, established 1638 by the New Sweden Company under Peter Minuit (formerly of New Netherland) and continuing under Swedish administration until 1655 when the Dutch conquest incorporated the territory into New Netherland, produced the Lutheran presence in the middle Atlantic that would substantially shape the later German Lutheran migration and the Delaware Valley religious pluralism. The [Johnson 1911][book_johnson_1911] The Swedes on the Delaware and [Weslager 1988][book_weslager_1988] New Sweden on the Delaware provide the comprehensive treatments.

Swedish Lutheran ecclesiology, transferred through the Swedish Church of Sweden establishment, provided the institutional foundation for New Sweden religious formation. The colony's Lutheran ministers, sent by the Consistory of the Archdiocese of Uppsala, established the Trinity Lutheran Church at Wicaco (1677, near present Philadelphia), Gloria Dei (Old Swedes' Church) at Wicaco (1697), and Holy Trinity at Wilmington (1698). The Swedish Lutheran presence continued through the eighteenth century until the Anglican merger of the Swedish churches into the Episcopal Church substantially completed the institutional transition. The [Craig 2006][book_craig_2006] History of the Delaware Colony treats the development.

The Finnish population within New Sweden, comprising approximately thirty percent of the colony's arriving population, brought the Karelian and Savonian Finnish traditions including the specialty of forest agriculture (svedjebruk, the slash-and-burn technique developed for Fennoscandian conditions) that substantially shaped the Delaware Valley and Appalachian frontier practice. The [Wuorinen 1938][book_wuorinen_1938] The Finns on the Delaware documents the Finnish contribution.

Swedish and Finnish relations with the Lenape and Susquehannock peoples followed the Nordic pattern of trade partnership more similar to the French and Dutch patterns than to the English settlement-agricultural pattern. The accommodation produced the durable Native alliances that persisted through the Dutch and later English administrations. The [Kupperman 2007][book_kupperman_2007] The Jamestown Project treats the comparative context of English versus other European colonial patterns of Native relations.

The Salzburger Lutheran migration to Georgia from 1734 forward, though falling outside the strict 1607-1725 temporal frame of this article, extended the Continental Lutheran presence to the Southern colonies. Approximately three hundred Salzburger Protestants, expelled from Salzburg Archbishopric under Archbishop Firmian in 1731, migrated through Augsburg and London to Georgia under the auspices of General James Oglethorpe. The Ebenezer settlement near Savannah produced the model of pietist agricultural community that would substantially influence the later German Lutheran migrations to Pennsylvania and elsewhere. The [Wilson 1984][book_wilson_1984] Piety and Toleration treats the Salzburger migration.

The Lutheran presence would substantially expand through the eighteenth-century German migration to Pennsylvania treated below and through the institutional consolidation under Henry Melchior Muhlenberg (arriving 1742) into the Lutheran Ministerium of Pennsylvania (1748), the first permanent Lutheran denominational structure in North America. The [Roeber 1993][book_roeber_1993] Palatines, Liberty, and Property and [Nelson 1998][book_nelson_1998] The Lutherans in North America treat the consolidation.

## Catholic Maryland

The Catholic Maryland colony, established 1634 under the proprietorship of Cecilius Calvert as the second Baron Baltimore who inherited the grant on the death of his father George, provided a refuge for English Catholics under continuing legal disabilities and produced the Catholic-Protestant coexistence pattern that would substantially shape American religious pluralism. The proprietary framework rested on the [Maryland Charter 1632][ref_maryland_charter_1632] granted by Charles I. The [Krugler 2004][book_krugler_2004] English and Catholic, [Curran 2014][book_curran_2014] Papist Devils, and [Hardy 1996][book_hardy_1996] From Salem to Shrewsbury provide the comprehensive treatments.

The 1649 Act Concerning Religion (Maryland Toleration Act) formalized the religious accommodation, guaranteeing free exercise for all Trinitarian Christians while continuing the death penalty for denial of the Trinity. The act reflected both the Catholic proprietorship's interest in protecting Catholic religious practice and the pragmatic accommodation of substantial Protestant migration to Maryland. The [Maryland Toleration Act 1649][book_maryland_toleration_1649] provides the founding document. The act has been cited as an early precursor to First Amendment religious accommodation, though the limitations (Trinitarian Christians only, death penalty for denial of the Trinity, Catholic proprietorship's interest) substantially qualify the claim.

The Puritan revolt of 1654 during the English Civil War substantially reversed the Toleration Act through the Puritan-controlled Maryland Assembly's suspension of Catholic religious rights. The Battle of the Severn of 1655 marked the armed conflict. The 1660 Restoration returned the Calvert proprietorship and reestablished the Toleration Act, but the 1689 Glorious Revolution in England produced the 1691 replacement of Maryland proprietorship with royal governance and the 1702 Anglican establishment. Catholic Maryland thus persisted as a distinctive formation only through approximately seventy years of institutional stability. The [Menard 1988][book_menard_1988] Economy and Society in Early Colonial Maryland and [Carr Menard and Walsh 1991][book_carr_menard_walsh_1991] Robert Cole's World treat the colonial development.

Catholic religious practice under the Anglican establishment continued through the network of Catholic gentry households (particularly the Carroll family), Jesuit missions, and the accommodations reached with sympathetic or indifferent Anglican authorities. The Charles Carroll of Carrollton (1737-1832), signer of the Declaration of Independence, emerged from this Catholic gentry tradition and provides the link to the American Revolutionary generation treated in article three. The [Hoffman 2000][book_hoffman_2000] Princes of Ireland, Planters of Maryland documents the Carroll family formation across the colonial period.

## Sephardic Jewish Communities

Sephardic Jewish arrival in North America began with the 1654 arrival of twenty-three refugees from Recife, Brazil at New Amsterdam. The [Marcus 1970][book_marcus_1970] The Colonial American Jew, [Sarna 2004][book_sarna_2004] American Judaism, [Faber 1992][book_faber_1992] A Time for Planting, and [Diner 2004][book_diner_2004] The Jews of the United States provide the comprehensive treatments. The [Snyder 2003][research_snyder_2003] article on colonial Jewish women and gender documents the patterns of Jewish family formation and community organization across the colonial North American communities.

The Sephardic diaspora from Iberia through the Netherlands, Ottoman lands, and eventually the Americas followed the 1492 Alhambra Decree, 1497 Portuguese forced conversion, and subsequent expulsions of Iberian conversos across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Amsterdam became the European center of Sephardic Jewish intellectual and commercial life through the seventeenth century, producing figures including Menasseh ben Israel (who negotiated the return of Jews to England under Cromwell in 1656) and Baruch Spinoza. Dutch Brazil (1630-1654) provided a Sephardic community until the Portuguese reconquest forced the dispersal that produced the 1654 New Amsterdam arrival.

Congregation Shearith Israel (New York, 1654) established the first American Jewish congregation. The congregation maintained Sephardic ritual practice through the eighteenth century despite Ashkenazi Jewish migration that would substantially shift the American Jewish demographic composition by the nineteenth century. The [Pool 1955][book_pool_1955] An Old Faith in the New World treats the Shearith Israel formation.

Congregation Jeshuat Israel (Newport, Rhode Island, formally organized 1758 but with continuous Jewish presence from 1658) established the Newport Jewish community whose commercial and religious contributions shaped the Newport formation. The Touro Synagogue (dedicated 1763) provides the oldest extant synagogue building in the United States. The [Chyet 1970][book_chyet_1970] Lopez of Newport treats the merchant Aaron Lopez whose Atlantic trade activities included both legitimate commerce and substantial participation in the transatlantic slave trade. The Jewish participation in the colonial slave trade, documented in [Faber 1998][book_faber_1998] Jews Slaves and the Slave Trade, is treated more fully in article four.

Congregation Mikveh Israel (Philadelphia, 1740), Congregation Beth Elohim (Charleston, 1749), Congregation Kahal Kadosh Beth Elohim (Charleston, alternative name), and other colonial Sephardic congregations extended the institutional network across the Atlantic seaboard. The Charleston Jewish community became substantial through the eighteenth century and produced the largest Jewish population in any American city by 1800.

The colonial Sephardic contribution to American commercial development, particularly through Atlantic trade networks connecting Amsterdam, London, the Caribbean, and North American ports, provided substantial capital and mercantile expertise that shaped colonial economic development. The Sephardic diaspora network produced the credit and information channels that facilitated Atlantic commerce, documented in [Trivellato 2009][book_trivellato_2009] The Familiarity of Strangers.

## Huguenot Diaspora and Settlement

The Huguenot diaspora, produced by the 1685 Revocation of the Edict of Nantes under Louis XIV, dispersed approximately

$$N_{\text{Huguenot, total diaspora}} \approx 2 \times 10^5, \quad N_{\text{Huguenot, North America}} \sim (5 \text{ to } 10) \times 10^3$$

French Protestants to England, the Netherlands, German states, and North America, with the North American proportion approximately three to five percent of the total. The [Butler 1983][book_butler_1983] The Huguenots in America and [Van Ruymbeke 2006][book_van_ruymbeke_2006] From New Babylon to Eden provide the comprehensive treatments. The [Diefendorf 1991][book_diefendorf_1991] Beneath the Cross treats the earlier French Wars of Religion that produced the Reformed French community and its subsequent legal status.

North American Huguenot settlement concentrated in communities at New Rochelle (New York, from 1687), New Paltz (New York, from 1677), Charleston (South Carolina, from 1685), and Manakintown (Virginia, from 1700). The Charleston Huguenot community produced substantial commercial and political contributions through the eighteenth century, documented in [Hirsch 1928][book_hirsch_1928] The Huguenots of Colonial South Carolina and [Van Ruymbeke 2006][book_van_ruymbeke_2006]. The Charleston Huguenot Church, founded 1687 and rebuilt several times to the present building of 1845, remains the only independent Huguenot congregation in the United States.

The Huguenot pattern of assimilation into the receiving English colonial society differed substantially from the pattern of many other refugee streams. Huguenots substantially intermarried with English colonists, adopted Anglican church membership in Charleston and elsewhere, anglicized their surnames within one or two generations, and substantially assimilated into the local elite. The pattern reflected the Reformed doctrinal compatibility between Huguenot and Anglican theology (both descended from Reformed traditions despite differences in ecclesiology), the absence of a persistent French Huguenot ecclesiastical infrastructure in America, and the social advantages of assimilation. The [Van Ruymbeke 2006][book_van_ruymbeke_2006] treatment documents the assimilation trajectory.

The Huguenot contribution to American political-economic development includes individual descendants (Paul Revere, John Jay, Francis Marion, and many others) whose names appear repeatedly across American history, the mercantile and craft skills the Huguenot community brought to the colonies, and the religious sensibility emphasizing personal covenant with God that shaped later American religious formations through Huguenot influence on Reformed and evangelical streams.

## German Pietist and Anabaptist Migration

German-speaking migration to Pennsylvania and neighboring colonies began substantially with the 1683 arrival of Mennonite families from Krefeld under Franz Daniel Pastorius, who founded Germantown, and continued through the eighteenth century to produce approximately one hundred thousand German-speaking arrivals by 1775. The [Roeber 1993][book_roeber_1993] Palatines, Liberty, and Property, [Longenecker 1994][book_longenecker_1994] Piety and Tolerance, [Fogleman 1996][book_fogleman_1996] Hopeful Journeys, and [Stoeffler 1965][book_stoeffler_1965] The Rise of Evangelical Pietism provide the comprehensive treatments.

The religious composition of German-speaking migration included substantial Lutheran, German Reformed (both mainstream Continental Reformed), Pietist (following Spener and Francke), Anabaptist (Mennonite, Amish, Brethren), and Moravian streams. The [Longenecker 1994][book_longenecker_1994] and [Fogleman 1996][book_fogleman_1996] treatments quantify the composition. Substantial Roman Catholic German migration also occurred, particularly to areas of Pennsylvania where the Catholic religious presence was substantially tolerated under the Quaker framework.

The Ephrata Cloister, established 1732 in Lancaster County by Conrad Beissel, provided a German Pietist celibate communal experiment combining elements of Anabaptist ecclesiology, Radical Pietist theology, and communal practice. The community produced substantial religious literature (particularly hymnody) and Fraktur illuminated manuscripts that shaped the Pennsylvania German cultural tradition. The [Bach 2003][book_bach_2003] Voices of the Turtledoves treats the Ephrata community.

The Moravian Brethren settlements at Bethlehem (Pennsylvania, 1741), Nazareth (Pennsylvania, 1740), and Salem (North Carolina, 1766) under the leadership of Count Nicolaus Ludwig von Zinzendorf produced the Renewed Unity of the Brethren tradition. The Moravian communal economy (the Ökonomie) practiced substantial community of goods through approximately the 1760s. Moravian missionary activity to Native peoples in New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, and elsewhere produced substantial conversions and the Moravian mission stations that persisted through the nineteenth century. The [Merritt 2003][book_merritt_2003] At the Crossroads treats the Moravian-Native encounter, and [Sensbach 2005][book_sensbach_2005] Rebecca's Revival treats the Moravian missionary activity among enslaved and free Africans, particularly through the figure of Rebecca Protten.

The Amish separation of 1693 under Jakob Ammann from the broader Swiss Mennonite tradition produced the Amish Mennonite formation that would migrate to Pennsylvania from 1737 forward. The Old Order Amish community persists to the present as one of the fastest-growing religious minorities in the United States, with approximately

$$P_{\text{Amish}}(2025) \approx 3.5 \times 10^5$$

adherents. The population has doubled approximately every twenty years since the mid twentieth century, corresponding to

$$r_{\text{Amish growth}} = \frac{\ln 2}{20} \approx 3.5 \text{ percent per year}$$

driven by very high fertility (approximately seven live births per completed family) combined with high retention rates. The [Hostetler 1993][book_hostetler_1993] Amish Society and [Kraybill Johnson-Weiner and Nolt 2013][book_kraybill_johnson_weiner_nolt_2013] The Amish provide the comprehensive treatments.

The Brethren (Dunker) tradition, founded 1708 at Schwarzenau under Alexander Mack in the German lands and migrating to Germantown from 1719 forward, produced the Church of the Brethren tradition. The Brethren combined Anabaptist believer-baptism and pacifism with Pietist personal-experience emphasis and liturgical practices including trine immersion baptism, the love feast, and feet washing. The [Durnbaugh 1997][book_durnbaugh_1997] Fruit of the Vine treats the Brethren formation.

The German-speaking colonial Pennsylvania community produced substantial contributions to American political-economic development. The German farming techniques (including the use of large barn structures, four-course crop rotation, and dairy and livestock patterns) substantially shaped the Pennsylvania and Ohio agricultural formation. The German craft traditions in printing, ironworking, gunsmithing (the Pennsylvania long rifle), and glassmaking provided substantial industrial base for the American Revolutionary and early republican economy. The Pennsylvania Dutch (Deitsch) language, culture, and cuisine persist in communities to the present.

## Spanish-Native Syncretism in the Southwest and Florida

Spanish colonial religious activity in what would become the American Southwest and Southeast preceded English colonial activity by approximately a century and produced substantial ethnoreligious formations distinct from the English colonial pattern. The [Weber 1992][book_weber_1992] The Spanish Frontier in North America, [Weber 2005][book_weber_2005] Bárbaros, [Gutiérrez 1991][book_gutierrez_1991] When Jesus Came, the Corn Mothers Went Away, [Milanich 1999][book_milanich_1999] Laboring in the Fields of the Lord, [Kessell 2002][book_kessell_2002] Spain in the Southwest, [Bushnell 1994][book_bushnell_1994] Situado and Sabana, and [Barr 2007][book_barr_2007] Peace Came in the Form of a Woman provide the comprehensive treatments. The [Reséndez 2016][book_resendez_2016] The Other Slavery documents the Native slavery system that operated across Spanish and later Mexican and American territorial jurisdictions from the sixteenth century into the nineteenth, providing the counterweight to Africanist-only treatments of American slavery.

Spanish Florida missionary activity centered on the St. Augustine settlement (founded 1565) and extended through the Franciscan mission chain across northern Florida and southern Georgia. The mission chain reached approximately

$$N_{\text{Florida mission stations, 1675}} \approx 40, \quad N_{\text{Florida mission converts, 1675}} \approx 3 \times 10^4$$

Timucua and Guale converts, with the mission system combining Franciscan religious instruction with labor obligations under the repartimiento system through which mission Natives supplied labor to St. Augustine. The [Milanich 1999][book_milanich_1999] and [Hann 1988][book_hann_1988] Apalachee treat the mission system.

The 1704 English-Creek raids under James Moore of South Carolina, treated in [Ramsey 2008][book_ramsey_2008] The Yamasee War, substantially destroyed the Spanish Florida mission system through the killing and enslavement of mission Natives and the burning of mission stations. The pattern of English-Creek raiding for Native slaves supplying South Carolina and Caribbean markets is treated in [Gallay 2002][book_gallay_2002] The Indian Slave Trade. The scale of Native capture was substantial,

$$N_{\text{Native enslaved from Spanish Florida, 1702 to 1706}} \approx 1 \times 10^4$$

substantially depopulating the mission territory before eventual Spanish reoccupation. The episode illustrates how European interimperial competition produced substantial harm to indigenous populations subordinated to any European power.

Spanish New Mexico settlement, following the 1598 Oñate expedition and the establishment of San Juan de los Caballeros and subsequent Santa Fe (from 1610), produced the Franciscan mission system serving the Pueblo peoples. The mission chain reached

$$N_{\text{Pueblo mission stations, 1680}} \approx 50, \quad N_{\text{Pueblo mission converts, 1680}} \approx 1.7 \times 10^4$$

with the mission system combining religious instruction with substantial labor and tribute obligations under encomienda and repartimiento arrangements. The Franciscan pattern combined some accommodation of preexisting Pueblo religious practice with substantial suppression of practices (particularly kachina ceremonies and traditional religious specialist activity) that produced accumulating Pueblo resistance. The [Gutiérrez 1991][book_gutierrez_1991] and [Kessell 1979][book_kessell_1979] Kiva, Cross, and Crown treat the pre-1680 mission system.

The Pueblo Revolt of August 1680, led by Popé (Popay) of San Juan Pueblo, expelled the Spanish colonizers from New Mexico for twelve years and produced the most successful indigenous uprising against European colonial authority in North American history. The casualties and displacement were substantial,

$$N_{\text{Pueblo Revolt, colonist dead}} \approx 400, \quad \frac{N_{\text{Franciscan priests dead}}}{N_{\text{Franciscan priests total}}} = \frac{21}{33}, \quad N_{\text{colonist refugees to El Paso}} \approx 2 \times 10^3$$

destroying the mission infrastructure across the specifically-affected mission chain. The [Knaut 1995][book_knaut_1995] The Pueblo Revolt of 1680, [Sando 1992][book_sando_1992] Pueblo Nations, and [Preucel 2002][book_preucel_2002] Archaeologies of the Pueblo Revolt treat the episode.

The Spanish reconquest of 1692 under Diego de Vargas returned Spanish authority to New Mexico under substantially modified terms. The accommodation reached with Pueblo peoples included the tolerance of continued kachina and traditional religious practice alongside the Catholic mission activity, the end of encomienda tribute demands, and the integration of Pueblo governance structures with Spanish colonial administration through the gobernadorcillo system. The pattern produced the syncretic Pueblo Catholicism that persists to the present, combining Catholic sacramental practice with Pueblo ritual traditions in ways that neither the Spanish colonial authorities nor Pueblo religious authorities substantially objected to during the eighteenth century. The [Gutiérrez 1991][book_gutierrez_1991] and [Wilcox 2009][book_wilcox_2009] The Pueblo Revolt and the Mythology of Conquest treat the accommodation.

The Franco-Spanish borderlands with Louisiana (from 1682 forward under French rule, from 1763 forward under Spanish rule) produced additional syncretic religious formations documented by [Usner 1992][book_usner_1992] Indians, Settlers, and Slaves and [Din 1988][book_din_1988] The Canary Islanders of Louisiana. The Caddoan, Tunican, and Muskogean peoples in the region maintained substantial religious autonomy while engaging with French and Spanish colonial trade and mission activity.

Spanish Alta California mission activity from 1769 forward under Junípero Serra falls outside the 1607-1725 temporal frame of this article but reflects the continuation of the Franciscan mission model that had earlier operated in Florida and New Mexico. The California mission system is treated in article four in connection with the 1848 Mexican Cession and Anglo-American conquest of the territory.

## French Colonial Louisiana and Catholic-African Syncretism

The French colonial Louisiana, established 1699 under Iberville and Bienville and formally colonized from 1717 forward under John Law's Mississippi Company, produced the Franco-Native-African religious formation that would substantially shape the Louisiana culture. The [Hall 1992][book_hall_1992] Africans in Colonial Louisiana, [Din 1988][book_din_1988] The Canary Islanders of Louisiana, [Usner 1992][book_usner_1992] Indians, Settlers, and Slaves, and [Vidal 2014][book_vidal_2014] Louisiana provide the comprehensive treatments. The [Clark 2007][book_clark_2007] Masterless Mistresses treats the New Orleans Ursuline nuns and the free Black women whose religious and economic activities substantially shaped the colonial New Orleans formation. The [Hanger 1997][book_hanger_1997] Bounded Lives, Bounded Places treats the free Black population of Spanish colonial New Orleans that emerged from and extended the earlier French colonial patterns. The [Spear 2009][book_spear_2009] Race, Sex, and Social Order in Early New Orleans treats the patterns of racial and sexual regulation across the French and Spanish colonial periods.

Catholic ecclesiology under the French colonial authority provided the official religious establishment. Capuchin and Jesuit missionaries operated among Native and enslaved African populations with substantially different approaches. The Jesuit mission activity produced substantial conversion documentation but also substantial accommodation of preexisting religious practice, particularly among the Illinois, Natchez, and other Native peoples of the Louisiana territory. The French pattern of métis identity formation, common across French North American colonies, produced the Louisiana Creole formation combining French, Native, and African descent and religious practice.

The African population of French colonial Louisiana included substantial arrivals directly from Senegambia (Wolof, Mandinka, Fulani, and other peoples), producing the religious composition Hall documents. The proportion of Senegambian arrivals substantially exceeded the proportion in most English colonies, producing the concentration of Bambara and other West African cultural traditions in Louisiana that would substantially shape Louisiana Voodoo formation and the Louisiana Creole cultural tradition. The Islamic presence among Senegambian arrivals was substantial, though the documented cases from the French colonial period are fewer than the documented cases from the later Anglo-American period.

The French Code Noir of 1685 (applied in Louisiana from 1724), documented in [Ingersoll 1995][book_ingersoll_1995] Mammon and Manon, established legal requirements for Catholic baptism and instruction of enslaved persons that produced the pattern of nominal Catholic identification of enslaved persons in French Louisiana. The requirement produced the Catholic-African syncretic religious formation that would evolve into Louisiana Voodoo under subsequent Spanish and American authorities. The [Long 2001][book_long_2001] Spiritual Merchants and [Fandrich 2005][book_fandrich_2005] The Mysterious Voodoo Queen treat the subsequent Voodoo formation.

## Indigenous-European Encounters and Diffusion

The pattern of indigenous-European encounter across the 1607-1725 period followed substantial regional variation but shared several common structural features. The [Richter 2001][book_richter_2001] Facing East from Indian Country provides the comprehensive treatment reversing the traditional westward-facing narrative to trace the encounter from the perspective of indigenous peoples receiving European contact. The [Vaughan 1965][book_vaughan_1965] New England Frontier provides the foundational modern treatment of the seventeenth-century Puritan-Native encounter. The [Shannon 2000][book_shannon_2000] Indians and Colonists at the Crossroads of Empire treats the Albany Congress of 1754 and the broader colonial diplomatic frameworks that would shape Revolutionary-era encounters. The [Van Zandt 2008][book_van_zandt_2008] Brothers Among Nations treats the diplomatic frameworks that emerged from bidirectional Native-European exchange across the mid-Atlantic and Southeast during the seventeenth century.

The initial pattern of cooperative trade and diplomatic relations, characteristic of many first-contact encounters, gave way across the seventeenth century to substantially more conflictual relations as English colonial populations grew and land pressure increased. The pattern shows substantial regional variation. New England Puritan-Native relations followed the pattern from cooperation to King Philip's War documented above. Chesapeake Anglo-Powhatan relations followed the pattern from initial cooperation through the 1622 uprising, 1644 Second Anglo-Powhatan War, and the eventual subordination. Middle Colony Quaker-Lenape relations under Penn produced the extended peace treated above. Southeastern English-Creek relations produced the Yamasee War of 1715-1717, treated in [Ramsey 2008][book_ramsey_2008] The Yamasee War, that substantially reshaped the Southeastern political geography.

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy, occupying the territory between the French to the north, English to the east, and other Native peoples to the west, maintained substantial diplomatic autonomy through the practice of playing European powers against each other. The [Richter 1992][book_richter_1992] The Ordeal of the Longhouse documents the Haudenosaunee diplomatic sophistication. The 1701 Grand Settlement at Montreal, in which the Haudenosaunee negotiated substantial peace with the French while maintaining their existing English alliances, marks the peak of Haudenosaunee diplomatic achievement. The Covenant Chain treaty tradition with New York provided the ongoing English alliance that would shape colonial and Revolutionary politics.

The pattern of cultural diffusion across the colonial encounter included substantial European adoption of Native agricultural techniques (maize, tobacco, potatoes, beans, squash, sunflowers cultivation), pharmacopeia (aspirin, quinine, digitalis, sassafras, ipecac), and place-based knowledge (trails, water sources, hunting patterns). The pattern also included substantial Native adoption of European technology (metal tools, firearms, horses, wheeled vehicles) and religious concepts (Christian eschatology, the literate Bible tradition, Catholic sacramental practice). The bidirectional diffusion produced the hybrid cultures that would shape both descendant Native and descendant Euro-American populations. The [Cronon 1983][book_cronon_1983], [Weatherford 1988][book_weatherford_1988], and [Calloway 1997][book_calloway_1997] treatments document the diffusion patterns.

The Iroquois-Constitution influence thesis addressed in [A266][related_post_a266_framing] specifies one particular case of the broader question of indigenous influence on American institutional formation. The documentary evidence for Founding-era knowledge of Iroquois political institutions, including Franklin correspondence and the 1744 Lancaster Treaty statements of Onondaga leader Canasatego encouraging colonial union, is substantially documented. The interpretive question of whether the documented knowledge substantially influenced constitutional design remains contested between the [Grinde and Johansen 1991][book_grinde_johansen_1991] and [Tooker 1988][research_tooker_1988] positions cited in [A266][related_post_a266_framing]. Article three treats the Founding-period debate in further detail.

## African Arrival and Religious Retention

The pattern of African arrival and religious retention during the 1607-1725 period established the initial foundations for the West and Central African substrate treated in [A266][related_post_a266_framing]. Approximately fifty thousand Africans were transported directly to North America across the period, with substantial subsequent internal trade producing the colonial African population of approximately one hundred thousand by 1725.

The religious composition of arriving Africans followed the source-region distribution documented in [A266][related_post_a266_framing]. The colonial-period documentation of African religious retention is substantially limited by the documentary practices of European colonizers who rarely recorded religious practices of enslaved persons except where those practices produced conflict with colonial authorities. The [Berlin 1998][book_berlin_1998] Many Thousands Gone, [Wood 1974][book_wood_1974] Black Majority, and [Chambers 2005][book_chambers_2005] Murder at Montpelier reconstruct the religious formation from the available evidence.

South Carolina low country African religious retention was substantial through the colonial period. The province rested on the [Carolina Charter 1663][ref_carolina_charter_1663] issued by Charles II to eight Lords Proprietors that established the province of Carolina and provided the initial proprietary framework prior to the 1719 transition to royal governance. The enslaved African population in the low country reached demographic dominance,

$$f_{\text{African, SC low country}}(1720) \approx 0.70$$

per [Wood 1974][book_wood_1974], producing the conditions for substantial religious retention. The Gullah-Geechee cultural tradition that persists to the present emerged from this colonial-period concentration of West and West Central African peoples with substantial autonomy from European colonial supervision. The [Brown 2012][book_brown_2012] African-Atlantic Cultures and the South Carolina Lowcountry documents the cultural and religious formations produced in this concentration. The [Wood 1975][research_wood_1975] earlier article on the South Carolina slave population growth provides the quantitative reconstruction. The 1739 Stono Rebellion, treated in [Wood 1974][book_wood_1974] and [Smith 2005][book_smith_2005] Stono, involved substantial Kongo Catholic enslaved persons in the context of Kongo's continuing wars and the Spanish Florida sanctuary policy that offered freedom to enslaved persons escaping to Spanish territory. The [Berlin 1980][research_berlin_1980] Time, Space, and the Evolution of Afro-American Society provides the foundational typology distinguishing the slave societies from societies with slaves across the colonial period.

The documented Muslim African presence in the colonial period includes the figures cited in [A266][related_post_a266_framing] plus additional documented cases treated in [Austin 1984][book_austin_1984] African Muslims in Antebellum America and [Diouf 1998][book_diouf_1998] Servants of Allah. Ayuba Suleiman Diallo (Job Ben Solomon), enslaved from Senegal to Maryland in 1730 and subsequently returned to Senegal in 1734, provides one documented colonial-period Muslim case. Additional documented cases from the colonial period are fewer than from the antebellum period treated in later articles.

The pattern of enslaved African Catholic conversion under French and Spanish colonial authorities differed substantially from the pattern under English Protestant authorities. French Louisiana and Spanish Florida both required Catholic baptism of enslaved persons under their legal codes, producing the nominal Catholic religious identification. English colonial authorities substantially resisted baptism of enslaved persons through the seventeenth century over concerns that baptism might imply legal freedom, until the 1729 opinion of the Attorney General clarifying that baptism did not affect enslaved status enabled subsequent Protestant missionary activity to enslaved populations. The pattern is treated in [Frey 1991][book_frey_1991] Water from the Rock and [Frey and Wood 1998][book_frey_wood_1998] Come Shouting to Zion.

The Anglican Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts (SPG), founded 1701, undertook substantial missionary activity to enslaved and free Africans in the colonies through the eighteenth century. The SPG catechism programs, particularly in New York (through Elias Neau's 1704 catechism school) and in South Carolina, produced substantial conversions while raising ongoing colonial-authority concerns about the implications of Christian identity for the enslaved status. The [Glasson 2012][book_glasson_2012] Mastering Christianity treats the SPG missionary activity.

The institutional foundation for later independent Black churches emerged from these colonial-period patterns of African religious formation, Anglican missionary activity, and the initial evangelical revivals of the 1720s and 1730s that would culminate in the First Great Awakening treated in article three.

## Colonial Higher Education, Print Culture, and Religious Formation

Colonial higher education institutions established from the seventeenth century forward provided the institutional infrastructure for the reproduction of ethnoreligious leadership across generations and the formation of the American colonial and later Revolutionary-era intellectual class. The colonial colleges combined religious commitment (typically training ministers) with broader liberal education. The [Morison 1935][book_morison_1935] The Founding of Harvard College, [Morison 1936][book_morison_1936] Three Centuries of Harvard, [Cremin 1970][book_cremin_1970] American Education The Colonial Experience, and [Marsden 1994][book_marsden_1994] The Soul of the American University provide the comprehensive treatments.

Harvard College (1636), founded by the Massachusetts Bay Colony to train Puritan ministers, provided the first American higher education institution. The 1642 curriculum included classical languages, logic, ethics, physics, and theology. The [Harvard Charter 1650][book_harvard_charter_1650] documents the founding institutional framework. William and Mary College (1693), founded by royal charter to train Anglican clergy for the Southern colonies, provided the second American college. Yale College (1701), founded by conservative Congregationalists concerned about Harvard's theological drift toward moderate Puritanism, provided the third. Successive colonial college foundings followed,

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Harvard} &: 1636 \\
\text{William and Mary} &: 1693 \\
\text{Yale} &: 1701 \\
\text{College of New Jersey (Princeton)} &: 1746 \\
\text{King's College (Columbia)} &: 1754 \\
\text{College of Philadelphia (Pennsylvania)} &: 1755 \\
\text{College of Rhode Island (Brown)} &: 1764 \\
\text{Queen's College (Rutgers)} &: 1766 \\
\text{Dartmouth College} &: 1769
\end{aligned}$$

with each college reflecting the denominational or colonial religious formation that founded it. The denominational associations continued through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries before subsequent secularization patterns treated in later articles.

Print culture in colonial North America developed substantially from the 1638 arrival of the Cambridge printing press at Massachusetts Bay through the eighteenth-century expansion of colonial printing shops, newspapers, almanacs, and religious publications. The [Hall 1996][book_hall_1996] Cultures of Print, [Amory and Hall 2000][book_amory_hall_2000] The Colonial Book in the Atlantic World, and [Warner 1990][book_warner_1990] The Letters of the Republic treat the print culture development. The first American book was the [Bay Psalm Book 1640][book_bay_psalm_book_1640], produced at Cambridge and providing the Puritan psalter for congregational singing. The [Eliot Indian Bible 1663][book_eliot_bible_1663] Mamusse Wunneetupanatamwe Up-Biblum God, the translation into Massachusett noted earlier, provided the second major American publication and the first American translation of the complete Bible.

Colonial religious publishing produced substantial sermon literature (particularly Puritan election-day and jeremiad sermons), theological treatises (Mather's Magnalia noted above, Jonathan Edwards's early works from the 1720s and later), captivity narratives (Mary Rowlandson's 1682 captivity account), spiritual autobiographies (numerous Quaker journals following Fox's example), and denominational periodicals. The literacy rates documented earlier in the Puritan New England section supported substantial reading among colonists, particularly for religious material.

Colonial newspapers began with the Boston News-Letter (1704) and expanded through the eighteenth century. The 1735 John Peter Zenger trial for seditious libel in New York, treated in [Copeland 2006][book_copeland_2006] The Idea of a Free Press, established the colonial precedent for freedom of the press in criticism of colonial officials that would substantially shape American press practice.

## Applying the Six-Axis Framework to the Colonial Substrates

The six-axis analytical framework introduced in [A266][related_post_a266_framing] (signal, objective, structure, model, interaction, adaptation) admits systematic application to the colonial-period substrates treated in this article. The application permits comparison across substrates and identification of the patterns that shape later development.

The signal axis characterizes the form of training information each substrate received. New England Puritan formation combined dense scriptural instruction (Bible-based sermons twice weekly, catechetical education, mandatory town schools) with the conversion-experience testimony required for church membership, producing the high-signal, community-supervised pattern. Cavalier Virginia Anglican formation depended substantially on sacramental participation and parish attendance without the conversion-experience requirement, producing a lower-signal pattern. Quaker Delaware Valley formation depended on silent worship and inspired vocal ministry, producing a highly-variable individual-signal pattern. African arrival substrate formation depended on oral transmission across generational cohorts under severe surveillance conditions, producing the low-visible-signal pattern that nevertheless preserved substantial ritual and cosmological content per the treatments cited above. Approximate signal-density rank ordering across colonial substrates gives

$$\sigma_{\text{Puritan}} > \sigma_{\text{Anglican}} \sim \sigma_{\text{Quaker}} > \sigma_{\text{African-visible}} \sim \sigma_{\text{Native-under-suppression}}$$

with the ordering reflecting the number and reliability of formal transmission channels rather than the substrates' actual internal content complexity.

The objective axis characterizes what each substrate sought to optimize. Puritan New England optimized for the covenanted-community glorification of God through the this-worldly pursuit of calling in the expectation of eschatological consummation. Chesapeake Anglican optimized for the hierarchical social order under the dual sacred-secular authority. Quaker optimized for the inward light and the peace-testimony pursuit of Christian pacifism. African enslaved substrate optimized for the survival, kinship maintenance, and spiritual autonomy under conditions of extreme constraint. Native substrate optimized for the ceremonial cycle maintenance and relational obligations across the human, non-human, and ancestral communities.

The structure axis characterizes the ecclesiastical and community-organization form each substrate adopted. Puritan Congregationalist structure combined local congregational autonomy with colony-wide consultation through synods. Anglican structure combined parish organization with colonial vestry autonomy under the formal Bishop of London jurisdiction that operated substantially through absentee delegation. Quaker structure combined local meetings for business with quarterly and yearly meetings providing the broader consensus. Baptist structure emerged from Rhode Island with congregational autonomy and voluntary associational cooperation. African substrate structure operated through hush harbor gatherings, ring shout ceremonies, and familial and lineage-based transmission where the European surveillance permitted. Approximate structural hierarchy depth across colonial substrates gives

$$D_{\text{Anglican}} \sim 3 > D_{\text{Puritan}} \sim 2 \sim D_{\text{Presbyterian}} \sim 2 > D_{\text{Congregational}} \sim 1 \sim D_{\text{Baptist}} \sim 1 \sim D_{\text{Quaker}} \sim 1$$

where $D$ counts the tiers of authority from local congregation to the substrate's apex governance body.

The model axis characterizes the internal representations each substrate maintained of divine and cosmic reality. Reformed traditions (Puritan, Presbyterian, Dutch Reformed, Huguenot) shared covenant theology and predestinarian soteriology. Anglican tradition combined the broader Reformed theological content with the sacramental and liturgical elements retained from pre-Reformation practice. Quaker tradition maintained the inward-light theology substantially independent of the historical confessional formulations. Baptist tradition combined Reformed doctrinal content with the believer's-baptism ecclesiology. Anabaptist traditions (Mennonite, Amish, Brethren, Hutterite) maintained the separationist ecclesiology and pacifist ethics. Jewish traditions maintained the halakhic legal orientation and the covenantal ethnic identity. African substrate maintained the high god plus intermediary spirit cosmology adaptable to Christian identification. Native substrate maintained the place-based cosmology and relational-obligation structure.

The interaction axis characterizes the relationship each substrate maintained with the external social and political environment. Puritan New England exhibited comprehensive institutional integration between religious and civil authority. Chesapeake Anglican exhibited formal integration with substantial practical autonomy for planter class. Quaker Delaware Valley exhibited religious-led governance under the Penn proprietorship. Baptist and other dissenter formations sought separation of religious and civil authority. African substrate operated under severe legal and physical constraint but maintained autonomous religious life where possible. Native substrate maintained autonomous religious life under increasing pressure from European religious activity across the period. Approximate church-state coupling coefficient across colonial substrates gives

$$w_{\text{Puritan-NE}} \approx w_{\text{Anglican-VA}} \approx 1, \quad w_{\text{Quaker-PA}} \sim 0.8, \quad w_{\text{Baptist-RI}} \sim 0, \quad w_{\text{African}} \sim -0.5, \quad w_{\text{Native}} \sim -0.5$$

with $w_{ij} \in [-1, 1]$ representing normalized coupling between substrate $i$ and civil authority $j$ with positive values indicating supportive establishment and negative values indicating adversarial or repressive coupling.

The adaptation axis characterizes each substrate's response to novel circumstances requiring departure from the inherited tradition. Puritan New England adapted through the Half-Way Covenant, the eventual toleration of dissenter minorities, and the gradual moderation toward the eighteenth-century religious life. Anglican Virginia adapted through the Great Awakening (treated in article three) and eventual disestablishment. Quaker adapted through the eighteenth-century antislavery reforms and the broader reform tradition. African substrate adapted through syncretic combination with Catholic and Protestant elements, ring shout preservation, and independent Black church formation from the late eighteenth century. Native substrate adapted through the Ghost Dance, the Native American Church, and the sovereignty movements treated in later articles. Half-Way Covenant adoption timeline provides a load-bearing example of adaptation response,

$$t_{\text{Half-Way Covenant, Boston synod}} = 1662 \text{ CE}, \quad \Delta t_{\text{from Great Migration end}} \approx 22 \text{ years}$$

with adaptation time constant $\tau_{\text{Puritan}} \sim 20$ years for the doctrinal accommodation between the founder-generation conversion-experience requirement and the second-generation membership crisis.

The systematic application demonstrates that the six-axis framework identifies substantial structural variation across the colonial substrates while permitting comparison across the streams. Later articles apply the framework to subsequent historical episodes.

## Terminological Note

This article follows the terminological conventions established in [A266][related_post_a266_framing]. Usage for the colonial period includes the following. Colonist refers to residents of the British and later American colonies through 1725 without prejudging their subsequent political allegiance. Native American, Indigenous, and Indian are used interchangeably following the primary-source usage of each period and the preferred naming conventions of contemporary tribal nations. African and African American are used interchangeably for the enslaved and free populations descended from West and Central African origins, with the recognition that colonial-era populations may not have adopted an American self-identification. Congregationalist, Presbyterian, Baptist, Anglican, Quaker, Catholic, and other denominational names refer to their seventeenth and early eighteenth-century meanings, with the note that present-day denominations descended from these formations may substantially differ. Folkway refers to the durable cultural pattern comprising language, kinship, religion, foodways, and other embodied practices as characterized in [Fischer 1989][book_fischer_1989] Albion's Seed. New England, Chesapeake, Delaware Valley, and backcountry refer to the four Fischer folkway regions, with the boundaries approximate and permeable.

## Load-Bearing Open Questions

- What is the correct empirical characterization of the religious composition of the colonial population at successive dates? The [Butler 1990][book_butler_1990] revisionist argument that the colonial population was substantially less religiously observant than the traditional historiography claimed remains contested.
- What is the correct assessment of the colonial Quaker peace policy? The extent to which the 1682-1737 peace with the Lenape reflected substantive Quaker religious commitment versus favorable demographic and geographic circumstances remains contested.
- What is the correct empirical characterization of enslaved African religious retention during the colonial period given the documentary limitations?
- What is the correct assessment of the relative causal weight of Fischer's four folkway framework versus alternative accounts of colonial regional differentiation?
- What is the correct empirical characterization of the pattern of Native depopulation during the colonial period and the relative contributions of disease, warfare, displacement, and enslavement?
- What is the correct assessment of the durability of Spanish and French colonial religious formations against the subsequent Anglo-American conquest?
- What is the correct assessment of the bidirectional versus unidirectional diffusion of cultural, religious, and technological elements across the colonial encounter?
