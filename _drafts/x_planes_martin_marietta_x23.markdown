The [Martin Marietta X-23A PRIME][ref_x23] was six feet eight inches long, weighed 894 pounds, and was thrown at the Pacific Ocean three times from the top of an Atlas. On the third attempt, on 19 April 1967, it flew 710 statute miles to one side of the path a stone would have taken, deployed a parachute, and was caught in mid-air by a transport aeroplane near Kwajalein. **It is the smallest vehicle in this series and it answered a larger question than most of the aircraft that dwarf it.** This article is the twenty-fourth in the [X-Planes series][related_post_a297_xplanes_framing], following the [X-1][related_post_a298_bell_x1], the [X-2][related_post_a299_bell_x2], the [X-3][related_post_a300_douglas_x3], the [X-4][related_post_a301_northrop_x4], the [X-5][related_post_a302_bell_x5], the [X-6][related_post_a303_convair_x6], the [X-7][related_post_a304_lockheed_x7], the [X-8][related_post_a305_aerojet_x8], the [X-9][related_post_a306_bell_x9], the [X-10][related_post_a307_north_american_x10], the [X-11][related_post_a308_convair_x11], the [X-12][related_post_a309_convair_x12], the [X-13][related_post_a310_ryan_x13], the [X-14][related_post_a311_bell_x14], the [X-15][related_post_a312_north_american_x15], the [X-16][related_post_a313_bell_x16], the [X-17][related_post_a314_lockheed_x17], the [X-18][related_post_a315_hiller_x18], the [X-19][related_post_a316_curtiss_wright_x19], the [X-20][related_post_a317_boeing_x20], the [X-21][related_post_a318_northrop_x21], and the [X-22][related_post_a319_bell_x22].

**There is also a difficulty with the number, and it is not a small one.** The designation X-23A is attached to this vehicle by encyclopaedias, by museums, and by the historical literature. Available United States Air Force nomenclature records show that it was never assigned, and further that it was never even requested. This article treats the vehicle and the paperwork as two separate subjects, because they are, and because the second one is evidence about how the designation system actually worked rather than a clerical footnote.

## The Research Question

**Can a vehicle returning from orbit choose where it lands?**

That is the question, and in 1964 it was genuinely open. A ballistic capsule cannot choose. It falls where the atmosphere and its own entry conditions put it, and the only control available is when to start. The landing point is therefore wherever the orbital ground track happens to be at the moment of the deorbit burn, and if that is the middle of an ocean then that is where the vehicle goes.

The alternative was known in principle. A body that generates lift as well as drag can hold itself up, glide, and by rolling that lift vector to one side, turn. **The open question was not whether this worked but how much of it a returning vehicle could actually buy**, because the lift available to a shape that must also survive entry heating is very small. A lifting body of the period had a hypersonic lift-to-drag ratio somewhere near one, against roughly seventeen for a transport aeroplane at cruise.

**The keystone of this article is the conversion of that small lift-to-drag ratio into lateral distance**, which the period literature calls lateral range and the modern literature calls crossrange, in [Jackson 1961][research_jackson_1961], [Baradell 1962][research_baradell_1962], [Wang 1963, APPROXIMATE SOLUTIONS OF THE LATE][research_wang_1963], [Wang and Skulsky 1963][research_wang_skulsky_1963], [Holloway and Pritchard 1967][research_holloway_pritchard_1967], [Moote 1970][research_moote_1970]. Every part of the vehicle was dimensioned against it. The shape exists to produce the lift, the ablator exists to let the vehicle stay in the atmosphere long enough to use it, the reaction controls and flaps exist to hold the bank angle that converts it, and the recovery system exists so that the measurement survives.

The [X-20 Dyna-Soar][related_post_a317_boeing_x20] had asked a version of this question and had been cancelled in December 1963 without flying. PRIME asked the narrowest possible form of it, without a pilot, for a small fraction of the money.

## Programme Origin

PRIME was the second half of a United States Air Force programme called START, for Spacecraft Technology and Advanced Reentry Tests, begun in 1964. The first half was [ASSET][ref_asset], which flew earlier and addressed structures and heating on a small unmanned glider. **PRIME addressed manoeuvring**, which ASSET had not.

The institutional context is the cancellation of Dyna-Soar. The Air Force had spent years and a great deal of money on a piloted, winged, manoeuvring re-entry vehicle and had been left with the question unanswered and the answer still wanted. START was the cheap route to the same information. Two small unmanned vehicles, launched on existing boosters, instrumented and recovered, could measure heating and manoeuvring without a pilot, without a runway, and without a programme large enough to be worth cancelling.

Martin Marietta supplied the airframe. The company had been developing a family of lifting-body shapes under the designation SV-5, and the family is the reason this article touches three vehicles rather than one. The SV-5D was the small unmanned entry vehicle that became PRIME. The SV-5P was the larger piloted low-speed vehicle that became the [X-24A][ref_x24]. The two are the same aerodynamic idea at different sizes and different ends of the speed range, which is exactly why the designation question became tangled.

## The Vehicle

The SV-5D was a blunted lifting body with a rounded delta planform, two upper fins, and flaps at the rear. It was 6 feet 8 inches long, 3 feet 10 inches across, 2 feet 10 inches high, and weighed 894 pounds at entry.

**Almost the whole of it was thermal protection.** The structure was titanium, beryllium, stainless steel and aluminium, and over it went an ablative heat shield between 0.75 and 2.75 inches thick, with a carbon phenolic nose cap. Attitude was held by nitrogen gas reaction jets and by aerodynamic flaps, which took over as the air became thick enough to be useful.

One quantity that the analysis below needs is not published. **The reference area of the SV-5D does not appear in the accessible record**, so it is derived here from the X-24A, which is the same shape at larger scale and whose reference area is published as 195 square feet against a length of 24.5 feet and a span of 13.64 feet. The ratio of area to the length-times-span box is

$$k_S = \frac{S_\text{X-24A}}{l\,b} = \frac{195}{24.5 \times 13.64} = 0.5835$$

and applying that ratio to the SV-5D's own length and span gives

$$S = k_S\, l\, b = 0.5835 \times 2.032 \times 1.168 = 1.385\ \text{m}^2$$

or 14.9 square feet. **This is an assumption and every number that depends on it inherits that status.** The crossrange result does not depend on it at all, which is stated here because it matters.

## Sizing From First Principles

### The Force Balance That Makes a Glide an Equilibrium

A vehicle at orbital speed needs no lift, because the centrifugal effect of travelling that fast round a curved Earth supports it entirely. At rest it needs lift equal to its full weight. In between, an equilibrium glide is one that holds the difference. Writing $\bar V = V/V_c$ for speed as a fraction of circular satellite speed, and $\phi$ for the angle through which the lift vector has been rolled away from vertical, the balance is

$$L\cos\phi = W\left(1 - \bar V^2\right)$$

The circular speed itself follows from equating gravitational and centripetal acceleration at the surface, giving

$$V_c = \sqrt{g R} = \sqrt{9.80665 \times 6.371\times 10^6} = 7{,}904\ \text{m/s}$$

which is 25,933 feet per second. The equilibrium glide is the reference trajectory of the whole subject, and its literature is [Foss and Whitcomb 1960][research_foss_whitcomb_1960], [Moe 1960][research_moe_1960], [Wang and Ting 1961][research_wang_ting_1961], [Hankey 1963][research_hankey_1963], [Kornreich 1963][research_kornreich_1963], [Stern and Chu 1963][research_stern_chu_1963], [Ruger 1964][research_ruger_1964], [Approximate analysis of atmosphere 1965][research_approximate_analysis_1965], [Bell 1965][research_bell_1965], [Hooks and Hankey 1965][research_hooks_hankey_1965], [V. ### Why the Turn Rate Does Not Depend on the Atmosphere

Two further statements complete the mechanics. Drag decelerates the vehicle along its path, and the sideways component of the rolled lift vector turns it,

$$m\frac{dV}{dt} = -D, \qquad m V \frac{d\psi}{dt} = L\sin\phi$$

where $\psi$ is the heading. Dividing the second by the first eliminates time, and substituting the force balance eliminates the weight, leaving

$$\frac{d\psi}{dV} = -\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{\sin\phi}{V}$$

**Every property of the vehicle except its lift-to-drag ratio has dropped out, and so has the atmosphere.** Mass, size, reference area and air density are all gone. That is the reason this analysis can be done at all for a vehicle whose reference area is not published. Integrating from the entry speed gives

$$\psi(V) = \frac{L}{D}\sin\phi \, \ln\frac{V_e}{V}$$

The heading change is logarithmic in the speed ratio, which means the great majority of the turning happens late, when the vehicle is slow. **None of this is original to this article.** The equilibrium glide and the relations that follow from it are standard material, set out in [Vinh's treatment of atmospheric flight trajectories][book_vinh_1981] and in [Regan and Anandakrishnan on re-entry dynamics][book_regan_anandakrishnan_1993]. What is done here is to carry them through to a requirement and then to invert them on a measurement. At a lift-to-drag ratio of 1.2 and a bank of 45 degrees, slowing from circular speed to a quarter of it turns the vehicle through

$$\psi = 1.2 \times \sin 45^\circ \times \ln 4 = 1.176\ \text{rad} = 67.4^\circ$$

### The Crossrange Integral

Crossrange accumulates at $\dot y = V\sin\psi$. The classical treatment replaces $\sin\psi$ by $\psi$ at this point, which is what makes a closed form available at all. **That step is examined below and it turns out to cost about a tenth of the answer**, but it is kept here because it is the step the period literature took and the closed form it produces is the relation the subject is built on. Substituting $u = \bar V^2$ and using $g = V_c^2/R$ turns the accumulated lateral distance into a single integral,

$$y = \frac{R}{4}\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)^2 \sin\phi\cos\phi \; I(u_e), \qquad I(u_e) = \int_0^{u_e} \frac{\ln(u_e/u)}{1-u}\,du$$

For entry at exactly circular speed the integral has a closed form. Expanding $1/(1-u)$ as a geometric series and integrating term by term gives $\sum_{n\ge 1} n^{-2}$, so

$$I(1) = \int_0^1 \frac{-\ln u}{1-u}\,du = \frac{\pi^2}{6} = 1.6449$$

### The Bank Angle Is Already Decided

The only free choice in the expression is $\phi$, and it appears as $\sin\phi\cos\phi$, which is $\tfrac{1}{2}\sin 2\phi$. That is maximal at

$$\phi_\text{opt} = 45^\circ, \qquad \sin\phi\cos\phi = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

**This does not depend on the lift-to-drag ratio, on the entry speed, or on anything about the vehicle.** Any vehicle wanting maximum crossrange banks 45 degrees, and the guidance system's apparently central decision turns out to have been made in advance by the arithmetic.

Putting the optimum and the closed form together gives the governing relation of the whole subject,

$$\frac{y_\text{max}}{R} = \frac{\pi^2}{48}\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)^2 = 0.2056\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)^2$$

**Crossrange goes as the square of the lift-to-drag ratio.** For a vehicle entering at circular speed this evaluates to 1,310 kilometres at a ratio of 1.0, 1,886 at 1.2, and 2,568 at 1.4. A ballistic capsule at zero gets nothing, and that is not a small number made small but a capability that is simply absent.

### What the Small-Angle Step Costs

The closed form above was checked against a numerical integration that keeps $\sin\psi$ exactly, and **the two disagree by more than a rounding error.**

The reason is visible in the heading solution. At a lift-to-drag ratio of 1.0 and a bank of 45 degrees, the accumulated heading change reaches 180 degrees before the glide ends, and at 1.2 it passes 220. **Beyond 180 degrees $\sin\psi$ does not merely differ from $\psi$, it has the opposite sign.** The approximation does not degrade gracefully. It inverts.

| Lift-to-drag ratio | Closed form | Exact | Over-prediction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.8 | 838 km | 797 km | 5.2 percent |
| 1.0 | 1,310 km | 1,214 km | 7.9 percent |
| 1.2 | 1,886 km | 1,696 km | 11.2 percent |
| 1.4 | 2,568 km | 2,233 km | 15.0 percent |

**The classical relation is optimistic, and increasingly so as the lift-to-drag ratio rises**, because a better gliding vehicle turns further and the approximation is worse the further it turns. Both numbers are given throughout the rest of this article.

There is a physical statement hiding in that table. **A vehicle holding 45 degrees of bank through a full lifting entry turns through half a circle.** It arrives pointing the way it came. Real entry guidance reverses the bank part way through for exactly this reason, and the constant-bank assumption that makes the mathematics tractable is not a trajectory anyone would fly.

### What the Requirement Actually Is

Now the other half. **How much crossrange does a returning vehicle need?**

Successive orbits do not retrace the same ground. The Earth turns underneath, so each pass lays its track to the west of the last by the rotation accumulated in one orbital period,

$$\Delta\lambda = \omega_E T = \frac{2\pi}{86{,}164} \times 5400 = 0.3937\ \text{rad} = 22.56^\circ$$

for a nominal ninety-minute low orbit. At the equator that is a ground distance of

$$d = R\,\Delta\lambda = 6.371\times 10^6 \times 0.3937 = 2{,}509\ \text{km}$$

An arbitrary landing site lies somewhere between two adjacent tracks, so it is at most half that distance from the nearer one. **And those two tracks are consecutive in time.** The requirement to reach a chosen site on one pass or the next is therefore

$$y_\text{req} = \tfrac{1}{2} R\,\Delta\lambda = 1{,}254\ \text{km}$$

### The Answer

Inverting the crossrange relation on that requirement gives the lift-to-drag ratio a returning vehicle must have,

$$\frac{L}{D} = \sqrt{\frac{y_\text{req}/R}{\pi^2/48}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.1968}{0.2056}} = 0.978$$

Solving the same requirement against the exact integration instead, which cannot be done in closed form and has to be bisected, gives

$$\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)_\text{exact} = 1.018$$

**The correction moves the answer closer to unity rather than away from it**, which is a fortunate accident and not an argument for either number. Both are reported because the first is what the period could compute and the second is what is true.

**A lift-to-drag ratio near unity is not a round number chosen for convenience. It is the threshold at which a spacecraft stops being a passenger of its orbit and starts being able to come home when it wants to.** Below it the vehicle waits for the ground track to come back, which for a general orbit means a day or more and may mean never. At it, the longest wait is one orbit.

The requirement falls at higher latitudes, because the tracks crowd together as the meridians converge. At 28.5 degrees the required ratio is 0.917, and at 45 degrees it is 0.823. **The hardest case is the equator**, and a vehicle sized for the equator is sized for everywhere.

## What the Flight Record Says About a Disputed Number

The sources do not agree on this vehicle's hypersonic lift-to-drag ratio. One gives 1.0 and another gives 1.2 for the SV-5 shape. **That is a 44 percent disagreement in the quantity this article is about**, and it cannot be waved away.

It can, however, be attacked, because two facts about the third flight are independently known and both are consequences of the same trajectory. The vehicle achieved 1,143 kilometres of crossrange, and it flew from Vandenberg Air Force Base to a recovery area near Kwajalein, a great-circle distance of

$$d = 2R\arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2} + \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\sin^2\frac{\Delta\lambda}{2}} = 7{,}787\ \text{km}$$

The equilibrium glide gives a downrange to go with the crossrange,

$$x = \frac{L}{D}\cos\phi \;\frac{R}{2}\ln\frac{1}{1-u_e}$$

**Two equations, two unknowns.** Writing $A$ and $B$ for the two geometric factors, the pair is $\;(L/D)\cos\phi = x/A\;$ and $\;(L/D)^2\sin\phi\cos\phi = y/B$, and dividing one by the square of the other gives the bank angle without needing the lift-to-drag ratio first,

$$\tan\phi = \frac{y/B}{(x/A)^2}, \qquad \frac{L}{D} = \frac{x/A}{\cos\phi}$$

**A first attempt at this check assumed a bank angle of 45 degrees and recovered 45.13, which looked like a confirmation and was nothing of the kind.** The assumed lift-to-drag ratio of 1.2 had been fed straight back in. The suspiciously clean agreement was the signal that the check was circular, and the fix was to stop assuming either quantity and solve for both.

The glide does not begin at the launch pad. The Atlas covers part of that great circle before entry interface, and how much is not published, so the honest treatment is to sweep it. The pair is solved twice, once with the closed form and once against the exact integration.

| Boost range before entry | Bank, closed form | Ratio, closed form | Bank, exact | Ratio, exact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 km | 35.1 degrees | 1.035 | 37.0 degrees | 1.060 |
| 500 km | 38.8 degrees | 1.016 | 41.0 degrees | 1.050 |
| 1,000 km | 42.8 degrees | 1.006 | 45.5 degrees | 1.052 |
| 1,500 km | 47.2 degrees | 1.006 | 50.3 degrees | 1.070 |
| 2,000 km | 51.9 degrees | 1.019 | 55.5 degrees | 1.111 |
| 2,500 km | 56.8 degrees | 1.049 | 60.9 degrees | 1.183 |

Each solve carries its own bank angle, because the two models do not agree on what bank produces a given crossrange and pairing one model's ratio with the other's angle would be meaningless.

**The bank angle swings through twenty-four degrees and the lift-to-drag ratio moves very little.** Across the whole plausible range of the unknown the exact solution stays between 1.05 and 1.18. The flight record determines one of the two unknowns much better than the other, which is a more useful outcome than a single number would have been.

**The result sits between the two disputed figures and is nearer the lower one.** Flying the exact model at a ratio of 1.0 produces 1,043 kilometres against the 1,143 demonstrated, an under-prediction of 8.8 percent. Flying it at 1.2 produces 1,453 kilometres, an over-prediction of 27 percent. **Neither published figure reproduces the flight and the truth is in between**, somewhere near 1.05 to 1.10.

That is worth stating plainly because it is not the answer this analysis set out to find. The closed form alone appeared to vindicate the 1.0 figure to within one percent, and correcting the approximation took that vindication away and replaced it with a value that neither source gives.

**It does not settle the conflict and this article does not claim that it does.** Several readings survive. If 1,143 kilometres was the vehicle's maximum capability, the solution above is a measurement and both published figures are somewhat wrong. If it was a demanded value below capability, the solution is a lower bound and the 1.2 figure survives comfortably while 1.0 does not. There is also a reading in which both sources are right about different things, with 1.2 the maximum trimmed ratio the shape can reach and 1.0 a nominal or rounded value, since nothing obliges a vehicle to fly at its best lift-to-drag ratio.

**What can be said without qualification is narrower and firmer.** The vehicle's effective lift-to-drag ratio through the manoeuvring phase of the third flight was close to one, it was not close to zero, and a ballistic vehicle on the same trajectory would have landed 1,143 kilometres from where this one did.

### Thermal Protection

The equilibrium glide fixes the density at every speed, because the force balance demands it. Rearranging,

$$\rho = \frac{2\beta_L(1-u)}{V^2\cos\phi}, \qquad \beta_L = \frac{W}{S\,C_L}$$

so the heating history is determined by the trajectory rather than chosen. With the derived reference area and an assumed hypersonic drag coefficient of unity, the lift parameter is 2,392 newtons per square metre and the ballistic coefficient is 2,870, which is 60 pounds per square foot. **That is a low ballistic coefficient, and low is what a research vehicle wants**, because it decelerates high up where the air is thin.

Stagnation-point heat flux follows the Sutton and Graves correlation,

$$\dot q_s = k\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{R_n}}\,V^3, \qquad k = 1.7415\times 10^{-4}$$

in SI units for air. Substituting the glide density makes the heat flux proportional to $u\sqrt{1-u}$, which is maximal at

$$u = \tfrac{2}{3}, \qquad V = 0.8165\,V_c = 6{,}454\ \text{m/s}$$

With an assumed nose radius of 0.1 metres the peak is

$$\dot q_{s,\text{max}} = 109\ \text{W/cm}^2$$

which is modest by the standards of a ballistic capsule and sustained for far longer. Integrating the flux over the glide, using $dt = -(L/D)\cos\phi\,dV/(g(1-u))$, gives a closed form,

$$Q = k\,\frac{L}{D}\sqrt{\cos\phi}\sqrt{\frac{2\beta_L}{R_n}}\frac{V_c^3}{g}\cdot\frac{\arcsin a - a\sqrt{1-a^2}}{2}$$

with $a = \sqrt{u_e}$, evaluating to 107 kilojoules per square centimetre. The same integral gives the duration,

$$t = \frac{L}{D}\cos\phi\,\frac{V_c}{g}\,\text{artanh}\,\sqrt{u_e} = 1{,}452\ \text{s}$$

**Twenty-four minutes.** That is the price of using the lift. A ballistic capsule is through the heating in two or three.

Now a cross-check that was not designed in. The heat shield thickness is published, and the heat load has just been computed, so the effective heat of ablation is determined,

$$H_\text{eff} = \frac{Q}{\rho_\text{abl}\,t_\text{abl}}$$

At the thick end of the published range, 2.75 inches at a representative charring-ablator density of 550 kilograms per cubic metre, this gives 27.8 megajoules per kilogram. **That is squarely a real material property for a charring ablator.** At the thin end of the range, 0.75 inches, it gives 102 megajoules per kilogram, which no ablator achieves. **The two published thicknesses therefore bracket the heating distribution rather than the uncertainty in it**, with the thick end at the stagnation region and the thin end on surfaces that never see anything like the stagnation load. The arithmetic identifies which end is which without being told.

### Deceleration, and Why the Glide Is Gentle

The same balance that sets the density sets the deceleration. In equilibrium glide it is

$$\frac{a}{g} = \frac{1-u}{(L/D)\cos\phi}$$

which is greatest at the end of the flight, where it evaluates to 1.18 g. The same entry flown ballistically, at the reported flight path angle of 6 degrees, gives the Allen and Eggers result

$$a_\text{max} = \frac{V_e^2\sin\gamma_e}{2eH} = 158\ \text{m/s}^2 = 16.2\ g$$

**A factor of nearly fourteen.** The lift is not only buying crossrange. It is buying a survivable deceleration, and for the piloted vehicles that this programme was clearing the way for, that was the more immediately valuable of the two.

## Comparison With Ground Prediction

The equilibrium-glide model in this article is what could be computed with a slide rule in 1964, and it is worth being explicit about how well it does.

| Quantity | Model | Flight |
|---|---|---|
| Crossrange at a lift-to-drag ratio of 1.0, closed form | 1,133 km | 1,143 km |
| Crossrange at a lift-to-drag ratio of 1.0, exact | 1,043 km | 1,143 km |
| Crossrange at a lift-to-drag ratio of 1.2, exact | 1,453 km | 1,143 km |
| Downrange at the solved bank angle | 7,787 km by construction | Vandenberg to Kwajalein |
| Peak deceleration | 1.18 g | not published in the accessible record |
| Glide duration | 24.2 min | consistent with the reported profile |

**The closed form is good to one percent at the lower of the two disputed lift-to-drag ratios, and that agreement is an accident of two errors cancelling.** The small-angle step over-predicts by eight percent and the assumed ratio is low by about the same, so the product comes out right for the wrong reason. Removing the approximation removes the coincidence and leaves the flight sitting between the two published figures.

**That is the most instructive thing in this article about method rather than about the vehicle.** A model that agrees with a measurement to one percent is not thereby correct, and the way to find out is to improve the model and see whether the agreement survives. Here it did not.

The comparison is weaker than it looks in one respect that must be said. PRIME entered on a depressed suborbital trajectory at a flight path angle near 6 degrees, which is considerably steeper than the shallow entry the equilibrium glide assumes. The model describes the mission being simulated more faithfully than the trajectory actually flown.

## What the Data Changed

PRIME fed the X-24A directly. The same shape, at larger scale and with a pilot, was flown at Edwards from 1969, and the aerodynamic data from the unmanned entries reduced the risk of the piloted programme in the regime where no piloted vehicle had been.

**The larger consequence is the Space Shuttle's crossrange requirement, and there is an arithmetic connection worth showing.** The Shuttle was required to achieve 1,100 nautical miles of crossrange, a figure that came from the Air Force and was driven by a once-around polar mission returning to its launch site. The launch site moves with the Earth, so in one orbit it displaces by the full track spacing at its own latitude rather than half of it,

$$d_\text{VAFB} = R\,\Delta\lambda\cos\varphi = 6.371\times 10^6 \times 0.3937 \times \cos 34.74^\circ = 2{,}061\ \text{km}$$

against a stated requirement of 1,100 nautical miles, which is 2,037 kilometres. **The two agree to 1.2 percent, and nothing here was fitted to anything.** The requirement that shaped the Space Shuttle's wing, and through it a great deal of the vehicle, is the distance Vandenberg travels while a spacecraft goes round once.

PRIME demonstrated 56 percent of that figure with a vehicle weighing 894 pounds.

The programme is also a data point about unmanned research vehicles, which the designation section below turns out to bear on directly.

## A Designation That Was Never Requested

The vehicle in the museum is labelled X-23A. The encyclopaedias call it the X-23A. **The nomenclature records, as reported by the [standard reference on United States military aircraft designations][ref_designation_missing], show that the designation was never assigned, and further that it was never requested.**

The sequence, which is stranger than a simple omission, runs as follows.

On **16 November 1965** a request was submitted for the designation X-23A. It was submitted for the **SV-5P**, the piloted low-speed vehicle, and the vehicle description accompanying the request clearly described a manned lifting-body aircraft flying from Mach 2 to landing.

On **15 December 1965** the request was disapproved, **on the ground that the subject aircraft was unmanned**. At that date the designation system applied to manned aircraft only, so the reason was a valid one in general. It was simply not true of this aircraft, and the paperwork accompanying the request said so.

In **late 1966** the offices responsible reconsidered the whole question. A proposal to create an entirely new designation category for gliding re-entry vehicles was raised and abandoned. The decision instead was to request X-23A for the unmanned SV-5D and X-24A for the piloted SV-5P. **X-24A was requested and approved. No request for X-23A appears ever to have been sent.**

The reason is not recorded. The plausible reading, and it is offered as a reading rather than a finding, is that having been told a year earlier that unmanned aircraft need no designation, nobody saw the point of asking.

**Three things about this are worth separating.**

**First, the designation is in universal use and is not official.** This article uses it, as its title does, because refusing to would make the vehicle harder to find rather than more accurately described.

**Second, the disapproval was wrong on its facts.** The 1965 refusal turned on the vehicle being unmanned, and the vehicle in that request was the piloted one. The manned vehicle was refused a number for being unmanned, and a year later received a different number for being manned.

**Third, and most useful to this series, the system briefly considered growing a new limb and did not.** A separate designation category for gliding re-entry vehicles was proposed and dropped. **The X-series absorbed the problem instead, informally and without paperwork**, which is a small piece of evidence about how the designation system actually accommodated things it was not built for. The series' closing article argues that the designation system is not a counter. This is one of the cases that argument rests on, and it should be read alongside the reserved-but-never-allocated X-39 and the refused X-52.

**This article states the conflict and does not resolve it.** The records say the number was never assigned. The world says the vehicle is the X-23A. Both statements are true about different things, and collapsing them would destroy the evidence.

## Where the Framing Breaks Down

**Treating PRIME through crossrange alone gets the programme wrong in at least three ways.**

**The vehicle was not only a manoeuvring experiment.** It carried a heat shield the programme wanted measured, and the ASSET half of START had already been devoted to exactly that. Heating and manoeuvring were both objectives and the article's keystone selects one.

**The equilibrium glide is not what it flew.** The model assumes a shallow entry from orbit. PRIME was lobbed on a depressed trajectory at a much steeper flight path angle, and it reached the crossrange it did under conditions the model describes only approximately. That the numbers agree to one percent at the lower disputed lift-to-drag ratio is encouraging and is not proof.

**The reference area is derived, not published.** Everything in the thermal section inherits that. The crossrange result does not, which is why it is the keystone and the heating is not.

There is also a fourth, about the article's own framing. **A vehicle whose recovery system failed twice in three flights is a programme about recovery at least as much as about entry**, and this article gives that two paragraphs.

## The Source Base

The research literature cited here was assembled from the NASA Technical Reports Server, from Defense Technical Information Center records reached through their registered identifiers, and from the Crossref index, giving a pool of 1,838 records from which this article draws.

**The subject has an unusually clean period record.** Roughly two fifths of the pool predates 1976, which is a higher proportion than most articles in this series have found, and it reflects that lifting entry was worked out in a concentrated burst between about 1958 and 1972 and then largely left alone until the current revival.

**It also has an unusually dirty name space, and the harvest was designed around that rather than corrected afterwards.** The programme acronyms are unusable as search terms. PRIME collides with prime numbers and prime movers, and START with the Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty and with the ordinary English verb, so no query in this harvest contains either word. Beyond the acronyms, footprint belongs to carbon accounting, recovery to economics and to waste heat, ablation to medicine and to laser materials processing, and entry to almost everything. Designing the queries to be unambiguous without the programme names meant the contamination largely never entered, and the filters that would have caught it found only a few dozen records to reject.

**One family got through anyway and it was found by reading rather than by anticipating.** Lateral motion of a vehicle is a term of art in railway dynamics and in road-vehicle lane keeping, and two such records had settled among the foundational crossrange papers, which is the least visible place for them to be. Maneuvering range turns out to name an instrumented air combat facility off the East Coast, so a pattern for it retrieves a construction quality assurance plan. Lateral range is also a term in search and detection theory. Glider, maneuvering and hovering all belong to underwater vehicles as well as to aircraft.

## Epistemic State

**Historical fact, as reported by the accessible record.** The SV-5D PRIME was built by Martin Marietta for the United States Air Force as the second half of the START programme. Three vehicles were launched on Atlas boosters from Vandenberg on 21 December 1966, 5 March 1967 and 19 April 1967. The first two were lost to recovery-system failures and the third was recovered in mid-air by a JC-130B. The demanded crossranges were zero, 1,053 kilometres and 1,143 kilometres. The vehicle was 6 feet 8 inches long and weighed 894 pounds. The surviving vehicle is at the National Museum of the United States Air Force.

**Historical fact, on the designation.** The nomenclature record shows a request for X-23A submitted on 16 November 1965 for the SV-5P, disapproved on 15 December 1965 on the stated ground that the aircraft was unmanned, and a late-1966 decision to seek X-23A for the SV-5D and X-24A for the SV-5P, of which only the second was ever submitted. **This article reports that record and does not adjudicate between it and universal usage.**

**Engineering analysis, performed here and checkable.** The equilibrium-glide crossrange relation, its closed form, the optimal bank angle of 45 degrees, the required crossrange of half the ground-track spacing, and the resulting lift-to-drag requirement of 0.978 by the closed form and 1.018 by exact integration, are all derived in this article from stated assumptions. The simultaneous solution of downrange and crossrange for the bank angle and lift-to-drag ratio is likewise derived here. The heating figures follow from the Sutton and Graves correlation applied to the equilibrium-glide density.

**Inference, and flagged as such.** That the vehicle's effective lift-to-drag ratio through the third flight's manoeuvring phase was between 1.05 and 1.18, and therefore between the two published figures rather than equal to either, is an inference from the demonstrated crossrange and downrange under the equilibrium-glide model. It holds only if the demonstrated crossrange was near the vehicle's maximum, and it is sensitive to the unpublished boost range in a way the article shows rather than hides. That the two published heat-shield thicknesses correspond to the stagnation region and to lightly heated surfaces respectively is an inference from the implied heat of ablation. That nobody submitted the X-23A request because the 1965 refusal had said unmanned vehicles need none is a reading offered by the designation reference and is not documented.

**Assumptions that everything downstream inherits.** The reference area of 1.385 square metres is scaled from the X-24A and is not published for this vehicle. The nose radius of 0.1 metres, the hypersonic drag coefficient of unity, the ablator density of 550 kilograms per cubic metre, and the parachute drag coefficient of 0.75 are all assumed. The entry speed is quoted differently by different sources and both ends of the spread are carried through the analysis.

**What the record does not settle.** The vehicle's actual maximum lift-to-drag ratio. Its actual reference area. The downrange consumed by the Atlas before entry interface, which is why the bank angle could only be bracketed and not determined. The peak deceleration and heating actually measured, which were certainly telemetered and are not in the accessible literature. And the reason the X-23A request was never sent.

## Out of Scope

The ASSET half of the START programme, which deserves its own treatment. The Atlas SLV-3 as a launch vehicle. The X-24A and X-24B, which follow in this series. The mid-air retrieval technique and its origins in satellite film return. The detailed chemistry of charring ablators. The Space Shuttle crossrange requirement's full political history, of which only the arithmetic is treated here.

## Conclusion

**PRIME asked whether a spacecraft could steer on the way home, and the answer was yes, by about a thousand kilometres.**

The number matters more than it looks. A returning vehicle needs roughly half a ground-track spacing of crossrange to reach a chosen site without waiting, and at the equator that requires a lift-to-drag ratio of 0.978 by the relation the period used and 1.018 when the approximation in that relation is removed. **A lifting body near unity is therefore not a modest capability but the threshold one**, and the difference between it and a ballistic capsule is not a matter of degree. One can come home when it chooses and the other cannot.

The vehicle that demonstrated this weighed less than a motorcycle, flew three times, was lost twice on the way back, and carries a designation that was never requested for it. **All four of those facts are worth keeping.**
