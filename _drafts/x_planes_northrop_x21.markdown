The [Northrop X-21A][ref_x21] is the only aircraft in this series that worked and was cancelled for it. Two [Douglas WB-66D][ref_b66] bombers were given new wings cut with thousands of fine spanwise slots, and pumps inside the wing drew a thin sheet of air through those slots to hold the boundary layer laminar. **It did what it was built to do.** By the end of the programme the aeroplane was flying with laminar flow over more than ninety-five percent of the area intended for it, which no aircraft of that size had ever achieved. Then the programme stopped. This article is the twenty-second in the [X-Planes series][related_post_a297_xplanes_framing], following the [X-1][related_post_a298_bell_x1], the [X-2][related_post_a299_bell_x2], the [X-3][related_post_a300_douglas_x3], the [X-4][related_post_a301_northrop_x4], the [X-5][related_post_a302_bell_x5], the [X-6][related_post_a303_convair_x6], the [X-7][related_post_a304_lockheed_x7], the [X-8][related_post_a305_aerojet_x8], the [X-9][related_post_a306_bell_x9], the [X-10][related_post_a307_north_american_x10], the [X-11][related_post_a308_convair_x11], the [X-12][related_post_a309_convair_x12], the [X-13][related_post_a310_ryan_x13], the [X-14][related_post_a311_bell_x14], the [X-15][related_post_a312_north_american_x15], the [X-16][related_post_a313_bell_x16], the [X-17][related_post_a314_lockheed_x17], the [X-18][related_post_a315_hiller_x18], the [X-19][related_post_a316_curtiss_wright_x19], and the [X-20][related_post_a317_boeing_x20].

**The received account is that laminar flow control failed on maintenance.** The slots clogged with insects, the aeroplane needed constant attention, and the cost of keeping it clean outweighed the fuel it saved. That account is repeated almost everywhere. It has one problem, which this article is largely about. **The service-maintenance evaluation was never performed.** It was one of the programme's stated objectives, the programme had not yet been able to address it when the money ran out, and the advisory group that reviewed the programme in October 1965 recommended a major wing modification before any meaningful maintenance data could be gathered. The modification was never made.

So the X-21 is remembered for answering a question it never asked. The standard inventory entry is [Jenkins Landis and Miller 2003 American X-Vehicles, An Inventory X-1 to X-50][book_jenkins_landis_miller_2003] and the vehicle compilation is [Miller 2001 The X-Planes, X-1 to X-45][book_miller_2001]. The indispensable source here is [Braslow 1999 A History of Suction-Type Laminar-Flow Control with Emphasis on Flight Research][book_braslow_1999], written by the engineer from the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, or NASA, who served as technical consultant to the Air Force on this programme, and the contemporary engineering account is [Kosin 1965][research_kosin_1965] in the Journal of Aircraft. Braslow reproduces the 1965 review group's report in full as a document appended to the monograph, the Northrop analysis of the cloud problem survives as [Hall 1964][ref_hall_1964], the description of the wing written while it was flying is [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963] in Air Force Magazine, and the technical results of the Northrop final report are summarised, with their numbers, in [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998].

## The Research Question

About half the fuel a subsonic transport burns in cruise goes to overcoming skin friction, and on an aircraft of that size the boundary layer is turbulent over nearly all of it. A laminar boundary layer exerts roughly a tenth of the shear. **The prize is therefore enormous and has been obvious since the 1930s**, and the whole history of the subject is a history of trying to collect it, in [Freeman 1935][research_freeman_1935], [Quinn 1944][research_quinn_1944], [Von Doenhoff 1944][research_von_doenhoff_1944], [Quinn 1947, Tests of the NACA 64 SUB 1 A212 A][research_quinn_1947_2], [Quinn 1947, Wind-tunnel Investigation of the][research_quinn_1947], [Pasamanick and Proterra 1948][research_pasamanick_proterra_1948], [Passamanick 1948][research_passamanick_1948], [Racisz and Quinn 1948][research_racisz_quinn_1948], [Cliett 1952][research_cliett_1952], [Rebuffet and Poisson-Quinton 1952][research_rebuffet_poisson_quinton_1952], [Torda 1952][research_torda_1952], [Northrop Aircraft Inc Hawthorne Ca 1956][research_northrop_aircraft_inc_hawthorne_ca_1956], [Raspet 1957][research_raspet_1957], [Northrop Corp Hawthorne Ca Norair Div 1964, Summary of Laminar Boundary Layer][research_northrop_corp_hawthorne_ca_norair_div_1964], [Northrop Corp Hawthorne Ca Norair Div 1964, Summary of Laminar Boundary Layer][research_northrop_corp_hawthorne_ca_norair_div_1964_2], [Kosin 1965][research_kosin_1965], [MacDermott et al 1965][research_macdermott_1965], [MacDermott et al 1966][research_macdermott_1966], [Cordner 1967][research_cordner_1967], [Anderson et al 1969][research_anderson_1969], [Khusnutdinova 1972][research_khusnutdinova_1972], [Wu 1973, Magnetohydrodynamic boundary laye][research_wu_1973], [Chuang 1974][research_chuang_1974].

**The X-21 was not built to discover whether this works.** It was known to work. It was built to find out three practical things. Whether a slotted suction wing can be manufactured to the required tolerance at acceptable cost. Whether laminar flow can be held on a large swept wing in real air. And whether such a wing can be operated and maintained by a squadron rather than by a laboratory.

It answered the first two. It never got to the third.

## Programme Origin

Werner Pfenninger brought continuous-suction laminar flow work to the Norair division of Northrop after the Second World War, and through the 1950s that group ran an extended series of Air Force contracts on slot arrangements, crossflow stabilisation and wing design. The wind tunnel and glove experiments that preceded the aeroplane are in [McCullough and Gault 1948][research_mccullough_gault_1948], [Sellars 1955][research_sellars_1955], [Yuan 1955][research_yuan_1955], [Yuan and Finkelstein 1956][research_yuan_finkelstein_1956], [Dannenberg et al 1957][research_dannenberg_1957], [Koh and Hartnett 1961][research_koh_hartnett_1961], [Adcock et al 1966][research_adcock_1966], [Dershin et al 1966][research_dershin_1966], [Dershin et al 1967, Direct measurement of compressibl][research_dershin_1967], [Dershin et al 1967, Direct measurement of skin fricti][research_dershin_1967_2], [Pfenninger et al 1967][research_pfenninger_1967], [Elkouh 1971][research_elkouh_1971], [Mills 1971][research_mills_1971], [Howard et al 1975][research_howard_1975], [Quaile and Levy 1975][research_quaile_levy_1975].

**The direct ancestor is a glove on a fighter.** In the middle 1950s Northrop flew a slotted suction section built into one wing of a Lockheed F-94, and the flights showed laminar flow over the whole of the suction section, in [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963]. [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998] records that the F-94A glove carried 69 slots, that it held fully laminar flow in level flight, climbs, turns and descents, and that it lost laminar flow in cloud and regained it within 30 seconds of leaving the cloud, which is the X-21's cloud result seen a decade early on a smaller wing.

**The programme was approved in stages.** The review group's report, reproduced in [Braslow 1999][book_braslow_1999], dates the X-21A Laminar Flow Control Demonstration Program from August 1960, following a report of the Aerospace Vehicles Panel of the Scientific Advisory Board on boundary layer control dated 25 June 1959, and lists the reviews that followed it. The Vehicles Panel reexamined the programme in November 1961 and November 1962, an advisory group of the Aeronautical Systems Division reviewed it on 22 and 23 June 1964, and the Air Force held a programme review on 13 January 1965. Braslow adds that the Wright Air Development Division chose the WB-66D for minimum cost, a high degree of safety and a short development time, and that the Federal Aviation Administration contributed money. [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963] gives the sums as 35 million dollars from the Air Force and 2 million from the Federal Aviation Agency.

The Air Force wanted the result for range. An aircraft with the drag of an X-21 wing could loiter far from base or cross an ocean without refuelling, and the 1963 case for the programme was put in exactly those terms in [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963], promising a lift-to-drag ratio raised from about twenty to more than thirty and an airframe more than forty percent smaller for the same mission. The example given was one model of the B-52, reduced from 400,000 pounds to 225,000 pounds without changing its speed, range, endurance or load, which is a reduction of

$$1 - \frac{225{,}000}{400{,}000} = 0.44$$

or 44 percent. **Those numbers are for an aircraft designed around laminar flow from the start.** They are not what a converted bomber can show, and the difference between the two turns out to matter more than it looks.

## The Aircraft and Its Suction System

The X-21A was a WB-66D with the engines moved and the wing replaced. Two General Electric J79 turbojets, which the cutaway drawing in [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963] labels YJ79-GE-13, went to pods on the rear fuselage, which cleared the wing of everything that would have disturbed the flow over it. The new wing spans 93.5 feet with 1,250 square feet of area and 30 degrees of quarter-chord sweep, reduced from the 35 degrees of the bomber. It was scaled from a [Douglas DC-8][ref_dc8] wing at about forty percent, so that the result would be representative of a large subsonic transport rather than of a research shape.

$$\bar{c} = \frac{S}{b} = \frac{1{,}250}{93.5} = 13.37\ \text{ft}$$

$$A\!R = \frac{b^{2}}{S} = \frac{93.5^{2}}{1{,}250} = 6.99$$

The suction surface is the part worth describing carefully, because it is routinely described wrongly. The wing skin carries spanwise slots running from root to tip, **between 0.0025 and 0.008 inches wide**, which is to say comparable with the cutting edge of a razor blade. Beneath each slot a plenum chamber is milled into an inner skin, and from each plenum a large number of small holes is drilled down into deep ducts formed between layers of bonded sandwich structure. Air is drawn through the slots into the plenums, through the holes into the ducts, and along the ducts at speeds reaching about two hundred miles per hour to four small gas turbines, two in each underwing nacelle, one serving the forward wing and one the rear, as [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963] describes the installation. The skin panels, usually about five feet by twenty, were set in the final assembly jig first and the spars and understructure added behind them, so that the wing was built from the outside in and the outer contour did not follow the structure's tolerances. Butz reports the programme's estimate that such a wing would cost only about twenty percent more to produce than a conventional wing of the same size and weight.

Two numbers make the surface concrete. With leading-edge slots about 0.0035 inches wide on a pitch of 0.75 inches, the values [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998] gives for the slots that controlled the flow at the attachment line, the fraction of the skin that is actually open is

$$\sigma = \frac{w}{p} = \frac{0.0035}{0.75} = 0.00467$$

**The suction surface is 99.5 percent solid.** And since the flow through the skin averages 0.145 feet per second, continuity gives the speed in the slots themselves as

$$v_{\text{slot}} = \frac{v_{w}}{\sigma} = \frac{0.145}{0.00467} = 31\ \text{ft/s}$$

or about 21 miles per hour, against the 200 miles per hour reported in the ducts below. The slot is not a hole the air rushes through. It is a very slightly porous skin.

**The figure of eight hundred thousand, which appears in almost every account as a count of slots, is a count of those metering holes.** [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963] states it plainly, describing air gathered in small chambers and channelled through pin-size holes, more than 800,000 of them, into the larger ducts. No primary document available here gives a count of the slots themselves, but their spacing bounds it. Joslin gives typical slot spacings of 1.1, 2.0 and 1.2 inches over 1 to 5, 5 to 40 and 40 to 100 percent of the chord. At the mean chord of 160.4 inches each surface therefore carries about

$$N_{\text{slot}} = \sum_{i} \frac{\Delta (x/c)_{i} \, \bar{c}}{s_{i}} = \frac{0.04 \times 160.4}{1.1} + \frac{0.35 \times 160.4}{2.0} + \frac{0.60 \times 160.4}{1.2} = 5.8 + 28.1 + 80.2 = 114$$

slots, where $\Delta (x/c)_{i}$ is the chordwise extent of each region and $s_{i}$ its slot spacing, so the wing has of the order of a hundred slots on each surface and a little over two hundred in all. The estimate ignores the closer spacing on the attachment line and treats the mean chord as typical. The distinction is not pedantry. A slot is a continuous span-long opening and a hole is a discrete metering orifice, and confusing the two makes the wing sound like a sieve when it is in fact a small number of very fine, very long, very accurately cut openings. The design and construction of such surfaces is treated in [Wainwright 1962][research_wainwright_1962], [Maddalon and Braslow 1990][research_maddalon_braslow_1990], [Mansy and Faruque 2023][research_mansy_faruque_2023], [Prasannakumar et al 2024][research_prasannakumar_2024].

## Sizing From First Principles

The natural question is whether the suction power exceeds the drag it saves. **At the suction coefficient assumed here it does not, and it is not close**, although the local coefficients the programme reports narrow the margin considerably, as the end of this section shows. The calculation is worth doing carefully because the answer removes the obvious explanation for the programme's fate and forces the search elsewhere.

Take a nominal cruise at Mach 0.75 and 40,000 feet. At that altitude the density is $5.851 \times 10^{-4}$ slugs per cubic foot and the speed of sound is 968.1 feet per second, so the flight speed and dynamic pressure are

$$V = M a = 0.75 \times 968.1 = 726.0\ \text{ft/s}$$

$$q = \tfrac{1}{2} \rho V^{2} = 154.2\ \text{lbf/ft}^{2}$$

and at the 83,000 pound gross weight the wing is working at

$$C_{L} = \frac{W}{q S} = \frac{83{,}000}{154.2 \times 1{,}250} = 0.431$$

The mean-chord Reynolds number is

$$Re_{\bar{c}} = \frac{V \bar{c}}{\nu} = \frac{726.0 \times 13.37}{5.075 \times 10^{-4}} = 1.913 \times 10^{7}$$

At that Reynolds number the mean skin-friction coefficients on a smooth flat plate are

$$C_{f,\text{turb}} = \frac{0.455}{(\log_{10} Re)^{2.58}} = 0.002713$$

$$C_{f,\text{lam}} = \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{Re}} = 0.000304$$

The ratio of the two is the whole prize,

$$\frac{C_{f,\text{lam}}}{C_{f,\text{turb}}} = \frac{0.000304}{0.002713} = 0.112$$

so the laminar boundary layer exerts **11.2 percent** of the turbulent shear and the saving available is

$$\Delta C_{f} = 0.002713 - 0.000304 = 0.002409$$

On the area the programme guaranteed to laminarise, which is 70 percent of a wing whose exposed wetted area is about 2,222 square feet, that coefficient is worth

$$\Delta D = q \, \Delta C_{f} \, S_{\text{lam}} = 154.2 \times 0.002409 \times 1{,}555 = 578\ \text{lbf}$$

### What the Suction Costs

The suction system ingests a mass flow set by the suction coefficient $C_Q$, the ratio of the velocity through the surface to the freestream velocity,

$$v_{w} = C_{Q} V_{\infty} = 2 \times 10^{-4} \times 726.0 = 0.145\ \text{ft/s}$$

**That is the whole of it.** The air is drawn through the skin at about a tenth of a mile per hour, and everything the wing achieves follows from removing it. The mass flow is

$$\dot{m} = \rho_{\infty} V_{\infty} C_{Q} S_{\text{wet}}$$

That air arrives in the ducts at a total pressure well below freestream total pressure, because it has passed through a slot narrower than a razor edge and along a duct. To expel it without a momentum penalty the system must restore it, and the ideal work per unit mass is the isentropic compression work. Writing $\Pi$ for the ratio of freestream total pressure to duct total pressure and $\kappa = (\gamma-1)/\gamma$, the shaft power is

$$P = \frac{\dot{m} c_{p} T_{t}}{\eta} \left[ \Pi^{\kappa} - 1 \right]$$

The equivalent drag is that power divided by flight speed, and dividing again by $q S_{\text{wet}}$ gives a drag coefficient on the same basis as the skin friction,

$$C_{D,\text{suction}} = \frac{2 C_{Q} c_{p} T_{t} \left[ \Pi^{\kappa} - 1 \right]}{\eta V^{2}}$$

**Density has cancelled.** That is worth pausing on. Substituting $c_p T = a^{2}/(\gamma-1)$ and $V = M a$ removes the atmosphere entirely and leaves

$$C_{D,\text{suction}} = \frac{2 C_{Q} \left[ \Pi^{\kappa} - 1 \right]}{\eta (\gamma-1) M^{2}} \left( 1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2} \right)$$

**The suction penalty is a function of Mach number and of the machinery, and of nothing else.** It does not depend on altitude at all. An aircraft climbing at fixed Mach number carries exactly the same suction drag coefficient at 20,000 feet as at 50,000, which is not obvious in advance and which removes an entire axis from the design trade.

With a design suction coefficient of $C_Q = 2 \times 10^{-4}$, a duct total-pressure recovery of 0.60 and a combined turbine and compressor efficiency of 0.75,

$$C_{D,\text{suction}} = 4.144 \times 10^{-4}$$

which is **17.2 percent** of the friction saving. Five sixths of the prize survives the pumping.

### How Far From Break-Even

Setting the suction penalty equal to the friction saving gives the suction coefficient at which the system stops paying,

$$C_{Q,\text{crit}} = \frac{\Delta C_{f} \, \eta (\gamma-1) M^{2}}{2 \left[ \Pi^{\kappa} - 1 \right] \left( 1 + \tfrac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2} \right)} = 1.163 \times 10^{-3}$$

**That is 5.8 times the design value.** In absolute terms, at the nominal condition and over the wing area the programme guaranteed to laminarise, the suction system moves 4.25 pounds of air per second and absorbs about 131 horsepower, while the drag it removes is 578 pounds of force, worth 763 horsepower at flight speed. Written as a ratio of powers,

$$\frac{\Delta D \, V}{P} = \frac{578 \times 726.0}{131 \times 550} = 5.8$$

which is the same 5.8 as the margin in suction coefficient, and necessarily so, since both sides of the comparison are linear in $C_Q$.

The margin is large enough that the interesting question is not whether it is positive but how it varies. It improves with Mach number, because the suction penalty falls as $1/M^{2}$ while the friction saving is nearly constant.

| Mach | $C_{D,\text{suction}}$ | $\Delta C_{f}$ | penalty as a fraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.40 | 0.001351 | 0.002578 | 0.524 |
| 0.50 | 0.000880 | 0.002518 | 0.350 |
| 0.60 | 0.000624 | 0.002469 | 0.253 |
| 0.70 | 0.000469 | 0.002428 | 0.193 |
| 0.75 | 0.000414 | 0.002409 | 0.172 |
| 0.80 | 0.000369 | 0.002392 | 0.154 |
| 0.85 | 0.000332 | 0.002376 | 0.140 |

**Laminar flow control is a high-speed technology**, and the reason is thermodynamic rather than aerodynamic. The work needed to recompress a unit of captured air is set by temperature, and the kinetic energy available to pay for it goes as the square of speed. That is why the application was always aimed at fast subsonic transports and why a slow aircraft is a poor candidate however clean its wing.

### What the X-21 Actually Drew

The suction coefficient of $2 \times 10^{-4}$ used above is an assumption, and the record of the programme allows it to be tested. **The local suction coefficients the X-21 design called for were higher.** [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998], summarising the Northrop final report, gives typical values of $v_{w}/V_{\infty}$ from $5 \times 10^{-4}$ in regions of nearly flat pressure to $10 \times 10^{-4}$ near the leading edge, and adds that at Mach 0.74 and 41,400 feet the measured suction distribution matched the predicted one in shape but exceeded it in level by 50 percent. Since the break-even coefficient above is fixed by the friction saving and the machinery, the margin at any other coefficient is simply

$$\frac{C_{Q,\text{crit}}}{C_{Q}} = \frac{1.163 \times 10^{-3}}{5 \times 10^{-4}} = 2.3 \qquad \frac{1.163 \times 10^{-3}}{10 \times 10^{-4}} = 1.16$$

Because the penalty is linear in $C_{Q}$, its share of the friction saving rises from 0.172 to $0.172 \times 2.5 = 0.43$ and $0.172 \times 5 = 0.86$. So where the suction was strongest the system was close to paying nothing back, and over the flat-pressure regions that make up most of the chord it returned a little more than twice what it cost.

The same summary reports the system's flow at one condition, 43,000 feet and Mach 0.75, as ranging from 1.94 to 7.18 pounds per second. At 43,000 feet, 3,000 feet into the isothermal layer whose scale height is $H = R T/g = 20{,}805$ feet, the density is

$$\rho_{43} = \rho_{40} \, e^{-3{,}000/H} = 5.851 \times 10^{-4} \times e^{-0.1442} = 5.065 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{slugs/ft}^{3}$$

and if those flows are totals over the whole wetted wing they imply an area-averaged coefficient of

$$\bar{C}_{Q} = \frac{\dot{m}}{\rho_{43} V_{\infty} S_{\text{wet}}} = \frac{7.18/32.174}{5.065 \times 10^{-4} \times 726.0 \times 2{,}222} = 2.7 \times 10^{-4}$$

at the upper figure and $0.74 \times 10^{-4}$ at the lower. **The summary does not say whether those flows are system totals or the flows of individual pumps or regions**, and the two readings differ by a factor of several. Read as totals, they bracket the assumed coefficient and leave a margin of about four at the upper figure. Read against the local design values, the margin is between one and two. The record therefore supports the sign of the conclusion, that suction cost less than it saved, and does not support the factor of six as a measured property of this aeroplane.

## Why the Testbed Could Not Show What Was Being Claimed

The trade above is per unit of laminarised area. The aircraft-level benefit depends on how much of the aeroplane is laminarised, and here the X-21 was structurally limited by being a conversion.

The wing is about 44 percent of the aircraft wetted area. The programme guaranteed laminar flow over 70 percent of the wing, which is 31 percent of the aeroplane.

The zero-lift drag has to be built from its parts rather than assumed, because the friction saving must be credited on the same basis the drag is charged. Taking a wetted-area ratio of 4.0 and a form factor of 1.20, which accounts for the thickness of a real wing over a flat plate,

$$C_{D_{0}} = k \, C_{f,\text{turb}} \frac{S_{\text{wet}}}{S} + C_{D,\text{other}} = 1.20 \times 0.002713 \times 4.0 + 0.0050 = 0.01802$$

where the residual 0.0050 is interference, excrescence and everything that is not skin friction. Drag build-up of this kind, and the span-efficiency factor the induced term needs, are period subjects with a settled literature, in [Report No. 349. With an Oswald efficiency of 0.80 the induced drag at the cruise lift coefficient is

$$C_{D_{i}} = \frac{C_{L}^{2}}{\pi A\!R\, e} = \frac{0.431^{2}}{\pi \times 6.99 \times 0.80} = 0.01055$$

$$C_{D} = C_{D_{0}} + C_{D_{i}} = 0.02857 \qquad \frac{L}{D} = \frac{C_{L}}{C_{D}} = 15.07$$

**That decomposition also settles the claim the article opened with.** The friction share of total drag is

$$\frac{k \, C_{f,\text{turb}} S_{\text{wet}} / S}{C_{D}} = \frac{0.01302}{0.02857} = 0.456$$

The assertion that about half a transport's fuel goes to overcoming skin friction came from the literature. This number came from geometry and a flat-plate correlation. **They agree to about nine percent, from two directions that share nothing**, which is worth more than either statement alone.

Now the saving. Over the laminarised wetted area the friction falls by $k \Delta C_{f}$ and the suction charges $C_{D,\text{suction}}$, which is a power and carries no form factor, so

$$\Delta D = \left( k \, \Delta C_{f} - C_{D,\text{suction}} \right) q \, S_{\text{lam}}$$

$$\frac{\Delta D}{D} = 0.108 \qquad \frac{L}{D} = 16.89$$

By the Breguet relation,

$$R = \frac{V}{c_{t}} \frac{L}{D} \ln \frac{W_{i}}{W_{f}}$$

range is proportional to lift-to-drag ratio at fixed speed, specific fuel consumption and fuel fraction, so **the aeroplane as flown was worth about twelve percent in range**. The relation itself is textbook material rather than journal material, treated in [Anderson 1999 Aircraft Performance and Design][book_anderson_1999], and the cruise-optimisation literature around it is narrow, being [Hale 1976][research_hale_1976], [Guynn 2011][research_guynn_2011]. **That literature is thin under this name**, because the word range in an aeronautical archive mostly means the distance to a target or a ballistic test facility rather than the distance an aircraft can cruise.

### What the 1963 Claim Actually Requires

**The case for laminar flow control was a doubling of range and a lift-to-drag ratio above thirty.**

Take the last row of that table seriously for a moment and laminarise the entire aeroplane, fuselage included, which is impossible but bounds the argument. The zero-lift drag becomes

$$C_{D_{0}}^{\ \text{lam}} = 1.20 \times 0.000304 \times 4.0 + 0.0050 = 0.00646$$

and adding induced drag and the suction charge over the whole wetted area,

$$C_{D}^{\ \text{best}} = 0.00646 + 0.01055 + 4.0 \times 4.144 \times 10^{-4} = 0.01866 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{L}{D} = 23.1$$

**Twenty-three, not thirty, with every square foot of the aeroplane laminar.** The reason is visible in the arithmetic. A lift-to-drag ratio of thirty at this lift coefficient allows a total drag coefficient of

$$C_{D} = \frac{C_{L}}{30} = \frac{0.431}{30} = 0.01435$$

and the induced drag alone is 0.01055, which is **73 percent of the entire budget**. Induced drag is set by the planform and is untouched by anything done to the boundary layer. Inverting for the aspect ratio that would leave room,

$$A\!R = \frac{C_{L}^{2}}{\pi e \left( C_{D} - C_{D_{0}}^{\ \text{lam}} - C_{D,\text{suction}} S_{\text{wet}}/S \right)} = 11.8$$

against the X-21's 6.99.

**So laminar flow control does not merely clean a wing. It moves the optimum planform.** Once friction is removed, induced drag dominates, and the aircraft that collects the full benefit wants a much longer, thinner wing than the one the X-21 inherited from a bomber. That is a second and independent reason the testbed could not demonstrate the case, and it is a stronger one than the wetted-area argument, because no amount of suction on this planform can reach the number. Nothing in that table reaches it, and the last row already assumes the impossible. The gap is not an error in the case. It is the difference between laminarising an existing aeroplane and designing one around laminar flow, where the wing shrinks, the fuel load shrinks, the structure lightens and the whole spiral runs inward. The transport design studies that worked this through are in [Ralph H. Jansen et al][research_ralph_h_jansen], [Ralph Jansen et al][research_ralph_jansen], [Orloff and Ciffone 1974][research_orloff_ciffone_1974], [Marino et al 1975][research_marino_1975], [Povinelli et al 1976][research_povinelli_1976], [Bonner et al 1977][research_bonner_1977], [Gratzer and George-Falvy 1978][research_gratzer_george_falvy_1978], [Klineberg 1978][research_klineberg_1978], [Sturgeon 1978][research_sturgeon_1978], [Aircraft energy efficiency program 1979][research_aircraft_energy_1979], [Klineberg 1979][research_klineberg_1979], [Swift and Mungur 1979][research_swift_mungur_1979], [Wright 1979][research_wright_1979], [Evaluation of laminar flow 1980][research_evaluation_of_1980], [Sturgeon 1980][research_sturgeon_1980], [Wagner et al 1984][research_wagner_1984], [Middleton et al 1985][research_middleton_1985], [Wagner 1986, Laminar flow control for transpor][research_wagner_1986_2], [Lange 1988][research_lange_1988], [Parikh and Nagel 1990][research_parikh_nagel_1990], [Arcara et al 1991][research_arcara_1991], [Fischer and Vemuru 1991][research_fischer_vemuru_1991].

**The programme knew this in 1963.** [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963] lists among the features of a future laminar flow control aircraft a wing of at least fifty percent more area than a conventional aircraft of the same gross weight, because once friction drag is removed from the sizing the span can be increased to cut the drag due to lift without an undue structural penalty. That is the planform argument above, stated by the programme while the X-21 was flying, and it confirms that the thirty was always a figure for a different wing.

**So the X-21 was an instrument that could not measure the quantity being argued about.** It could show that the flow stayed laminar, and it did. It could not show the aircraft-level benefit, because the aircraft-level benefit belonged to a different aeroplane.

## The Attachment Line, Which Is What Actually Went Wrong

On a swept wing the flow divides along a line near the leading edge, and the boundary layer along that line is fed spanwise from the root outward. If it becomes turbulent anywhere, the turbulence can propagate along the line and contaminate everything outboard of it. **No amount of suction downstream helps, because the flow arrives already turbulent.** This is spanwise contamination, and the literature on it is [Cooke 1962][research_cooke_1962], [Cumpsty and Head 1967, The Calculation of Three-Dimensio][research_cumpsty_head_1967], [Cumpsty and Head 1969][research_cumpsty_head_1969], [Cebeci 1974][research_cebeci_1974], [Poll 1979][research_poll_1979], [Cumpsty and Head 1980][research_cumpsty_head_1980], [Hall et al 1984][research_hall_1984], [Hall and Mackerrell 1988][research_hall_mackerrell_1988], [Holmes 1991][research_holmes_1991], [Naval K Agatwal et al 1992][research_naval_k_agatwal_1992], [Joslin 1996, Simulation of Nonlinear Instabili][research_joslin_1996_2], [Joslin 1996, Simulation of Three-Dimensional S][research_joslin_1996], [Smith 1996, Instability and Transition of Flo][research_smith_1996], [Poll 2007][research_poll_2007], [Reed and Saric 2013][research_reed_saric_2013], [Yatskih et al 2016][research_yatskih_2016], [Manuilovich 2017, Flow along an attachment line con][research_manuilovich_2017], [Manuilovich 2017, Stabilization of flow over an att][research_manuilovich_2017_2], [Liu 2019, Structures of Skin Friction, Surf][research_liu_2019], [Fedorov and Egorov 2021][research_fedorov_egorov_2021], [Xi et al 2021][research_xi_2021], [Xi et al 2025][research_xi_2025].

The governing parameter is an attachment-line Reynolds number formed from the spanwise velocity and the boundary-layer scale at the line. It is worth assembling rather than quoting, because each part is a separate physical statement. The flow along the line moves at the spanwise component,

$$W_{e} = V_{\infty} \sin\Lambda = 726.0 \times \sin 33.2^{\circ} = 397.5\ \text{ft/s}$$

The boundary layer there does not grow, because it is continuously stretched by the chordwise acceleration away from the line, which for a leading edge of radius $r$ is

$$\left. \frac{d U_{e}}{ds} \right|_{\text{al}} = \frac{2 V_{\infty} \cos\Lambda}{r} = 7{,}385\ \text{s}^{-1}$$

That balance fixes a constant thickness scale,

$$\ell = \sqrt{\frac{\nu}{d U_{e}/ds}} = 2.62 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{ft} = 0.0031\ \text{in}$$

**Three thousandths of an inch, which is the width of a slot.** The Reynolds number on that scale is

$$\bar{R} = \frac{W_{e} \ell}{\nu} = \sin\Lambda \sqrt{\frac{V_{\infty} r}{2 \nu \cos\Lambda}}$$

The sweep in that expression is the **leading-edge** sweep, not the quarter-chord sweep quoted for a planform. With a taper ratio of 0.30 the X-21 wing carries

$$\tan\Lambda_{LE} = \tan\Lambda_{c/4} + \frac{1-\lambda}{A\!R(1+\lambda)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Lambda_{LE} = 33.2^{\circ}$$

Below about 100 an introduced disturbance decays. Above about 245 it is sustained and propagates. Between the two, whether the attachment line stays laminar depends on the size of the disturbance introduced. Evaluating along the span, with leading-edge radius taken as 0.008 of the local chord,

| span station | chord, ft | $\bar{R}$ |
|---|---|---|
| root | 20.57 | 205 |
| 0.3 | 16.25 | 183 |
| 0.5 | 13.37 | 166 |
| 0.7 | 10.49 | 147 |
| tip | 6.17 | 113 |

**The X-21 sat in the ambiguous band along its entire span, and highest at the root.** That is the worst place to be. It is not the safe regime, where disturbances die whatever the wing does, and it is not the clearly hopeless regime, where nothing can be laminar. It is the regime in which the answer depends on how large a disturbance the wing root, the fuselage junction or an accumulation of insects happens to present, which is precisely the behaviour the programme reported and precisely the behaviour that is hardest to design against.

The result is sensitive to the leading-edge radius, which is assumed here rather than measured. At a radius of 0.012 chord the root value rises to 252 and crosses the sustaining threshold outright. **The X-21's wing root therefore sits on the boundary of attachment-line contamination, and which side of it depends on a geometric detail of about one percent of chord.** The most visible external feature of the aeroplane, a large fairing over the forward wing-root region, exists to tailor the pressure field exactly there.

**The X-21's own design rule says the same thing in different variables.** The analysis behind the wing required the momentum-thickness Reynolds number on the attachment line to stay below 100, according to [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998]. The two parameters are related through the spanwise velocity profile of the swept attachment-line flow, the swept Hiemenz solution, whose spanwise momentum thickness in units of $\ell$ is

$$\frac{\theta}{\ell} = \int_{0}^{\infty} g(\eta) \left[ 1 - g(\eta) \right] d\eta = 0.404 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad R_{\theta} = 0.404 \, \bar{R}$$

where $g = w/W_{e}$ is the spanwise velocity ratio, obtained by integrating $f''' + f f'' + 1 - f'^{2} = 0$ and $g'' + f g' = 0$ numerically, and $\eta = y/\ell$. The design limit is therefore

$$\bar{R} < \frac{100}{0.404} = 247$$

which is the upper threshold of 245 quoted above to within the precision of either. The experimental basis of that threshold, from Cumpsty and Head in 1969 to [Poll 1979][research_poll_1979], is reviewed by Joslin. At the root the table gives $R_{\theta} = 0.404 \times 205 = 83$. **The wing met its own criterion along the whole span**, so the contamination it suffered was not a failure of the criterion. It came from large disturbances, the turbulent fuselage boundary layer above all, and for large disturbances the relevant threshold is the lower one near 100, which the whole span exceeded.

The cure was accordingly to remove the disturbance rather than to change the wing. The review group of October 1965, in its report reproduced in [Braslow 1999][book_braslow_1999], concluded that apart from some uncertainty about contamination by turbulence from the fuselage the flights had verified the method of aerodynamic design, that the cause of the leading-edge trouble had been believed found by the middle of 1964, and that two means of controlling contamination from the fuselage had been developed and proven in flight, a special fence and a suction leading-edge portion containing vertical slots. Joslin lists a fence, vertical slots and a gutter at the wing-fuselage junction.

The related instability, in which the spanwise pressure gradient on a swept wing drives a crossflow profile with an inflection point, is the reason sweep is hostile to laminar flow in the first place, and its treatment is [Krahn 1955][research_krahn_1955], [Mikolowsky and McMahon 1973][research_mikolowsky_mcmahon_1973], [Dagenhart et al 1989][research_dagenhart_1989], [Mousseux et al 1989][research_mousseux_1989], [Reed 1989][research_reed_1989].

## Surface Tolerance

A slotted suction wing must be smoother than a conventional one, because roughness and waviness trigger transition that suction downstream cannot undo. The tolerance literature is [Effect of uniformly distributed roughness on turbulent skin-friction drag at supersonic speeds 1959][research_effect_of_1959], [Van Driest and Blumer 1961][research_van_driest_blumer_1961], [Lyons et al 1962][research_lyons_1962], [Nagamatsu et al 1964][research_nagamatsu_1964], [Young 1965][research_young_1965], [Thompson 1970][research_thompson_1970], [Merkle et al 1974][research_merkle_1974], [Kubota and Berg 1977][research_kubota_berg_1977], [Boudreau 1978][research_boudreau_1978], [Finson et al 1980][research_finson_1980], [Leventhal and Reshotko 1981][research_leventhal_reshotko_1981], [Kendall 1982][research_kendall_1982], [Coleman et al 1989][research_coleman_1989], [Bertolotti 1993][research_bertolotti_1993], [Masad 1996][research_masad_1996], [Saric 1997][research_saric_1997], [Stewart and Simpson 2005][research_stewart_simpson_2005], [Ceccio et al 2007][research_ceccio_2007], [Saric 2008][research_saric_2008], [Zhong 2009][research_zhong_2009], [Saric 2010][research_saric_2010], [Borodulin et al 2015][research_borodulin_2015], [Crouch et al 2015][research_crouch_2015], [Malik et al 2015][research_malik_2015], [Ustinov and Kachanov 2021][research_ustinov_kachanov_2021], [Borodulin et al 2022, Roughness Induced Transition Dela][research_borodulin_2022], [Xu and Wu 2022][research_xu_wu_2022], [Zoppini et al 2022, Transition Due to Isolated Roughn][research_zoppini_2022], [Zoppini et al 2022, Transition due to isolated roughn][research_zoppini_2022_2], [Nakagawa et al 2023][research_nakagawa_2023], [Zheng et al 2026][research_zheng_2026].

**The tolerance is computable and it is brutal.** A three-dimensional roughness element trips a laminar boundary layer when the Reynolds number formed on its own height and the velocity at that height passes a critical value of about 600. Near the wall the Blasius profile is linear, $u/U_{\infty} = 0.332\,\eta$, so

$$Re_{k} = \frac{u_{k} k}{\nu} = \frac{0.332\, k^{2} V_{\infty}^{3/2}}{\nu^{3/2} x^{1/2}} = 600$$

Solving for the admissible height at a station one foot behind the leading edge,

$$k_{\text{crit}} = \left( \frac{600 \, \nu^{3/2} x^{1/2}}{0.332 \, V_{\infty}^{3/2}} \right)^{1/2} = 0.0123\ \text{in}$$

**About a hundredth of an inch, and it scales as $x^{1/4}$, so it is smaller further forward**, which is exactly where the wing splices and the fairing putty were.

**This is where the programme spent its first two years.** The wings were built to close tolerances and the tolerances were still not close enough. The problem was solved, but it consumed the schedule. [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998], drawing on Fowell and Antonatos, gives the mechanism. The wings were built from many panels, the splices between them were filled with epoxy to meet the step and waviness tolerances at the high unit Reynolds number, and the epoxy cracked and chipped under the wing loading and the temperature changes of flight. The cause was traced to the bonding process, which was changed until the fills held.

Five thousand feet into the isothermal layer the density is $\rho_{40} e^{-5{,}000/H} = 4.601 \times 10^{-4}$ slugs per cubic foot, the viscosity is unchanged, and the kinematic viscosity rises in proportion to $6.454 \times 10^{-4}$ square feet per second. Since the admissible height above goes as $\nu^{3/4} V^{-3/4}$,

$$k_{\text{crit},45} = 0.0123 \left( \frac{6.454}{5.075} \right)^{3/4} \left( \frac{726.0}{774.5} \right)^{3/4} = 0.0140\ \text{in}$$

one foot behind the leading edge at $V = 0.8 \times 968.1 = 774.5$ feet per second. **The allowed steps bracket the three-dimensional criterion**, rearward steps tighter than it and forward steps looser. A step is a two-dimensional disturbance with its own criterion, so the agreement is one of scale rather than an identity, but it shows the arithmetic and the hardware were working on the same thousandths of an inch. The review group of October 1965 found that a previously suggested criterion for single surface waves had been confirmed in flight, and that vibration of isolated panels at a discrete frequency did not disturb the laminar flow. Joslin adds that the wing tolerated a gap 0.125 inches wide and 0.18 inches deep at 60 percent chord on the lower outboard wing.

## Atmospheric Particles

Flying in or near cirrus, the X-21 lost laminar flow. On emerging from the cloud it regained it immediately. The Northrop study of the problem, [Hall 1964][ref_hall_1964], opens with the observation that numerous flights had shown total loss of laminar flow control within or near visible cloud, and erratic performance in light haze when the humidity was relatively high. The mechanism is that ice crystals passing through the boundary layer generate turbulent wedges, and the effect is not a contamination of the surface but a continuous seeding of the flow.

**Both halves of that observation follow from one calculation.** A point disturbance in a laminar boundary layer does not simply trip the flow at that point. It produces a turbulent spot that grows as it convects, spreading laterally at a roughly constant angle, and the mechanism and its measurement are [Schubauer and Klebanoff 1955][research_schubauer_klebanoff_1955], [Schubauer and Klebanoff 1956][research_schubauer_klebanoff_1956], [Sokolov et al 1980][research_sokolov_1980], [Glezer et al 1986][research_glezer_1986], [Henningson et al 1987][research_henningson_1987], [Henningson et al 1988][research_henningson_1988], [Seifert et al 1992][research_seifert_1992], [Singer and Joslin 1995][research_singer_joslin_1995], [Simon and Boyle 1998][research_simon_boyle_1998], [Zhang 2000][research_zhang_2000], [LaGraff 2001][research_lagraff_2001], [Blackwelder et al 2007][research_blackwelder_2007], [Clark et al 2007][research_clark_2007], [Fehrs and Kaiser 2025][research_fehrs_kaiser_2025]. The spreading half-angle is about ten degrees, so a particle entering at the leading edge contaminates

$$A_{w} = \bar{c}^{\,2} \tan\alpha = 13.37^{2} \times \tan 10^{\circ} = 31.5\ \text{ft}^{2}$$

which means that

$$n = \frac{S}{A_{w}} = \frac{1{,}250}{31.5} \approx 40$$

wedges are enough to blanket the wing. The laminar boundary layer is thin,

$$\delta = \frac{5 \bar{c}}{\sqrt{Re}} = 0.0153\ \text{ft} = 0.18\ \text{in}$$

so the rate at which particles enter it across the span is

$$\dot{N} = N V_{\infty} b \, \delta$$

At the cirrus number density of ten thousand crystals per cubic metre assumed here, which is low for cirrus as shown below, that is about 294,000 encounters per second. **The wing is blanketed in roughly 140 microseconds.** On leaving the cloud the wedges simply convect away in

$$t = \frac{\bar{c}}{V_{\infty}} = \frac{13.37}{726.0} = 18\ \text{ms}$$

**So the loss is instantaneous with five orders of magnitude to spare, and the recovery is instantaneous because nothing has been left behind on the surface.** That is the difference between an ice crystal and an insect, and it is why the two problems needed entirely different answers.

**Hall's analysis supplies what the assumed density lacks.** Working at Mach 0.75 and at 25,000 and 40,000 feet, the same nominal condition used throughout this article, he took cirrus crystals to be hexagonal columns with a length 2.5 times their diameter and found a critical size at 25,000 feet of 17 by 42 micrometres, below which a crystal is too small to start a turbulent spot. Joslin gives the critical diameters as 17 and 32 micrometres at the two altitudes. Hall's typical cirrus condition is columns 100 micrometres long and 40 wide at an ice content of 0.01 grams per cubic metre. Treating each column as a cylinder of ice of density $\rho_{\text{ice}} = 917$ kilograms per cubic metre, the number density is

$$N = \frac{\text{IWC}}{\rho_{\text{ice}} \, \tfrac{\pi}{4} d^{2} L} = \frac{0.01\ \text{g/m}^{3}}{917{,}000\ \text{g/m}^{3} \times \tfrac{\pi}{4} \times (40 \times 10^{-6})^{2} \times 100 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^{3}} = 8.7 \times 10^{4}\ \text{m}^{-3}$$

where IWC is the ice water content, nearly nine times the density assumed above, and every one of those crystals is well above the critical size. The conclusion that the wing is blanketed at once is therefore conservative.

**The same model shows why haze mattered.** Hall defined the threshold of erratic operation as a time-averaged turbulent area of ten percent of the wing. If wedges arrive at random at rate $\dot{N}$, each covering $A_{w}$ for the convection time $t$, the time-averaged turbulent fraction is

$$\phi = 1 - \exp \left( - \frac{\dot{N} A_{w} t}{S} \right)$$

and setting $\phi = 0.10$ gives

$$\dot{N}_{10} = \frac{-\ln 0.9 \times 1{,}250}{31.5 \times 0.0184} = 227\ \text{s}^{-1} \qquad N_{10} = \frac{\dot{N}_{10}}{V_{\infty} b \, \delta} = \frac{227}{29.4\ \text{m}^{3}/\text{s}} = 7.7\ \text{m}^{-3}$$

with the volume swept by the boundary layer, $V_{\infty} b \delta = 726.0 \times 93.5 \times 0.0153 = 1{,}039$ cubic feet per second, converted to cubic metres. **About eight crystals of critical size in each cubic metre are enough to make laminar flow erratic on this crude model**, four orders of magnitude below a typical cirrus, which is why air that looks clear can still carry enough of them. Hall reports exactly that, with total loss in cloud where the visibility was several thousand feet and erratic, partially degraded performance in haze through which flight test engineers and pilots could see 30 to 60 miles horizontally.

**The recovery observed in flight was slower than the convection time.** The review group of October 1965 recorded that laminarity lost in cloud was reestablished a few seconds after return to clear air, against the 18 milliseconds computed above. Since the wing itself clears in milliseconds, the seconds are most plausibly the time taken to leave the diffuse edge of a cloud and the haze around it, which is an inference rather than a finding of the programme. The F-94A glove a decade earlier regained laminar flow within 30 seconds of leaving cloud.

**The claim that the data did not exist is checkable, and it holds up.** Hall wrote in 1964 that quantitative data from sampling of ice particles in natural clouds was practically nil, and took his typical crystal sizes from three references that disagreed in magnitude. Measuring ice-crystal concentration in cloud is a subject of its own, in [Mossop and Ono 1969][research_mossop_ono_1969], [Mossop and Hallett 1974][research_mossop_hallett_1974], [Davis and Fischer 1983][research_davis_fischer_1983], [Baumgardner et al 2014][research_baumgardner_2014], [Huang 2021][research_huang_2021], and what that literature shows is a handful of campaigns rather than the climatology an operator would need. The quantity the X-21 required was not a concentration in one cloud on one day. It was a distribution over altitude, season and route, and **that is a different and much larger measurement programme than anyone had run**.

**The honest statement about this literature is that it is very small.** Work in the archives and journals connecting atmospheric ice particles to laminar flow is essentially [Davis 1982][research_davis_1982], [Davis and Fischer 1983][research_davis_fischer_1983], [Davis et al 1986][research_davis_1986], and the reason is the one the programme itself gave. There was no statistical description of particle size and concentration as a function of altitude, season and geography, so the operational significance could not be computed. **A theory was produced, in [Hall 1964][ref_hall_1964], and the data to apply it did not exist.** Hall's own recommendation was that further work be predominantly experimental, by controlled laboratory tests and by flight tests carrying instruments to sample the ice particles. [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998] records that the cloud encounters of NASA's JetStar leading-edge flight tests in the 1980s, which did carry a particle spectrometer, agreed with Hall's criteria.

## Dependent Systems

The suction pumps are not free of the airframe. Four turbines and their ducting occupy volume inside a wing that must also carry fuel and structure, and the ducts are large because a small duct has its own boundary-layer losses, which is a pumping penalty on the pumping system.

**The duct size is a check on the suction coefficient from outside the model.** The mass flow over the guaranteed area is 0.132 slugs per second, the contemporary description reports duct speeds reaching 200 miles per hour, and the duct runs at roughly the recovered density, so

$$A_{\text{duct}} = \frac{\dot{m}}{\rho_{d} V_{d}} = \frac{0.132}{3.51 \times 10^{-4} \times 293} = 1.28\ \text{ft}^{2}$$

which is 185 square inches in total, or about 46 square inches in each of four ducts, near enough seven inches square. **That fits inside a wing of 13.4 foot chord without difficulty**, and it is arrived at from a reported duct velocity and a computed mass flow that share no input. The pumping installation and its ducting are treated in [Briggs 1952][research_briggs_1952], [Maddalon and Braslow 1990][research_maddalon_braslow_1990], [High Reynolds Number Hybrid 1999, High Reynolds Number Hybrid Lamin][research_high_reynolds_1999_2], [Prasannakumar et al 2024][research_prasannakumar_2024], which is a small literature for a component that decides whether the whole scheme is worth installing. Had the assumed suction coefficient been an order of magnitude wrong, the duct would not have fitted in the wing. The turbojet installation itself was moved to the rear fuselage to keep the wing clean, which is why the aeroplane looks as it does.

**The suction was metered finely and the noise was kept out deliberately.** [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998] records 96 suction control valves employed to control the suction in the slots independently, and a system that operated between 85 and 130 percent of the nominal design flow so that the unproven method of estimating the flow could be tested. Engine noise can trip a laminar boundary layer, so a movable plug in each engine inlet set up a standing shock wave that kept the compressor noise from reaching the wing, in [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963]. The review group of October 1965 found that laminar flow persisted at external sound levels about 6 decibels above the established criterion, that noise inside the suction ducts did no harm unless a duct was driven at a resonance large enough to disturb the flow outside the slots, that atmospheric turbulence strong enough to change the angle of attack by one degree either way and to produce 0.3 g at the centre of gravity did not disturb the laminar flow, and that small changes of flight condition, of about 0.04 in lift coefficient or 0.03 in Mach number, did not affect it appreciably. Joslin adds that slot Reynolds numbers of 120 to 140 produced disturbances at the slot that dominated any noise from the ducts, so the slots were held below about 100.

## The Flight Test Record

First flight was on 18 April 1963 from Edwards Air Force Base, in the [Northrop X-21A][ref_x21] reference entry, and [Braslow 1999][book_braslow_1999] places the start of flight research in April 1963. Two aircraft were converted, serials 55-0408 and 55-0410. By August 1963 the flights had covered altitudes from 8,000 to 45,000 feet and speeds up to Mach 0.8, in [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963]. [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998] gives the envelope as Mach 0.3 to 0.8 at altitudes from 5,000 to 44,000 feet, and the programme as more than 200 flights with the laminar flow control system operating.

**The progress was real and slow.** When the suction system was first switched on, about the outer twelve percent of the wing went laminar. By the middle of 1963 that had reached roughly the outer half, through adjustments to pumping pressures and to the flow distribution among the leading-edge slots, and the project managers were already persuaded that closer slot spacing would be needed on the inboard leading edges, in [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963]. By the end of the programme, in [Braslow 1999][book_braslow_1999], laminar flow was being obtained over more than ninety-five percent of the area intended for laminarisation.

### The Laminar Run, Year by Year

The chordwise results are better compared as the Reynolds number of the laminar run, the product of the laminar fraction of the chord and the chord Reynolds number,

$$Re_{x} = \frac{x_{\text{lam}}}{c} \, Re_{c}$$

Joslin gives the progress on the mean aerodynamic chord as follows.

| period | laminar chord | $Re_{c}$ | $Re_{x}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| end of 1963 | nearly 0.60 | $20 \times 10^{6}$ | $12 \times 10^{6}$ |
| 1964 | 0.70 | $20 \times 10^{6}$ | $14 \times 10^{6}$ |
| 1964 | 0.30 to 0.55 | $30 \times 10^{6}$ | up to $16.5 \times 10^{6}$ |
| 1965 | 0.96 | $20 \times 10^{6}$ | $19.2 \times 10^{6}$ |
| 1965 | 0.81 | $30 \times 10^{6}$ | $24.3 \times 10^{6}$ |
| 1965 | 0.59 | $40 \times 10^{6}$ | $23.6 \times 10^{6}$ |

**The run Reynolds number roughly doubled in two years and then stopped growing with chord Reynolds number.** In 1965 the run at $30 \times 10^{6}$ and at $40 \times 10^{6}$ is the same to within four percent, which is what a fixed limit on the run, rather than on the fraction of chord, would produce. The review group of October 1965 reported laminar runs up to a length Reynolds number of 46 million on some parts of the wing, against consistently obtained maxima of less than half that, below 23 million, the year before, and judged that design procedures suitable for 60 million were known but would need flight verification. It also recorded that some direct measurements of overall drag by speed-power methods agreed well with prediction.

The chord Reynolds numbers are consistent with the geometry used in this article. With the assumed taper ratio of 0.30 the root chord is $c_{r} = 2S/[b(1+\lambda)] = 20.57$ feet and the mean aerodynamic chord is

$$\bar{c}_{\text{MAC}} = \tfrac{2}{3} c_{r} \frac{1 + \lambda + \lambda^{2}}{1 + \lambda} = \tfrac{2}{3} \times 20.57 \times \frac{1.39}{1.30} = 14.66\ \text{ft}$$

so at Mach 0.70 and 40,000 feet the chord Reynolds number is $1.913 \times 10^{7} \times (0.70/0.75) \times (14.66/13.37) = 1.96 \times 10^{7}$, the $20 \times 10^{6}$ of Joslin's example flight at that condition, on which 74 percent of the upper surface and 61 percent of the lower were laminar.

Set against the programme guarantee of seventy percent of the wing, that is a success. **Set against the purpose of the aeroplane, it is one objective out of three.**

### The Review Group of October 1965

The review group met on 14 and 15 October 1965 at the Norair Division in Hawthorne, California, and dated its report 8 November 1965. It was chaired by Holt Ashley and signed by members from NASA, the Federal Aviation Agency, Lockheed, Douglas, Boeing, North American Aviation and Colorado State University, and its report is reproduced in [Braslow 1999][book_braslow_1999]. Braslow's narrative says that the third objective, service experience comparable to an operational aircraft, had not even been initiated, because the effort had gone into surface tolerance and spanwise contamination. **The report's own wording is milder.** It says that the programme had not been able completely to address the operational questions because of difficulties with the basic aerodynamic design, that those difficulties had been overcome, and that the programme should now be directed toward answering them.

Those questions were specific. Can the structure be built to the roughness and waviness criteria, and at what weight and complexity? How far does the surface deteriorate in service? What does it take to keep the wing clean of insects, mud and rain on a routine basis? How tolerant is laminar flow of rain, sleet, ice crystals, cloud and gusts? And what do manoeuvres, off-design lift and non-optimum suction do to it? The group judged an operational suitability evaluation the minimum effort that could justify further funds, recommended that maintainability data be collected to Air Force standard procedures so that availability could be compared with non-laminar aircraft, and asked that the modified aircraft go to the Air Force Flight Test Center well ahead of Northrop's schedule. It asked for serious consideration of a wholly new set of metal upper wing surfaces needing no filler, judged closing out the flight programme at once particularly undesirable because a large military transport with laminar flow control, perhaps drawn from the C-5A line, was under consideration, and called for systems analyses comparing laminar flow control with better engines, lighter structures, hydrogen fuel and refuelling.

The operational phase never took place. The modification was never made, and the reason recorded by the programme's NASA consultant is the resource demands of the war in Vietnam rather than any finding about laminar flow.

### The Maintenance the Programme Did Record

**The programme was not silent on maintenance, and what it recorded points away from insects.** In August 1963 [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963] reported that the early flight results had substantiated an estimate that the airframe of a laminar flow control aircraft would cost about ten percent more to maintain than a conventional one, that the wing would probably not need cleaning after every flight, and that steam cleaning once a month would keep it adequately free of dirt and grease. He also reported that insects sticking to the wing had not so far bothered the suction system. The review group of October 1965 confirmed that insect strikes had not disturbed the laminar flow, and warned in the same sentence that the flights had been made over a desert where insects are sparse and small, so the result said nothing about operation worldwide. [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998], drawing on Fowell and Antonatos, records where the ground maintenance time actually went, which was mostly to repairing the epoxy-filled splices between wing panels. **The maintenance burden the X-21 did record was a manufacturing defect, not an operational one**, and it is the defect the review group's new metal upper surfaces were meant to remove.

## Comparison With Ground Prediction

The wind tunnel and glove work of the 1950s predicted that suction would hold laminar flow to high chord Reynolds numbers, and the flight article confirmed it. On that axis the ground prediction was good. [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998] records about 160 hours of high-speed and 1,300 hours of low-speed wind tunnel testing of a model X-21A wing, and good agreement between tunnel and flight once aeroelastic deformation and the flight pressure distributions were allowed for. The suction requirement was the exception, measured 50 percent above prediction at Mach 0.74 and 41,400 feet, which is the theory erring on the side that costs power. Companion tunnel tests showed that a sudden loss of laminar flow would not endanger the aircraft, although the lateral-directional and long-period longitudinal motions might need more artificial damping than a turbulent aircraft.

**On two axes it was not, and both failures were of the same kind.** The severity of spanwise contamination had been seen in British swept-wing experiments and in small-scale tunnel tests, and its significance had gone unrecognised until a full-size swept wing met it. The surface tolerance achievable in manufacture was likewise predicted from panels rather than from wings, and a wing has splices where a panel does not.

**Neither is a failure of theory.** Both are failures of scale, in the specific sense that the feature which caused the trouble did not exist on the model. A tunnel model has no spanwise splice and no fuselage junction of the relevant size. The transition-prediction methods that have since been built to carry this kind of analysis are [L. ## What the Data Changed

**Laminar flow control stopped being a question of physics and became a question of engineering practice**, and the change is due more to this programme than to any other.

Attachment-line contamination went from an unrecognised curiosity to a named design constraint with a criterion attached, and every subsequent swept laminar wing has had to satisfy it. The manufacturing tolerance problem was shown to be solvable, expensively. The atmospheric particle problem was identified and left open for want of data.

**What did not change is the thing the programme is remembered for.** No service-maintenance evaluation was made, and the maintenance the programme did log was dominated by repairs to filled splices, so nothing was learned about whether a well-made wing could be kept clean in service, and the widespread belief that the X-21 settled that question is not supported by the record of the programme.

### Predicting Transition, Which They Could Not Do

### Making the Surface Is Still the Hard Part

Sixty years on, the manufacturing problem the programme spent its first two years on remains the one that decides whether the technology is affordable, in [Drake et al 2005][research_drake_2005], [Bender and Drake 2006][research_bender_drake_2006], [Leigh et al 2010][research_leigh_2010], [Tabarés and Cuenca 2022][research_tabares_cuenca_2022], [Li et al 2024, A pressure-loss model for flow-th][research_li_2024_3], [Dou et al 2026][research_dou_2026], [Kashaev et al 2026][research_kashaev_2026].

**What has changed is the aperture.** The X-21 cut slots and drilled more than eight hundred thousand metering holes with purpose-built tooling. The modern surface is laser-drilled microperforated titanium, with holes measured in tens of micrometres and counted in millions, made by a process that did not exist and would have been unimaginable as a production method in 1963. **The tolerance requirement has not relaxed at all**, which the roughness criterion in this article shows was never going to happen, since it is set by the physics of the boundary layer rather than by the state of the art.

### What the System Costs the Aircraft

The article charges the suction system a thermodynamic power and states plainly that it does not charge weight, volume or structural penalty. The literature that does carry those is [Perry and Ansell 2017][research_perry_ansell_2017], [Zhao and Yu 2024][research_zhao_yu_2024], [Improving Wind Turbine Power with Boundary Layer Suction 2025][research_improving_wind_2025], and it is thin.

**That thinness is itself informative.** The installed cost of a suction system is exactly the quantity a manufacturer needs and exactly the quantity that is hardest to publish, because it is specific to an airframe and commercially sensitive. The public literature is rich on the aerodynamics and poor on the installation, which is the same imbalance the X-21 programme had.

### What the Survey Shows

**The X-21 was right about the physics and the field has never disputed it.** Laminar flow control works, the suction power is a small fraction of the drag saved, and nothing in sixty years has overturned either statement.

**What the field did instead was retreat from the installation.** Hybrid laminar flow control gave up the aft suction. Natural laminar flow gave up the suction. Riblets gave up laminar flow. Each step traded aerodynamic benefit for a surface that could be manufactured and maintained, and every one of those trades is a decision about the question the X-21 never got to answer.

**And that question is now being answered in the affirmative.** Insect residue is being defeated by coatings rather than by cleaning, the crossflow is being controlled rather than avoided, and the surface is being made by processes that did not exist. The verdict passed on this programme in 1965 was that laminar flow control was impractical to maintain. **It was passed without evidence, and the evidence that has accumulated since largely contradicts it.**

## Where the Framing Breaks Down

The calculation above treats the suction system as a compressor restoring a total pressure, and charges the aircraft the ideal work divided by an efficiency. **That is a thermodynamic accounting, not an installation.** It does not include the weight of the ducts and turbines, the volume they take from the fuel tanks, the drag of the nacelles that house them, or the structural penalty of a skin perforated by some two hundred slots. A real trade would carry all of those, and they act against the technology rather than for it.

The whole-aircraft figures rest on an assumed wetted-area breakdown, an assumed form factor and an assumed non-friction drag increment. They are the weakest numbers in the article and are offered as an order of magnitude. **They are also the numbers most sensitive to consistent bookkeeping.** A zero-lift drag coefficient that implies a form factor, set against a laminarised area credited with a flat-plate saving that carries none, understates the benefit by about a quarter, which is why the coefficient is built here from its parts so that the charge and the credit use the same basis.

The aspect-ratio argument inverts a drag polar that already contains three assumptions, so the figure of 11.8 should be read as evidence that the required planform is very different rather than as a specification for it.

The attachment-line calculation assumes a leading-edge radius and a taper ratio. The conclusion drawn from it, that the wing root sits near the contamination threshold, survives across the plausible range, but the precise value does not.

**The suction coefficient is the least well constrained input of all.** The value used is a design figure from the laminar flow control literature rather than a measured X-21 quantity, and the entire margin over break-even scales inversely with it. The local values the programme reports are two and a half to five times higher, and the reported flow rates can be read either way, so the margin of 5.8 is an upper estimate for the nominal machinery and the true figure for the X-21 lies somewhere between about one and about four.

The cloud calculation assumes a number density of ten thousand crystals per cubic metre, which no source states. Hall's typical cirrus gives nearly nine times as many, so the assumption errs toward understating the effect, and the threshold of about eight crystals per cubic metre for erratic operation inherits every crudeness of the wedge model, including the assumption that every wedge starts at the leading edge and spans the mean chord.

## The Source Base

The programme is unusually well documented for one that ended without a production application, and the reason is that a NASA engineer who consulted on it wrote its history thirty years later.

[Braslow 1999][book_braslow_1999] is the load-bearing source for the programme narrative, including the Vietnam attribution, and it reproduces the primary document behind the rest, the report of the review group that met in October 1965. [Kosin 1965][research_kosin_1965] is the contemporary engineering description in the Journal of Aircraft. The most useful description of the wing's internal construction is [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963], a trade-press account published while the aeroplane was flying, which is where the distinction between the slots and the metering holes is stated plainly. [Hall 1964][ref_hall_1964] is the Northrop report on the cloud problem, now on the NASA Technical Reports Server. The quantitative results of the Northrop final report, its suction coefficients, flow rates, tolerances and year-by-year laminar extents, are known here through the summary in [Joslin 1998][ref_joslin_1998], because the Northrop reports themselves, held by the Defense Technical Information Center, were not retrievable. Where this article quotes those numbers it quotes a careful secondary summary of a primary document rather than the document.

**A caution about the secondary literature.** The figure of eight hundred thousand slots is repeated by reference works, encyclopaedias and aviation histories alike. The 1963 description in [Butz 1963][ref_butz_1963] makes clear that the number counts drilled holes connecting plenums to ducts, and the slot spacings Joslin reports put the slots at of the order of a hundred on each surface. **The error is not consequential for the physics and it is very consequential for the impression the reader forms**, since it converts a precisely engineered surface into something that sounds like a colander.

### The Shape of the Reference Base

**This article's reference base leans toward the contemporary literature.** Counting the research records in the References that carry a year, 209 of 973, or 21.5 percent, are dated 1965 or earlier and 474, or 48.7 percent, are dated 2011 or later.

**One homonym in this subject lives inside aerodynamics rather than outside it.** Boundary layer control means suction to hold laminar flow and reduce drag, and it also means suction or blowing to delay separation and increase lift. They share a name, a mechanism and often an author, and they are different technologies answering different questions, so a title that names boundary layer control does not by itself place a record in this subject.

### What the Survey Admits

**The phrase boundary layer belongs to meteorology as much as to aerodynamics**, and a whole journal is devoted to the atmospheric boundary layer. Operating-room laminar air flow, crossflow filtration and a Cretaceous stick-insect fossil share this subject's vocabulary in the same way and none of them is part of it.

**Records are admitted when their titles match this article's subject vocabulary**, the subject being suction laminar flow control, the stability theory behind it, the surfaces it needs and the insects and ice that contaminate them. A person has read the titles that the exclusion patterns flag and a random sample of the rest, and records read as off topic, and others of their kinds, are removed. The survey's 979 records come from NASA's Technical Reports Server, the Defense Technical Information Center, the Office of Scientific and Technical Information and the journal literature. Of these, 28.8 percent are report-server records, and their median year is 2007. Many words this subject depends on also name something else, and a record that shares only such a word is excluded. Perforated also names panels made to absorb sound, laminar also names flames and the internal flow and heat transfer of tubes, channels and fins, and coating also names protection against corrosion and the surfacing of pavements, insulators and face masks. Non-Newtonian, magnetohydrodynamic and porous-medium flows, chemical mixers, fuel cells, surgery, seaweed and power grids share the vocabulary of the boundary layer without its subject. Notices of erratum, correction, retraction or withdrawal, figure, table and supplementary-file records, peer-review reports and journal front matter are excluded as well, because a survey counts research works and those are parts of works or editorial events rather than works.

**Some records of doubtful relevance are kept.** Laminar flow with suction or injection through the porous wall of a tube or channel, magnetohydrodynamic boundary-layer control with suction, anti-icing and leading-edge erosion coatings for wind turbine blades, and drag reduction by polymers and riblets in liquids each share with this subject either the mechanism of wall suction, the problem of a contaminated leading edge or the aim of reducing skin friction, so each is kept and a reader may weigh it accordingly.

**Not every research title has been read for relevance, so the off-topic share that remains is an estimate from a sample.** The most recent measurement read 300 records drawn at random from those not previously read and found 5 off topic, or 1.7 percent, a film that blocks ultraviolet light, a turbopump cavitation study, two studies of non-Newtonian flow and a study of convection in a porous medium. The records that sample exposed have been removed, together with others of their kinds, so that figure overstates what remains by an unknown amount, and no later unread sample has been drawn.

## Epistemic State

**Established by the programme and not in dispute.** Laminar flow was obtained and held on a large swept wing over more than ninety-five percent of the intended area. Surface tolerance and spanwise contamination were the two problems that consumed the schedule, and both were overcome.

**Computed here, and dependent on stated assumptions.** The suction penalty is about a sixth of the friction saving at the nominal cruise condition, the break-even suction coefficient is about six times the design value, and the suction drag coefficient is independent of altitude. The last of those is exact given the model. The first two carry the uncertainty of the suction coefficient and the duct recovery. Against the local suction coefficients the programme reports, the margin falls to between about one and two, so what survives the primary record is that suction cost less than it saved, not that it cost a sixth.

**Inferred, and offered as inference.** That the X-21 as a converted bomber could not have demonstrated more than about twelve percent in range follows from the wetted-area accounting, and the wetted areas are estimated. That the 1963 claim of a lift-to-drag ratio above thirty is unreachable on this planform at any level of laminarisation follows from induced drag alone consuming 73 percent of the drag budget such a ratio allows, and that argument is stronger than the wetted-area one because it does not depend on how much of the aeroplane is laminarised.

**Asserted widely and not supported by the record.** That the X-21 demonstrated laminar flow control to be impractical on maintenance grounds. The maintenance evaluation was not performed. The maintenance the programme did record went mostly to repairing filled splices, and insects did not disturb the laminar flow in the desert where it flew.

**Read in the record, with a caveat on wording.** Braslow writes that the service-experience objective had not even been initiated by October 1965. The review group's report says the programme had not been able completely to address the operational questions. Both agree that no operational evaluation took place.

**Unknown then and largely unknown still.** The operational significance of atmospheric ice particles, for want of statistics on particle size and concentration along real routes.

## Out of Scope

Supersonic laminar flow control, which the programme discussed and did not test, and which later became its own subject. The detailed stability theory of the slotted surface, including the disturbances the slots themselves introduce. The structural and weight penalties of the suction wing, which are named as a limitation above and not computed. The economics of laminar flow control for commercial operators, which belongs to the transport design studies rather than to this aeroplane.

## Conclusion

The X-21A did the hard part. It held laminar flow on a large swept wing in real air, having first beaten a surface-tolerance problem and an attachment-line problem that nobody had properly anticipated, and it did so with a suction system that, on the assumptions used here, cost about a sixth of what it returned, and on the local coefficients the programme reports cost between about two fifths and most of it, since the penalty fraction of 0.172 scales with the coefficient to 0.43 at $5 \times 10^{-4}$ and 0.86 at $10 \times 10^{-4}$.

**The power trade was never the difficulty.** The margin over break-even is a factor of six at the assumed suction coefficient and nearer two at the coefficients the programme reports, the penalty falls with Mach number, and it does not depend on altitude at all. An aeroplane designed around this from the start would have collected far more of it than a converted bomber ever could, which is why the case for the technology was always made about an aircraft that did not exist.

What the programme was for, in the end, was to find out whether such a wing could be lived with. **It never found out.** The maintenance objective had not been reached when the funding stopped, the recommended wing modification that would have made the evaluation meaningful was never made, and the aeroplane was retired to Edwards to be photographed. The verdict that laminar flow control is impractical because of maintenance was reached anyway, and it has outlived every engineer who could have said that the experiment was not run.

**That is the unusual thing about this aircraft in a series full of failures.** The X-19 fell out of the sky. The X-20 was cancelled with a mock-up. The X-21 succeeded at what it was asked to do and was then remembered for failing at something it was never allowed to attempt.
