Space agencies and the aerospace industry have written down,
in considerable detail,
how to plan a programme,
how to track its progress
and how to account for its money.
This article surveys those documents.
It covers the plan and its baselines,
the work breakdown structure,
the schedule,
the cost estimate,
earned value management,
the risk register,
the periodic status report
and the financial reports that agencies and companies publish.

The survey finds three things.
The first is that the regimes keep the same small set of records under vocabularies that differ.
The second is that they measure performance in two different ways.
United States practice computes earned value,
which prices the work done at its budget and compares that figure with the plan and with the cost.
The three European management standards read for this survey contain no earned value management.
They compare a baseline with a current forecast,
milestone by milestone and work package by work package.
The third is that the phrase cost per mission names at least five quantities of different kinds,
and a reader cannot compare a figure quoted without its definition with another.

The article has three parts.
The first describes the records one at a time,
from the plan to the status report,
and sets the practice of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration,
NASA,
beside United States defence practice
and the standards of the European Cooperation for Space Standardization,
ECSS.
The second describes reporting to overseers and to the public,
the several meanings of cost per mission,
the financial reporting of a commercial launch company
and the launch manifest.
The third draws out what the practices share
and consolidates it into a status report and a set of financial records that a small programme can use.
The description of each document rests on the document itself.
The synthesis and the consolidated structures are the author's,
and the [Epistemic State](#epistemic-state) section separates the two.

The article does not teach accounting or scheduling,
it gives no legal or financial advice,
and it characterises the performance of no agency and no company.

## Kinds of Record

Eight kinds of record recur.

- **The plan.**
  A signed agreement states what the programme will do,
  how it will manage the work
  and what it commits to.
- **The baseline.**
  An approved statement of scope,
  schedule
  and cost serves as the reference for every later measurement.
- **The work breakdown structure.**
  A tree divides all the work into elements that someone can plan,
  cost
  and report.
- **The schedule.**
  A network of activities and milestones carries dates and logical links.
- **The estimate.**
  A documented prediction of cost appears before the work as a cost estimate
  and during the work as an estimate at completion.
- **The risk register.**
  A list holds the things that may go wrong,
  each with a likelihood,
  a consequence
  and a decision.
- **The status report.**
  A periodic account sets progress against the baseline.
- **The financial statement.**
  An accounting report shows resources,
  costs
  and position for a period.

The words differ by regime.

| Term | NASA and United States usage | European usage |
|---|---|---|
| Programme and project | A programme directs one or more projects, and a project has requirements, a life-cycle cost, a beginning and an end | The ECSS management standards speak of the project and of a chain of customers and suppliers |
| Plan | Program Plan and Project Plan | Project management plan |
| Baseline | Agency Baseline Commitment, Management Agreement and performance measurement baseline | Baseline schedule, original baseline cost plan and current baseline cost plan |
| Work breakdown structure | A product-oriented family tree | A hierarchy of activities derived from a product tree |
| Schedule | Integrated master schedule | Baseline schedule and current working schedule |
| Estimate | Life-cycle cost estimate and estimate at completion | Cost estimate report and estimate at completion |
| Risk | The potential for shortfalls against stated objectives | An undesirable situation with a likelihood and a negative consequence |
| Status report | Monthly reviews and the contractor's monthly performance report | Progress report, schedule progress report and cost and manpower report |
| Financial statement | Balance sheet, net cost, changes in net position and budgetary resources | Financial position, financial performance, changes in net assets and cash flows |

This article writes programme in the British spelling.
It keeps the spelling Program where the word is part of the name of a NASA document,
and it leaves every quotation as its source prints it.

NASA defines the first pair of terms in its directive on space flight programmes and projects.
A programme has
"a management structure that initiates and directs one or more projects."
A project is narrower.
"Space flight projects are a specific investment identified in a Program Plan having defined requirements, a life-cycle cost, a beginning, and an end."
[NASA 2024a][agency_nasa_nid_7120_148].
The directive names four types of programme,
namely single-project,
uncoupled,
loosely coupled
and tightly coupled,
and it sorts projects into three categories by life-cycle cost and priority,
with breaks at 365 million and 2 billion dollars.

The European terms have a weaker footing than a reader might expect.
The ECSS standard on cost and schedule management defines only six terms of its own.
It sends the reader to the ECSS glossary for cost breakdown structure,
estimate at completion
and estimate to completion
[ECSS 2008a][ecss_m_st_60c].
The current glossary,
issued in 2023,
does not contain those three terms,
and neither document defines schedule,
baseline
or milestone as a term
[ECSS 2023][ecss_s_st_00_01c_rev1]
[ECSS 2026][ecss_definitions_2026].
The working definitions are therefore the descriptive sentences of the standard itself,
which later sections quote.

## Plans and Baselines

### The Governing NASA Directive

NASA governs space flight programmes and projects with one directive,
NASA Procedural Requirements 7120.5.
Revision F took effect on 3 August 2021,
and its fourth change dates from 14 November 2024
[NASA 2021a][agency_nasa_npr_7120_5f].
An interim directive of 9 December 2024 restates and amends it.
The page of the directives library for revision F says of the interim text that it
"is the interim directive to NPR 7120.5F and contains the most recent requirements. Please use NID 7120.148."
NPR stands for NASA Procedural Requirements
and NID for NASA Interim Directive.
The standing of the interim text needs a caution.
Its cover prints an expiration date of 9 December 2025,
which has passed.
A single line in the library's listing of interim directives records an extension to 1 August 2027,
and the author found no reissued cover.
This article cites revision F as amended by the interim directive,
and its quotations follow the interim text
[NASA 2024a][agency_nasa_nid_7120_148].

### The Program Plan and the Project Plan

The directive requires two plans and gives a template for each in an appendix.
The Program Plan
"is an agreement among the program manager, Center Director, and Mission Directorate Associate Administrator (MDAA)."
The Project Plan adds one party.
"H.1.1 The Project Plan is an agreement among the project manager, program manager, Center Director, and the Mission Directorate Associate Administrator (MDAA)."
The template leaves no section to choice.
"H.1.3 Each section of the Project Plan template is required. If a section is not applicable to a particular project, indicate by stating that in the appropriate section and provide a rationale."
The Program Plan carries the same rule,
and the omission of a section that does apply needs a waiver.

The Project Plan template has six numbered parts.

| Part | Heading | Sections |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | Project Overview | Introduction, objectives, mission description and technical approach, authority and governance and management structure and implementation approach, stakeholder definition |
| 2.0 | Project Baselines | Requirements baseline, work breakdown structure baseline, schedule baseline, resource, joint cost and schedule confidence level |
| 3.0 | Project Control Plans | Twenty-nine plans, beginning with the technical, schedule and cost control plan, the safety and mission assurance plan and the risk management plan |
| 4.0 | Waivers or Deviations Log | A log |
| 5.0 | Change Log | A log |
| 6.0 | Appendices | Appendices |

The remaining control plans cover,
among other subjects,
acquisition,
technology development,
systems engineering,
software,
verification and validation,
reviews,
mission operations,
logistics,
science data,
configuration management,
security
and knowledge management.
The Program Plan template follows the same pattern with six sections in its overview,
five baselines
and twenty-two control plans.
The project template prints the heading of its section 2.4 as the single word Resource,
where the programme template prints Resource Baseline.

Part 2.0 is the part that matters for later measurement.
The template describes what it holds.
"Project baselines consist of a set of requirements, cost (including project-held UFE), schedule, and technical content that forms the foundation for program/project execution and reporting done as part of NASA's performance assessment and governance process."
UFE stands for unallocated future expenses,
which a later paragraph defines.
The section on the work breakdown structure asks for the structure and its dictionary down to the second level.
The section on the schedule asks for a summary.
"Present a summary of the project's IMS, including all critical milestones, major events, life-cycle reviews, and KDPs throughout the project life cycle."
IMS stands for integrated master schedule
and KDP for key decision point,
the event at which a decision authority allows a project to pass to its next phase.
The section on resources asks for money by year and by element.
"Present the project funding requirements by fiscal year."
The template asks for the new obligation authority in real-year dollars for prior,
current
and remaining years,
and it continues with a second instruction.
"Provide a breakdown of the project's funding requirements to the WBS Level 2 elements."
WBS stands for work breakdown structure.

The first control plan binds the project to report.
The template tells the author of the plan to
"Describe how the project plans to report technical, schedule, and cost status to the program manager, including the frequency and level of detail of reporting."
The same section asks how the project will monitor its commitment
and how it will act before cost growth reaches the threshold for a new baseline.

### Two Baselines and the Rule for Rebaselining

NASA keeps two nested envelopes of cost and schedule for each project.
The inner one belongs to the manager.
"2.4.1.2 The Management Agreement contained within the Decision Memorandum defines the parameters and authorities over which the program or project manager has management control."
The outer one is the Agency's baseline,
which for a programme or project of 250 million dollars or more is a commitment to the Office of Management and Budget and to Congress,
and the agency sets it in the decision memorandum at the key decision point that opens implementation,
known as KDP C.
The directive calls it the Agency Baseline Commitment,
which it abbreviates to ABC,
and insists that it is unique.
"Only one official baseline exists for a NASA program or project, and it is the Agency Baseline Commitment."
The difference between the two is money held above the project.
The directive defines unallocated future expenses as the part of the estimated cost,
needed to meet a stated confidence level,
that the estimate has not yet allocated to elements of the work breakdown structure.
An advisory of May 2025 states the arithmetic.
"This UFE is equal to the ABC minus the MA"
[NASA 2025a][agency_nasa_naii_7120_1].
MA stands for Management Agreement.
That NASA therefore runs each project against two nested baselines is the author's reading of these passages.

A third baseline serves earned value.
The directive defines the performance measurement baseline as
"The time-phased budget plan for accomplishing all authorized work scope in a project's life cycle, which includes both NASA internal costs and supplier costs."
It excludes the unallocated future expenses.

The directive limits the occasions on which the outer baseline may change.
NASA must rebaseline a programme or project when its estimated development cost exceeds the development cost in the commitment by 30 per cent or more,
and for a project above 250 million dollars Congress must also have reauthorised it.
Two further triggers are judgements of the NASA Associate Administrator,
namely that events outside the agency make a new baseline appropriate,
or that the scope has changed or an interruption has halted the project.
The paragraph ends by closing the door on anything less.
"ABCs for projects are not rebaselined to reflect cost or schedule growth that does not meet one or more of these criteria."
Development cost has a defined span in the directive,
from the approval to proceed to implementation at the start of Phase C
to operational readiness at the end of Phase D.

Growth below that threshold leads to a replan and not to a new baseline.
The Government Accountability Office,
GAO,
describes the replan in its annual assessment of NASA's major projects.
"It is driven by changes in program or project cost parameters, such as if development cost growth is 15 percent or more of the estimate in the baseline report or a major milestone is delayed by 6 months or more from the baseline's date."
[GAO 2026][gao_26_108556].

### The European Project Management Plan

The ECSS standards arrange the same matter around a contract between a customer and a supplier,
and the chain of such contracts runs from the agency down to the smallest subcontractor.
The standard on project planning and implementation is ECSS-M-ST-10C,
whose first revision dates from 6 March 2009
[ECSS 2009][ecss_m_st_10c_rev1].
The standards on cost and schedule management,
ECSS-M-ST-60C,
and on risk management,
ECSS-M-ST-80C,
both date from 31 July 2008
[ECSS 2008a][ecss_m_st_60c]
[ECSS 2008b][ecss_m_st_80c].
All three stood on the list of active ECSS standards in November 2026.

Every supplier writes a plan.
"Each supplier in the customer-supplier chain shall prepare a project management plan (PMP) in conformance with Annex A."
The supplier submits it to the customer for approval.
The annex fixes eleven sections.

1. Introduction.
2. Applicable and reference documents.
3. Objectives and constraints of the project.
4. Project organization.
5. Project breakdown structures.
6. Configuration,
   information and documentation management.
7. Cost and schedule management.
8. Integrated logistic support.
9. Risk management.
10. Product assurance management.
11. Engineering management.

Sections 6,
7,
10
and 11 may give a brief description and refer to a separate plan.
Section 8 refers to a standard numbered ECSS-M-ST-70.
The ECSS lists of active and superseded standards hold no document of that number.
They hold only ECSS-M-70A of 1996,
which the ECSS site marks as cancelled and superseded by a standard on integrated product support issued in November 2024
[ECSS 2024][ecss_i_st_30_10c].

The European baselines are two,
one of dates and one of money.
The baseline schedule
"is developed by the supplier and agreed by the contractual parties and is the reference for schedule reporting and performance measurement."
The baseline cost plan is the set of financial data on which customer and supplier have agreed.
The standard states its evolution as a sum.
"Original Baseline Cost Plan (OBCP) + Approved changes = Current Baseline Cost Plan (CBCP)"
The only route to a new baseline is therefore an approved change,
which the standard calls a contract change notice and abbreviates to CCN.
"The implementation of the CCN results in a new baseline agreed for the project, against which cost control, analysis and reporting is performed."
The standard gives the change notice twelve fields,
among them the reason for the change,
its price,
its effect on the schedule
and the signatures.

The two regimes thus answer the question of rebaselining differently.
NASA sets a numerical threshold and two judgements above the project.
ECSS sets no threshold and requires the agreement of both parties to each change.
That comparison is the author's inference from the two texts.

## The Work Breakdown Structure

### Definition and Completeness

NASA's handbook on the subject is in its fifth revision,
Revision E of June 2025,
and carries a new report number.
The directive still cites the number of the 2010 edition
[NASA 2025b][agency_nasa_wbs_handbook_2025].
The handbook defines the structure by its products.
"A WBS is a product-oriented family tree that identifies the hardware, software, services, and all other deliverables required to achieve an end project objective."
It then states that the tree holds everything.
"Work scope not contained in the project WBS should not be considered part of the project."
A second sentence covers every performer.
"All project effort must be included, including all NASA in-house, contracted, international partner, university, and any other performing entity implementations."
A third forbids double counting,
since a properly structured tree lets cost,
schedule
and performance data roll up from the lowest elements
"without allocation of a single element of work scope to two or more WBS elements."
The handbook does not use the phrase 100 per cent rule.

The defence standard does use it.
MIL-STD-881F of 13 May 2022 is the standard practice of the Department of Defense,
DoD,
for the work breakdown structures of defence materiel
[DoD 2022a][mil_std_881f].
Its definition begins with the same image,
"A product-oriented family tree composed of hardware, software, services, data, and facilities."
Its rule of completeness reads as follows.
"This is called the 100% rule, which states the next level of decomposition of a WBS element (child level) must represent 100% of the work applicable to the next higher level (parent level)."

The European standards build the tree in two steps.
The glossary defines the product tree as a
"hierarchical representation of the product into successive levels of detail"
and the work breakdown structure as a
"hierarchical representation of the activities necessary to complete a project"
[ECSS 2026][ecss_definitions_2026].
The planning standard derives the second from the first by adding support functions such as management,
engineering
and product assurance.
It states the purpose of the structure in one sentence.
"The WBS is the principal structure used in managing a project and provides a framework for managing cost, schedule and technical content."
Its rule of completeness sits among the requirements for the document.
"All defined work-packages together shall cover the total work scope."
The cost and schedule standard adds that proper use of the structure ensures
"that all work is included without duplication of effort."
ECSS does not call this a hundred per cent rule either.
The customer approves the product tree,
the work breakdown structure
and the project's organisation breakdown structure,
and each work package has a single responsible manager.

### NASA's Standard Level 2 Elements

NASA fixes the top of the tree.
"The top two levels of a project WBS are dictated and controlled by the Agency through standard, level-two WBS templates."
Level 1 is the whole project.
Project management develops and controls the levels below the second,
and the agency's accounting system captures cost to a depth of seven levels.

The handbook's dictionary gives the eleven standard elements of the second level for a space flight project.
The directive shows the same eleven numbers in a figure,
and the two documents do not word five of the elements alike.

| Number | Handbook dictionary, Revision E | Directive figure, read by eye |
|---|---|---|
| 01 | Project Management | Project Management |
| 02 | Systems Engineering | Systems Engineering |
| 03 | Safety and Mission Assurance | Safety and Mission Assurance |
| 04 | Science / Technology | Science/Technology |
| 05 | Payload | Payloads |
| 06 | Spacecraft(s) | Spacecraft |
| 07 | Mission Operations System | Mission Operations |
| 08 | Launch Vehicle / Services | Launch Vehicle/Services |
| 09 | Ground System(s) | Ground Systems |
| 10 | Systems Integration and Testing | Systems Integration and Testing |
| 11 | Education and Public Outreach | STEM and Public Outreach |

The figure in the directive is an image,
and the author read its labels by eye from the edition of revision F that the directives library publishes as web pages.
The differences in elements 05,
06,
07
and 09 are small.
Element 11 differs in a word.
The figure's label begins with the abbreviation STEM,
commonly read as science,
technology,
engineering
and mathematics,
where the handbook's label begins with Education.
A project that copies the template should say which of the two documents it followed.

The handbook's dictionary defines each element.
Project management,
for example,
covers
"The business and administrative planning, organizing, directing, coordinating, analyzing, controlling, and approval processes used to accomplish overall project objectives, which are not associated with specific hardware or software elements."
The handbook notes that the template assumes a typical spacecraft development project,
and it allows a project to delete an element that does not apply.
"The elements such as payload, launch vehicle/services, ground system(s), and mission operations (system) that are not applicable may be deleted."

### The Defence Standard's Appendices

MIL-STD-881F gives a template for each kind of system in an appendix.
Its foreword makes the standard binding.
"This Standard is mandatory for all Acquisition Category (ACAT) programs."
The standard defines three levels,
the whole system,
its major elements
and their subordinate hardware,
software
and services,
and its appendices run deeper.
"In the appendices of this MIL-STD, the WBS is defined to Level 3, 4, or 5 depending on the system."
The standard contains no rule that a structure must have three levels.
That rule appears in the cost guide of the Government Accountability Office.
"However, each WBS should, at the very least, include three levels."
[GAO 2020][gao_20_195g].

Appendix F covers space systems.
Its first level is the space system,
and its second level has five elements.

- 1.1 SEIT/PM and Support Equipment
- 1.2 Space Vehicle 1...n (Specify)
- 1.3 Ground Segment
- 1.4 Orbital Transfer Vehicle (OTV)
- 1.5 Launch Vehicle 1...n (Specify)

The abbreviation in the first element stands for systems engineering,
integration,
test
and programme management.
The fifth element refers the reader to Appendix I,
which covers launch vehicle systems and has twelve elements at its second level.

- 1.1 Systems Engineering, Integration, Test, Program Management (SEIT/PM)
- 1.2 Launch Vehicle
- 1.3 Mission Integration and Analysis 1...f (Specify)
- 1.4 Launch Operations Site 1...n (Specify)
- 1.5 Launch Site 1...n (Specify)
- 1.6 System Test and Evaluation
- 1.7 Training
- 1.8 Data
- 1.9 Peculiar Support Equipment
- 1.10 Common Support Equipment
- 1.11 Industrial Facilities
- 1.12 Initial Spares and Repair Parts

The two templates differ in kind.
NASA's second level mixes products,
such as the spacecraft,
with functions,
such as systems engineering and safety.
The defence template for a space system gathers the functions into one element and gives the rest to products.
That observation is the author's.

### The Dictionary and the Work Package Description

A tree of titles is not enough,
and each regime requires a text for each element.
NASA's handbook explains why.
"A WBS dictionary lists and defines the WBS element contents. It is prepared by the project team for the purposes of ensuring that all work scope has been identified and to eliminate duplication and overlap of work assignments."
It lists eight items for each element.

1. The element title.
2. The element code.
3. A description of the content,
   with quantities,
   associated work
   and contract end items where they apply.
4. The index of the structure.
5. The paragraph number of the scope definition,
   for example in a statement of work.
6. The number and title of any specification associated with the element.
7. The date,
   the revision number,
   the authorisation of the revision
   and the approved changes.
8. The budget and reporting number,
   which is the charge code.

A dictionary for a contract adds the contract line item and the contract number.
The handbook treats the dictionary as a controlled document under the baseline.

The ECSS counterpart is the work package description,
which the planning standard defines in a normative annex with eighteen elements.

1. Project name and project phase.
2. Title of the work package.
3. Unique identification and issue number.
4. Supplier or entity in charge.
5. Name and organisation of the work package manager.
6. Supplier's country.
7. Product to which the tasks belong,
   as a link to the product tree.
8. Objectives.
9. Tasks.
10. Inputs needed.
11. Interfaces or links with other tasks or work packages.
12. Constraints,
    requirements,
    standards
    and regulations.
13. Expected outputs.
14. Deliverables.
15. Location of delivery.
16. Start event,
    with its date.
17. End event,
    with its date.
18. Excluded tasks.

The last element is worth copying,
since a statement of what a package excludes prevents two packages from each assuming that the other holds a task.
The description carries no field for cost or labour.
Cost enters through the forms of the cost and schedule standard,
which a later section describes.
That reading of the annex is an inference.

ECSS also names the level at which reporting happens.
"Control work packages are identified by the supplier at the level in the WBS where visibility and control is required, and for which reporting is to be performed. The control work packages represent the total work-scope and are agreed by the customer."
An informative annex offers eleven questions to decide whether an element needs further decomposition.

## The Schedule

### The NASA Schedule Handbook

NASA's handbook on schedule management is in its second revision,
effective 15 March 2024
[NASA 2024b][agency_nasa_schedule_handbook_2024].
It defines the central record as a network.
"Integrated Master Schedule. An IMS is the complete, time-phased, logically-linked network of all P/p effort that is required to ensure that all objectives are met within approved commitments."
The handbook writes P/p for programme or project.
The word integrated has a stated meaning.
In the handbook's words,
it
"implies the incorporation of all activities, even contractor and subcontractor efforts, necessary to complete the P/p."

Four further terms carry most of the weight.

- **Baseline.**
  "The schedule performance baseline, or simply, the schedule baseline or baseline IMS, is the time-phased schedule of all the work to be performed, against which performance measurements are tracked and corrective actions are taken to ensure that the P/p is performing to plan with acceptable risk."
  The project keeps it under change control.
- **Critical path.**
  "Critical Path. The longest continuous string of activities from time now to P/p completion with the least amount of float."
- **Float.**
  "Float or Slack. In general terms, float is the number of workdays that an activity can be delayed without impacting the start of a later activity."
  The schedule computes it.
  "It is calculated by subtracting early dates from late dates (i.e., Float = Late Dates - Early Dates)."
- **Margin.**
  "Margin is intentionally loaded in the IMS just like any other activity; however, these activities do not have any defined scope, nor do they have any associated budget."

The handbook keeps the last two apart.
Float falls out of the logic of the network,
and margin is a quantity of time that the project plans on purpose against its risks and uncertainties.
The project manager owns the margin and releases it only by a formal process.
"P/ps will create a margin burndown curve and track against that burndown curve."

Milestones come in two kinds.
Sponsors own the control milestones.
The project controls the notification milestones and must tell the sponsor of any change to them,
and the handbook has the project compile these into a milestone registry.

The handbook sorts schedule reports into three kinds.
"These include: Status Reporting, Progress Reporting, and Forecasting."
Status reporting gives actual data alone.
The handbook is strict about the boundary.
"It does not include any analysis of schedule metrics, trends, comparisons to previous versions, or forecast projections."
Progress reporting compares the plan with the actual data,
and forecast reporting projects forward.
Under the first kind the handbook describes a timeline report and a milestone status report.
Under the second it adds a milestone variance report that compares baseline dates with current dates.
The figures in the handbook are examples,
and the author found no mandatory layout among them.
The rhythm is monthly.
"Typically, the IMS is statused at least monthly to support routine P/p reporting, such as monthly status reviews and other internal reviews."

The schedule file itself is also a deliverable.
A memorandum of the Office of the Chief Financial Officer of 15 August 2025 requires projects to submit their integrated master schedules in the native format of the scheduling tool
[NASA 2025c][agency_nasa_bpm_25_05].
"a. All NPR 7120.5 projects and single project programs over Life Cycle Cost (LCC) of \\$50 Million dollars shall submit quarterly."

### The Auditor's Ten Best Practices

The Government Accountability Office publishes a guide to best practices for project schedules,
dated December 2015
[GAO 2015][gao_16_89g].
The guide names ten best practices.

1. Capturing all activities.
2. Sequencing all activities.
3. Assigning resources to all activities.
4. Establishing the duration of all activities.
5. Verifying that the schedule can be traced horizontally and vertically.
6. Confirming that the critical path is valid.
7. Ensuring reasonable total float.
8. Conducting a schedule risk analysis.
9. Updating the schedule using actual progress and logic.
10. Maintaining a baseline schedule.

The guide warns against reading the list as a procedure.
"These best practices are in no particular order; they are not intended as a series of steps for developing the schedule."
It groups the ten under four characteristics.
"GAO's research tells us that the four characteristics of a high-quality, reliable schedule are that it is comprehensive, well-constructed, credible, and controlled."
A table in the guide assigns practices 1,
3
and 4 to the first characteristic,
practices 2,
6
and 7 to the second,
practices 5
and 8 to the third,
and practices 9
and 10 to the fourth.
The guide's definition of the critical path is shorter than NASA's.
It is the path of longest duration through the sequence of activities.

### The Defence Master Plan and Master Schedule

Defence practice places a plan of events above the schedule.
A guide of May 2023 defines the pair
[DoD 2023b][mil_imp_ims_guide_2023].
"The IMP is an event-based plan consisting of a hierarchy of project events, with each event supported by specific accomplishments, which are to be satisfied by specific criteria."
IMP stands for integrated master plan.
The schedule extends the plan downward.
"The IMS is an extension of the information contained within the IMP, reflecting not only the Events, Accomplishments, and Criteria identified in the IMP, but also Tasks and subtasks subordinate to the Criteria."
The guide adds that the approved plan generally becomes part of the contract.

### European Practice

The ECSS cost and schedule standard uses one document definition for two schedules,
the baseline schedule and the current working schedule.
"The schedule shall provide the following information, for both the baseline and current working schedules: 1. key milestones; 2. main and key inspection points (MIP / KIP); 3. identification of the activities; 4. flow of the activities (logical links between activities); 5. start / finish date of activities; 6. duration of activities; 7. identification of the critical path activities; 8. lower suppliers planned end dates."
The total float of each activity against the agreed milestones must be available,
and the standard recommends presentation as a Gantt chart.
The supplier submits the baseline schedule for the customer's approval.
The working schedule has a lesser standing,
which the standard states candidly.
"The current schedule identifies the most realistic view of the project status given by the supplier to the customer, and hence does not necessarily reflect an agreed and accepted project status."

The standard and the glossary define the critical path in different words.
The standard has
"chain of activities critical to the timely completion of the project"
and the glossary has
"chain of activities that determines the earliest completion of the project"
with a note that a delay of any task on the path extends the project.

ECSS names two instruments for watching the schedule.
The first is the milestone trend chart,
which plots the forecast date of each milestone against the date of each report.
A flat line means a steady milestone,
a rising line a delay
and a falling line an acceleration.
The second is a traffic light.
The standard prints thresholds of ten days for it,
in a descriptive clause and under a figure that it labels as an example.
The author therefore reads those thresholds as an illustration and not as a requirement.

## Cost Estimating

### Methods

NASA's handbook on cost estimating stands at Version 4.0 of February 2015,
and NASA's page of guidance documents still offered that version in November 2026
[NASA 2015a][agency_nasa_ceh_2015].
It names three basic methods and describes each.

- **Analogy.**
  "The analogy method uses the cost of a similar system, adjusts for differences, and estimates the cost of the new system."
- **Parametric.**
  "Parametric cost estimates are a result of a cost estimating methodology using statistical relationships between historical costs and other program variables such as system physical or performance characteristics, contractor output measures, or personnel loading."
- **Engineering build-up.**
  This method,
  which the handbook also calls grassroots,
  "produces a detailed project cost estimate that is computed by estimating the cost of every activity in a Work Breakdown Structure, summing these estimates, and adding appropriate overheads."

The handbook adds a fourth technique for a project already under way,
which is extrapolation from actual costs by means of earned value.
It states the weakness of the parametric method plainly.
"The implicit assumption in parametric cost estimating is that the same forces that affected cost in the past will affect cost in the future."
The informative annex of the ECSS cost and schedule standard lists the same three families under the names parametric,
analogy based
and bottom-up.

### The Steps of an Estimate

The NASA handbook divides the work into three parts and twelve tasks.

1. Receive the customer request and understand the project.
2. Build or obtain a work breakdown structure.
3. Define or obtain the project technical description.
4. Develop ground rules and assumptions.
5. Select the cost estimating methodology.
6. Select or build the cost model or tool.
7. Gather and normalise data.
8. Develop the cost estimate.
9. Develop and incorporate the cost risk assessment.
10. Document the cost estimate.
11. Present the cost estimate results.
12. Update the cost estimate as required.

Tasks 1 to 3 form the part on project definition,
tasks 4 to 7 the part on cost methodology
and tasks 8 to 12 the part on the estimate itself.
The handbook cautions that the order is not rigid.
"It is in practice often an iterative process that is rarely linear."

The cost guide of the Government Accountability Office,
dated March 2020,
also counts twelve steps,
and they are not the same twelve
[GAO 2020][gao_20_195g].

1. Define the estimate's purpose.
2. Develop the estimating plan.
3. Define the program.
4. Determine the estimating structure.
5. Identify ground rules and assumptions.
6. Obtain the data.
7. Develop the point estimate.
8. Conduct sensitivity analysis.
9. Conduct risk and uncertainty analysis.
10. Document the estimate.
11. Present the estimate to management for approval.
12. Update the estimate to reflect actual costs and changes.

The guide attaches eighteen best practices to the steps,
and its table attaches none to the second step.
It measures the result against four characteristics.
"The four characteristics of a high-quality, reliable cost estimate are that it is comprehensive, well documented, accurate, and credible."
The first of them restates the rule of the work breakdown structure in terms of money.
"A comprehensive estimate includes all possible costs, ensures that no costs were omitted or double-counted, and explains and documents key assumptions."

The two lists agree on the frame.
Both open with what the estimate is for and with a structure for it,
both place ground rules and assumptions before data,
both require documentation and presentation,
and both end with an update.
The auditor's list separates sensitivity analysis from risk analysis,
where NASA's has a single task for cost risk.
That comparison is the author's.

NASA's directive adds a duty of record.
"The program or project shall document the basis of estimate (BOE) for cost estimates and planned schedules in retrievable program or project records."

### The European Cost Estimate Report

The ECSS standard defines the document and not the procedure.
Its cost estimate report has nine sections.

1. Introduction.
2. Applicable and reference documents.
3. Quality of estimate.
4. Hypothesis used.
5. Method and cost model description.
6. Cost breakdown tables.
7. Cost sensitivity analysis.
8. Cost risk assessment.
9. Recommendations.

The fourth section must state eleven hypotheses,
among them the economic conditions,
the currency,
the inclusions and exclusions,
the technology readiness
and the main cost drivers.
The fifth must name the method used for each cost item.
The eighth rests on the risk register,
and it asks for a range.
"The supplier shall provide cost figures associated with confidence level."
The standard also fixes the cost categories into which every estimate and report must fall.
The five main categories are direct manpower,
internal special facilities,
supplies and other direct costs,
subcontracts
and non-production expenses.
Profit is not among them,
since the standard does not count it as an expense of the supplier.

### Life-Cycle Cost

NASA's directive defines the quantity that its baselines commit to.
"Life-Cycle Cost. The total of the direct, indirect, recurring, nonrecurring, and other related expenses both incurred and estimated to be incurred in the design, development, verification, production, deployment, prime mission operation, maintenance, support, and disposal of a project, including closeout, but not extended operations."
The definition adds that the figure includes the cost of the launch vehicle.
Two features deserve notice.
The figure joins costs already incurred to costs still estimated,
so it is part record and part forecast for most of a project's life.
And it includes disposal and closeout but excludes extended operations.

The United States statute on defence acquisition reporting defines a quantity that the author reads as wider.
Its full life-cycle cost
"means all costs of development, procurement, military construction, and operations and support, without regard to funding source or management control."
[United States Code 2026a][usc_10_4351].

The ECSS standard on integrated product support describes life cycle cost analysis in a clause of principles.
The author found no requirement in it that defines a cost record,
and reads the cost records of the European standards as remaining in the cost and schedule standard.

### The Joint Cost and Schedule Confidence Level

NASA does not commit to a point estimate alone.
It commits to a probability.
"Joint Cost and Schedule Confidence Level. The probability that cost will be equal to or less than the targeted cost and schedule will be equal to or less than the targeted schedule date."
The directive abbreviates the term to JCL.
The cost handbook gives the method,
which loads cost and risk onto a summary schedule.
The handbook numbers its steps from zero.

- Step 0 identifies the goals of the analysis.
- Step 1 develops a summary analysis schedule.
- Step 2 loads cost onto the schedule activities.
- Step 3 incorporates the risk list.
- Step 4 conducts an uncertainty analysis.
- Step 5 calculates and views the results.

The requirement applies at KDP C to every single-project programme and to every project whose estimated life-cycle cost exceeds 250 million dollars.
The directive sets two levels.
Mission directorates plan and budget such projects
"based on a 70 percent JCL or as approved by the Decision Authority."
Funding must be consistent with the Management Agreement and
"in no case less than the equivalent of a 50 percent JCL"
unless the decision authority approves otherwise.
A single-project programme,
or a project whose estimated life-cycle cost exceeds 250 million dollars,
must calculate the figure again when NASA rebaselines it.

The budget request shows how the figure reaches the public.
Its table of development cost for each project carries a column for the confidence level,
with a note on what the number means.
"Estimates that include combined cost and schedule risks are denoted as JCL (joint confidence level); all other CLs (confidence levels) reflect cost confidence without necessarily factoring the potential impacts of schedule changes on cost."
[NASA 2026c][agency_nasa_budget_2027].

### The Cost Analysis Data Requirement

NASA also requires each project to leave its cost history behind for later estimators.
The directive defines the cost analysis data requirement as a document in three parts.
The first is a narrative,
the second is technical data in tables,
and the third holds the project's life-cycle cost estimate,
its schedule
and its risk identification.
The cost handbook says that a project prepares the document six times in its life,
at the system requirements review,
the preliminary design review,
the critical design review,
the systems integration review,
launch
and the end of the mission.
The directive gives the purpose,
which is to supply data for the cost and schedule estimates of new projects.

## Earned Value Management

### The Idea and Its Standard

NASA's directive defines the method in one sentence.
"Earned Value Management. A project management approach for measuring and assessing project performance through the integration of technical scope with schedule and cost objectives during the execution of the project."
The governing industry standard is Standard 748 of the Electronic Industries Alliance,
EIA,
which the documents cite as EIA-748,
Earned Value Management Systems.
Its owner sells it,
and the author did not read it.
The Department of Defense restates its thirty-two guidelines in an interpretation guide of 14 March 2019,
which says of itself that it
"is used as the basis for the DoD to assess EVMS compliance with the 32 Guidelines."
[DoD 2019b][mil_evmsig_2019].
EVMS stands for earned value management system.
The guide arranges the guidelines in five categories.

| Category | Guidelines | Subjects, abridged from the guide's short titles |
|---|---|---|
| Organization | 1 to 5 | Authorised work elements, organisational structure, integration of subsidiary processes, overhead management, control accounts |
| Planning, Scheduling, and Budgeting | 6 to 15 | Scheduling, products and milestones, the performance measurement baseline, budgets by cost element, objective measures, level of effort, management reserve and undistributed budget, reconciliation to target cost |
| Accounting Considerations | 16 to 21 | Direct costs, summaries by work breakdown structure and by organisation, indirect costs, unit and lot costs, material cost and quantities |
| Analysis and Management Reporting | 22 to 27 | Schedule and cost variance, significant variances, indirect cost variances, management reporting, corrective actions, estimates at completion |
| Revisions and Data Maintenance | 28 to 32 | Timely changes, reconciliation of baseline and budget, retroactive changes, unauthorised revisions, documented baseline changes |

NASA's implementation handbook of November 2021 reproduces the guidelines,
and its text of guideline 22 shows where the two variances come from
[NASA 2021b][agency_nasa_evm_handbook_2021].
The guideline asks for two comparisons at least monthly.
"Comparison of the amount of planned budget and the amount of budget earned for work accomplished. This comparison provides the schedule variance."
The second follows in the same form.
"Comparison of the amount of the budget earned and the actual (applied where appropriate) direct costs for the same work. This comparison provides the cost variance."

### The Quantities

A project measures three quantities and sets or estimates two.
Each of the three measured quantities has an older name and a newer one,
and the documents use both.
The cost guide of the Government Accountability Office defines the three measured ones in a table that it credits to the Department of Defense.

- **Budgeted cost for work scheduled.**
  The guide abbreviates it to BCWS,
  and the newer name is planned value,
  PV.
  "The sum of the budgets for all work packages and planning packages scheduled to be accomplished within a given time period."
- **Budgeted cost for work performed.**
  The abbreviation is BCWP,
  and the newer name is earned value,
  EV.
  "The sum of the budgets for completed work and completed portions of ongoing work within a given time period."
- **Actual cost of work performed.**
  The abbreviation is ACWP,
  and the newer name is actual cost,
  AC.
  "The costs actually incurred and recorded in the earned value management system for accomplishing the work performed within a given time period."
- **Budget at completion.**
  The abbreviation is BAC.
  "The sum of the estimated budgets for all cost elements."
- **Estimate at completion.**
  The abbreviation is EAC.
  NASA's handbook defines it as
  "A value (expressed in dollars and/or hours) developed to represent a realistic projection of the final cost of a task (or group of tasks) when completed."

The second quantity is the one that gives the method its name.
It is neither a cost nor a date.
It is the budget of the work that the project has done.

### Variances and Indices

No single free document prints every formula.
The defence implementation guide prints none in symbols except the variance at completion.
The cost guide of the Government Accountability Office prints the two indices,
one to-complete index
and one general form of the estimate at completion,
and gives the variances in words.
NASA's handbook prints the two variances and four indices in its glossary.
A one-page reference card of the Department of Energy,
DOE,
dated 10 July 2019,
prints every one in symbols except the to-complete index against the budget
[DOE 2019][doe_evms_gold_card_2019].
The equations below follow those sources,
and the text names the source of each.
The extracted copies flatten subscripts,
so the subscripts shown here restore what the sources print.

Let $\mathrm{CV}$ be the cost variance,
$\mathrm{SV}$ the schedule variance,
$\mathrm{BCWP}$ the budgeted cost for work performed,
$\mathrm{ACWP}$ the actual cost of work performed
and $\mathrm{BCWS}$ the budgeted cost for work scheduled.
NASA's glossary prints the two variances as follows.

$$
\mathrm{CV} = \mathrm{BCWP} - \mathrm{ACWP}
\tag{1}
$$

$$
\mathrm{SV} = \mathrm{BCWP} - \mathrm{BCWS}
\tag{2}
$$

The energy card prints the same pair and also writes each in the newer names,
as earned value less actual cost and as earned value less planned value.
Both variances are in the unit of the budget,
which the defence report gives in both dollars and hours,
including the schedule variance,
which measures the budget of the work that is late and not the number of days.
A positive value is favourable.
The card adds percentage forms,
dividing the cost variance by the earned value and the schedule variance by the planned value.

With $\mathrm{CPI}$ the cost performance index
and $\mathrm{SPI}$ the schedule performance index,
NASA's glossary,
the auditor's guide
and the energy card all print the two ratios in the same form.

$$
\mathrm{CPI} = \mathrm{BCWP} / \mathrm{ACWP}
\tag{3}
$$

$$
\mathrm{SPI} = \mathrm{BCWP} / \mathrm{BCWS}
\tag{4}
$$

The auditor's guide explains the reading.
"A CPI value greater than 1.0 indicates the work accomplished cost less than planned, while a value less than 1.0 indicates the work accomplished cost more than planned."
The schedule index reads the same way.
"An SPI value greater than 1.0 indicates more work was accomplished than planned, while an SPI value less than 1.0 indicates less work was accomplished than planned."

### Estimates at Completion

The estimate at completion has several forms,
and the auditor's cost guide treats the plurality as a virtue.
"EVM data can be used to develop a multitude of EACs, and it is a best practice to develop more than one EAC."
EVM stands for earned value management.

The energy card defines the work remaining first.
Let $\mathrm{WR}$ be the work remaining,
$\mathrm{BAC}$ the budget at completion
and $\mathrm{EV}\_{\mathrm{cum}}$ the cumulative earned value,
which is the cumulative budgeted cost for work performed.

$$
\mathrm{WR} = \mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{EV}_{\mathrm{cum}}
\tag{5}
$$

The card then prints four forms of the estimate.
Let $\mathrm{EAC}$ be the estimate at completion,
$\mathrm{AC}\_{\mathrm{cum}}$ the cumulative actual cost,
$\mathrm{CPI}\_{\mathrm{cum}}$ and $\mathrm{SPI}\_{\mathrm{cum}}$ the two indices computed from cumulative values,
and $\mathrm{CPI}\_{\mathrm{3mo}}$ the cost performance index of the three most recent reporting periods.
The card labels the first form general.

$$
\mathrm{EAC} = \mathrm{BAC} / \mathrm{CPI}_{\mathrm{cum}}
\tag{6}
$$

It labels the second by the index that it uses.

$$
\mathrm{EAC}_{\mathrm{CPIcum}} = \mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{cum}} + \mathrm{WR} / \mathrm{CPI}_{\mathrm{cum}}
\tag{7}
$$

The third it calls composite,
because it divides the work remaining by the product of both indices.

$$
\mathrm{EAC}_{\mathrm{composite}} = \mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{cum}} + \mathrm{WR} / ( \mathrm{CPI}_{\mathrm{cum}} \times \mathrm{SPI}_{\mathrm{cum}} )
\tag{8}
$$

The fourth replaces the cumulative index by the recent one.

$$
\mathrm{EAC}_{\mathrm{CPI3mo}} = \mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{cum}} + \mathrm{WR} / \mathrm{CPI}_{\mathrm{3mo}}
\tag{9}
$$

The card prints an asterisk where the third equation here sets a multiplication sign.
It defines the recent index as the earned value of the last three periods divided by their actual cost.
The first two forms give the same number whenever both use cumulative values,
since the actual cost equals the earned value divided by the cost index.
The card does not say so,
and the remark is the author's algebra.

The auditor's guide prints one general form that contains all of these.
Its terms are the cumulative actual cost of work performed,
the budget at completion,
the cumulative budgeted cost for work performed
and an efficiency index that the estimator chooses.

$$
\mathrm{EAC} = \mathrm{ACWP}\ (\mathrm{cumulative}) + ( \mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{BCWP}\ (\mathrm{cumulative}) ) / \mathrm{efficiency\ index}
\tag{10}
$$

The guide says that the index may be the cost index,
the schedule index
or their product,
and that each may be cumulative,
current
or an average over time.

A last form needs no index at all.
With $\mathrm{ETC}$ the estimate to complete,
the card defines the estimate at completion as the cost to date plus a fresh estimate of the rest.

$$
\mathrm{EAC} = \mathrm{ACWP} + \mathrm{ETC}
\tag{11}
$$

This is the form that a manager's own bottom-up forecast takes.
It corresponds to the estimate of the European standards,
which add forecast expenses to actual expenses and use no index.

With $\mathrm{VAC}$ the variance at completion,
the card and the glossary of the defence implementation guide print the final difference alike
[DoD 2019a][mil_evmig_2019].

$$
\mathrm{VAC} = \mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{EAC}
\tag{12}
$$

A negative value forecasts an overrun.

### The To-Complete Performance Index

The last index looks forward.
NASA's glossary defines it in words before it gives the formulas.
"The future cost efficiency needed to accomplish the remaining work within a financial goal such as the Budget at Completion (BAC) or the Estimate at Completion (EAC)."
The abbreviation is TCPI.
The energy card prints the form that takes the estimate at completion as the goal.

$$
\mathrm{TCPI}_{\mathrm{EAC}} = \mathrm{WR} / ( \mathrm{EAC} - \mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{cum}} )
\tag{13}
$$

The auditor's guide prints the same relation in the older names,
with $\mathrm{BCWR}$ the budgeted cost of work remaining,
which a footnote defines as the budget at completion less the budgeted cost for work performed.

$$
\mathrm{TCPI} = \mathrm{BCWR} / ( \mathrm{EAC} - \mathrm{ACWP} )
\tag{14}
$$

The guide credits its table of indices to the Department of Defense and to the Project Management Institute.
The institute sells its practice standard,
and the author did not read it.
NASA's glossary prints two forms,
one against each goal,
with $\mathrm{BCWP}\_{\mathrm{cum}}$ and $\mathrm{ACWP}\_{\mathrm{cum}}$ the cumulative values.

$$
\mathrm{TCPI}_{\mathrm{BAC}} = ( \mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{BCWP}_{\mathrm{cum}} ) / ( \mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{ACWP}_{\mathrm{cum}} )
\tag{15}
$$

$$
\mathrm{TCPI}_{\mathrm{EAC}} = ( \mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{BCWP}_{\mathrm{cum}} ) / ( \mathrm{EAC} - \mathrm{ACWP}_{\mathrm{cum}} )
\tag{16}
$$

Neither the energy card nor the auditor's guide prints the form against the budget,
although the guide names it in a later table.
The auditor's guide gives the index its use.
"A rule of thumb is that if the TCPI is more than 5 percentage points higher than the CPI, the EAC is too optimistic."

### A Worked Example

Consider a small programme with a budget at completion of 1,000 units of its currency.
At the status date the plan called for work budgeted at 400 units.
The work actually completed carried a budget of 360 units,
and it cost 450 units.

The cost variance is negative 90 units,
and the schedule variance is negative 40 units.
The programme has overspent on the work it did,
and it has done less work than it planned.
The cost performance index is 0.80
and the schedule performance index is 0.90.
Each unit spent has bought 80 hundredths of a unit of planned work.

The work remaining is 640 units.
The general estimate at completion is 1,250 units,
and the form that adds the remaining work to the actual cost gives the same 1,250 units,
as the algebra requires.
The composite form gives about 1,339 units,
because it assumes that the schedule shortfall will also cost money to recover.
Against the general estimate the variance at completion is negative 250 units.

The to-complete index against the budget is about 1.16.
To finish within the original 1,000 units the programme would have to earn 116 hundredths of a unit for every unit it spends from now on,
after earning 80 hundredths so far.
The gap is far wider than the five percentage points of the rule of thumb.
By the reasoning of that rule,
a forecast that the programme will finish on its budget would be too optimistic.
The to-complete index against the general estimate is 0.80,
which equals the cost index,
since that estimate assumes by construction that the efficiency to date continues.

### When It Is Required

NASA's directive sets one threshold for the project as a whole.
Projects and single-project programmes whose life-cycle cost or initial capability cost is
"estimated to be greater than \\$250M shall perform earned value management (EVM) and comply with EIA-748"
for the work done in house and the work under contract alike.
Reporting against the performance measurement baseline begins in Phase B.
Below the threshold the method is optional for in-house work.
The 2021 handbook records the origin of the figure.
"In late 2017, NASA raised the EVM project threshold from \\$20 million (M) to \\$250M Life Cycle Cost (LCC) for in-house work"
Contracts follow NASA's supplement to the federal acquisition regulation whatever the size of the project.
The author did not read the supplement.
The 2021 handbook states the contract thresholds as 20 million dollars for compliance with the standard
and 100 million dollars for a system that the agency or its designee has accepted.
The directive also requires an integrated baseline review wherever it requires earned value.

The defence thresholds appear in the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement,
which the author read as displayed in November 2026 under a change dated 7 May 2026
[DoD 2026][mil_dfars_234_2].
The first threshold is clear.
"For cost or incentive contracts and subcontracts valued at \\$20,000,000 or more, the earned value management system shall comply with the guidelines in the American National Standards Institute/Electronic Industries Alliance Standard 748, Earned Value Management Systems (ANSI/EIA-748)."
Below it the application of the method
"is optional and is a risk-based decision"
and for firm-fixed-price contracts of any value the regulation discourages it.
The second threshold is the one at which the government must formally determine that the contractor's system complies.
The regulation states it as follows.
"For cost or incentive contracts and subcontracts valued at \\$50,000,000 or more, the contractor shall have an earned value management system that has been determined by the cognizant Federal agency to be in compliance with the guidelines in ANSI/EIA-748."
The department's own implementation guide of 18 January 2019 gives a different figure for the same determination.
"Compliance with the Guidelines and an EVMS that has been determined to be acceptable by the Cognizant Federal Agency (CFA) are required for DoD cost reimbursement or incentive contracts and agreements valued at or greater than \\$100M."
The guide adds that its thresholds are in then-year dollars.
The author could not resolve the difference between 50 million and 100 million dollars from the documents read,
and reports both figures as their sources state them.
A reader who must apply the threshold should consult the contracting authority.

### What the European Standards Use Instead

The three ECSS management standards read here contain no earned value management.
The phrase does not occur in the standards on planning,
on cost and schedule
or on risk,
and the publisher's export of the defined terms of all active ECSS standards holds no such term.
The cost and schedule standard names no cost performance index and no schedule performance index.
The author did not search the ECSS handbooks.

The standard does require measurement.
"The supplier shall perform continuous performance measurement of the project."
What it measures is the distance between a baseline and a forecast.
On the side of the schedule the instruments are the milestone trend chart
and the comparison of the current working schedule with the baseline schedule.
On the side of cost there are two.
The first is the deviation between planned and actual cost for each control work package.
The second is the estimate at completion,
which the standard defines together with the estimate to completion.
"The EAC gives an estimate of the total expenses of the project upon its completion."
The second term follows.
"The ETC is a part of the EAC. The ETC is the total expenses estimated for work to be performed from the defined cut-off date and until the work is completed."
The supplier must build the estimate from the facts of the project and not from an index.
The requirement asks for an estimate
"showing commitments and payments, taking account of the schedule, physical progress of the work, including all agreed, submitted or known potential changes."
Physical progress thus enters the European estimate as a judgement,
where earned value turns it into a number.
That contrast is the author's inference.

For a fixed price contract the European estimate at completion is simpler still.
"For Fixed Price contracts, the EAC is based on the agreed milestone payment plans."
The milestone payment plan lists each milestone with its number,
the nature of the payment,
its definition,
its indicative date
and its amount,
and the supplier certifies the achievement of each one.
Payment by milestone is itself a coarse measure of earned value,
though the standard does not describe it so,
and that remark is also the author's.

The standard does print one formula,
for restating a price from the economic conditions of the contract to those of the present.
Let $P$ be the revised amount at current economic conditions,
$P\_{0}$ the basis amount at the reference economic conditions,
$a$,
$b$
and $c$ weighting factors that sum to one,
$S$ and $M$ agreed indices at current conditions,
and $S\_{0}$ and $M\_{0}$ the values of those indices at the reference conditions.

$$
P = P_{0} \times ( a + b \times S / S_{0} + c \times M / M_{0} )
\tag{17}
$$

The standard explains that the indices generally reflect the prices of raw materials and the pay of the labour categories that the project uses.
The standard requires suppliers to express cost information in the economic conditions that the customer specifies.

## Risk

### Definitions

Each regime defines risk in a sentence,
and the three sentences differ in what a risk threatens.
NASA's directive on risk management,
NASA Procedural Requirements 8000.4C,
took effect on 19 April 2022
[NASA 2022a][agency_nasa_npr_8000_4c].
It ties risk to objectives.
"Risk. The potential for shortfalls with respect to achieving explicitly established and stated objectives."
The directive characterises a risk operationally as a set of triplets,
each made of a scenario,
its likelihood
and its consequence.
The defence guide on the subject,
dated September 2023 and read with its change 2.2,
ties risk to three kinds of programme objective
[DoD 2023a][mil_rio_guide_2023].
"A risk is a potential future event or condition that may have a negative effect on achieving program objectives for cost, schedule, and performance."
The ECSS glossary ties it to the project.
A risk is an
"undesirable situation or circumstance that has both a likelihood of occurring and a potential negative consequence on a project"
[ECSS 2026][ecss_definitions_2026].

The defence guide separates a risk from two neighbours.
"An issue is an event or condition with negative effect that has occurred (such as a realized risk) or is certain to occur (likelihood of 5) that should be addressed."
The second neighbour looks the other way.
"An opportunity offers potential future benefits to the program's cost, schedule, or performance baseline."
The guide allows a programme to keep the three in one register.

### The Risk Statement

NASA recommends a sentence form.
The directive gives it in a note,
which attributes the form to NASA's risk management handbook of 2011.
"Given that [CONDITION], there is a possibility of [DEPARTURE] from the baseline adversely impacting [ASSET], thereby leading to [CONSEQUENCE]."
The second version of NASA's risk management handbook,
dated November 2024,
adds a fifth slot for the objective
[NASA 2024c][agency_nasa_rm_handbook_2024].
"Given that [Condition], there is a possibility of [Departure] affecting [Asset], which can result in [Consequence], adversely affecting the achievement of [Affected Objective(s)]"
The handbook defines the slots.
The condition is a present fact that causes concern.
The departure is a possible change from the baseline plan.
The asset is an element of the organisation's portfolio,
which the handbook compares to an element of a work breakdown structure.
The consequence is the undesired state of the asset.
The same note adds a narrative to the sentence.
"Each risk statement or scenario is accompanied by a descriptive narrative, which captures the context of the risk by describing the circumstances, contributing factors, uncertainty, range of possible consequences, and related issues (such as what, where, when, how, and why)."

The other two regimes are looser.

### Scales and the Matrix

The three regimes differ most on scales,
and a reader should know which of the published scales bind anyone.

**Defence.**
The defence guide prints a five-level scale of likelihood under the title of typical criteria.

| Level | Name | Probability of occurrence |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Near Certainty | More than 80 per cent and up to 99 per cent |
| 4 | Highly Likely | More than 60 per cent and up to 80 per cent |
| 3 | Likely | More than 40 per cent and up to 60 per cent |
| 2 | Low Likelihood | More than 20 per cent and up to 40 per cent |
| 1 | Not Likely | More than 1 per cent and up to 20 per cent |

Its scale of consequence has five levels,
named critical,
significant,
moderate,
minor
and minimal impact,
with separate criteria for cost,
schedule
and performance.
The guide labels that table a sample.
Its cost criteria for the three highest levels are increases of 10 per cent or more,
of 5 to less than 10 per cent
and of 1 to less than 5 per cent over the acquisition programme baseline.
The worst of the three scores governs.
"The assessment should capture the greatest anticipated impact in any area as if the risk were fully realized, that is, without further risk reduction or mitigation activities."
A matrix then turns the two numbers into a level.
"This matrix converts the combination of likelihood and the maximum of the cost, schedule, and performance consequence scores to form a risk level for each risk: low (green); moderate (yellow); or high (red)."
The matrix is a colour figure with no text layer.
The author read its cells from the image,
so the table below is a reading and not a quotation.

| Likelihood level | Consequence levels rated moderate | Consequence levels rated high |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 2 | 3, 4 and 5 |
| 4 | 2 and 3 | 4 and 5 |
| 3 | 3 and 4 | 5 |
| 2 | 4 and 5 | None |
| 1 | 5 | None |

Every other cell is low.
On that reading 6 cells are high,
8 are moderate
and 11 are low.
The guide also prices a risk.
"The cost exposure or risk-weighted consequence of a risk can be expressed as its EMV, which is the likelihood of the risk multiplied by the cost consequence of the risk if realized."
EMV stands for expected monetary value.

**Europe.**
Every scale in the ECSS standard is an example.
The standard requires the project to state its scales in a risk management policy document,
and it offers its own figures as a proposal.
"In the examples, five categories are used for illustration only; more or fewer categories or designations are also possible."
The example scale of severity runs from 1 to 5 under the names negligible,
significant,
major,
critical
and catastrophic,
and the figure leaves its cost thresholds for the project to determine.
The example scale of likelihood runs from A to E under the names minimum,
low,
medium,
high
and maximum,
each with a frequency.
"B Low Will seldom occur, about 1 in 1000 projects"
The standard defines the risk index as a combination of the two scores,
and in the example the index is a pair such as E4 and not a product.
The example then sorts the 25 pairs into five magnitudes.
Three pairs are very high,
3 are high,
4 are medium,
8 are low
and 7 are very low.
The example marks the first three magnitudes as unacceptable and the last two as acceptable.
The author read the shading of the printed figure from the page image.
Red covers very high and high,
yellow covers medium and low,
and green covers very low.
Yellow therefore spans one unacceptable magnitude and one acceptable one,

**NASA.**
No NASA agency document that the author read prints a standard matrix with scales.
The directive leaves the categories to each project's risk management plan,
which defines categories of likelihood and of consequence severity where the project needs them.
The 2024 handbook argues against the traditional use of the matrix.
It says that the practice of placing each risk scenario into a cell of such a matrix
"purely on the basis of mostly qualitative assessment criteria, can no longer be considered valid."
It still allows the display.

"such a risk can, if so desired for any practical reasons, still be displayed and communicated by means of a 5x5 matrix."
The author did not obtain the standards of NASA's individual centres

### Dispositions

NASA's directive requires a decision on every risk and allows exactly one.
"i. Only one of the following possible risk dispositions is applied to any given risk."

| Disposition | What the risk database must then hold |
|---|---|
| Accept | The rationale, the assumptions, the acceptance criteria and the interval at which someone reviews the assumptions |
| Mitigate | A mitigation plan with contingency planning, and the parameters that will show whether the mitigation works |
| Close | The rationale for closure and the approval of management |
| Watch | Tracking requirements, with triggering events, decision points, dates or milestones |
| Research | The effort to obtain more information, documented and tracked |
| Elevate | The action taken, when none of the five applies and the decision passes to the next higher level |

For the dispositions of mitigate,
watch
and research the directive requires that
"an entity is designated to implement the disposition."

The defence guide names four options,
"the four risk mitigation options: accept, avoid, transfer, or control"
and the ECSS standard two,
to accept or to reduce.
ECSS adds a rule about who may accept.
"Risks are formally accepted by the next higher level responsibility within the customer/supplier chain."
The ECSS process as a whole is normative.
It has four steps and nine tasks,
which run from defining the policy and the plan,
through identifying and assessing the risks
and deciding and acting on them,
to monitoring,
communicating
and submitting the remaining risks for acceptance.

### The Agency Financial Report

A United States agency publishes its accounts each year in a form that the Office of Management and Budget,

[OMB 2026][omb_a136_2026].
The Circular defines the set of statements.
In its words the term financial statements refers
"to the balance sheet (Balance Sheet), statement of net cost (SNC), statement of changes in net position (SCNP), statement of budgetary resources (SBR), statement of custodial activity (SCA), statement of social insurance (SOSI), statement of changes in social insurance amounts, and the accompanying notes."
The last three apply to agencies that collect revenue for others or run social insurance,
and a space agency publishes the first four.

NASA's report for fiscal year 2025 carries a transmittal dated 16 January 2026
[NASA 2026b][agency_nasa_afr_2025].

and other information.
The financial section holds four principal statements,
and the report describes each.

- **The balance sheet.**
  "Consolidated Balance Sheet provides information on assets, liabilities, and net position as of the end of the reporting period."
- **The statement of net cost.**
  "Consolidated Statement of Net Cost reports net cost of operations during the reporting period by theme and at the entity level."
- **The statement of changes in net position.**
  "Consolidated Statement of Changes in Net Position reports the beginning balances of net position, current financing sources and use of resources, unexpended resources for the reporting period, and ending net position for the current reporting period."
- **The statement of budgetary resources.**
  "Combined Statement of Budgetary Resources reports information on the sources and status of budgetary resources for the reporting period."

The statement of net cost is the one that states what the agency's work cost.

"It is a measure of gross costs of operations less earned revenue, and represents the cost to taxpayers for achieving each theme at the entity level."
NASA reports four themes,
which it names discover,
explore,
innovate
and advance.
For fiscal year 2025 the statement shows gross costs of 25,599 million dollars,

and a net cost of 23,699 million.

NASA's chosen level is the theme.
The statement therefore gives no cost for any single mission,
and the Circular defines no cost per mission and no cost per launch.

The statement of budgetary resources is the budget against actual statement of the federal system,
with a qualification.
The Circular describes it.
"The SBR and related disclosures provide information about how budgetary resources were made available during the period and their status at the end of the period."
Its form follows the government's report on budget execution,
and the Circular's illustration has three blocks.

| Block | Leading lines | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Budgetary Resources | Unobligated balance from prior year budget authority, appropriations, spending authority from offsetting collections, total budgetary resources | What the agency had available to commit |
| Status of Budgetary Resources | New obligations and upward adjustments, unobligated balance at end of year, total budgetary resources | What the agency committed and what remains |
| Outlays, Net and Disbursements, Net | Outlays, distributed offsetting receipts, agency outlays | What the agency paid out |

The first two blocks must balance.
"The total amount shown for the status of budgetary resources must equal total budgetary resources in the section above."
NASA's statement for fiscal year 2025 shows total budgetary resources of 41,460 million dollars,
new obligations and upward adjustments of 28,773 million
and net agency outlays of 24,576 million.
The qualification is this.
The statement sets resources against their use.
It has no column headed budget beside a column headed actual,
it does not arrange its figures by mission,
and it measures obligations and outlays and not the accrued cost of work.
That reading of the Circular is the author's.
The two views meet in the notes.
One note of NASA's report reconciles the statement to the actual amounts in the President's Budget,
and another reconciles net cost to net outlays.

### Unit Cost Reporting to the Legislature

United States defence law defines cost per unit by statute.
Section 4351 of Title 10 of the United States Code defines two unit costs for a major defence acquisition programme
[United States Code 2026a][usc_10_4351].
The programme acquisition unit cost is the total cost of development,
procurement
and system-specific military construction,
divided by the number of fully configured end items that the programme is to produce.
The procurement unit cost is the total of all funds programmed to be available for obligation for procurement,
divided by the number of fully configured end items that the programme is to procure.
The statute's own term is procurement unit cost,
without the word average that departmental usage adds.

The same section required periodic reports under a fixed name.
"Reports under this section shall be known as Selected Acquisition Reports."
The quarterly report had to state the quantity of items,
the programme acquisition cost,
both unit costs,
the procurement cost,
the reasons for any change in cost or schedule since the previous report,
the major contracts with the reasons for their variances
and the highlights of the programme.
The comprehensive annual report added the baseline estimates,
the risks,
a history
and a full life-cycle cost analysis.
The article uses the past tense because the section ends with a termination clause.
"The requirements under this section shall terminate after the final submission covering fiscal year 2023."
The clause stood in the code as the author read it in November 2026.
On the face of the statute the duty to submit these reports has therefore lapsed,
while the definitions in the section remain in use by the chapter that follows it.
The author did not investigate what reporting has replaced it.

The following chapter turns the unit costs into thresholds
[United States Code 2026b][usc_10_4371].
Growth in either unit cost of at least 15 per cent over the current baseline estimate,
or at least 30 per cent over the original baseline estimate,
is significant.
Growth of at least 25 per cent over the current estimate,
or at least 50 per cent over the original,
is critical.
The statute states the percentages in constant base year dollars.
After a critical breach the statute presumes termination unless the Secretary certifies the programme to Congress.

**The audit figure.**
The Office of Inspector General,
OIG,
of NASA audited the management of the Artemis missions and published its report on 15 November 2021
[NASA OIG 2021][agency_nasa_oig_ig_22_003].
"We also project the current production and operations cost of a single SLS/Orion system at \\$4.1 billion per launch for Artemis I through IV"
SLS stands for Space Launch System.
The report draws the boundary of the figure with care.
"The \\$4.1 billion total cost represents production of the rocket and the operations needed to launch the SLS/Orion system including materials, labor, facilities, and overhead, but does not include any money spent either on prior development of the system"
A footnote gives the components.
They are 1 billion dollars for the Orion capsule,
300 million for the service module that the European Space Agency supplies,
which the report values through a barter agreement,
2.2 billion for the rocket
and 568 million for the ground systems costs of the launch.
The four components sum to 4,068 million dollars,
which is consistent with the report's figure of 4.1 billion.
The figure is a projection by an auditor and not a line in NASA's accounts.
One of its components is the value of a barter and not a payment.

**The life-cycle cost.**
The Government Accountability Office reports the cost of the same Orion spacecraft as a different quantity.
Its table gives an original baseline life-cycle cost estimate of 11,283.5 million dollars and a current estimate of 14,756.0 million,
with the key schedule milestone moved from April 2023 to April 2026.
A second table gives the estimate at the latest approved rebaseline,
of August 2021,
as 13,811.0 million.
The report states that the Orion programme completed its baseline for its initial capability demonstration with Artemis II.
The auditor's baselines generally include formulation,
development
and operations.
These figures are therefore not a cost of any one flight.
For NEO Surveyor the same report shows 1,595.1 million dollars in both columns.

Space Exploration Technologies Corp.,

The copy that the author read is an archive capture of May 2025,

The sheet does not say which services the price includes,

The figure is out of date and the article does not rely on its last digits.

**The award value.**
NASA announces the value of each launch service contract that it awards.
The release of 2 July 2024 for a space telescope mission on a Falcon 9 reads as follows

"The firm-fixed-price contract has a value of approximately \\$69 million, which includes launch services and other mission related costs."
The release of 25 November 2024 for the Dragonfly mission on a Falcon Heavy uses the same sentence with a value of approximately 256.6 million dollars
[NASA 2024e][agency_nasa_release_c24_042].
Each figure is a price to one customer for one mission.
It is not the provider's cost,
and neither release says what the other mission related costs are.

**The company metric.**
Rocket Lab Corporation defines a cost per launch among the key metrics of its annual report
[Rocket Lab 2026][rocketlab_10k_fy2025].
"Cost per launch is calculated by taking actual costs of the launch vehicles that occur in the period, regardless of whether the costs were recognized at point-in-time or over-time method and all period costs in the period of launch."
The report states the result.
"For the years ended December 31, 2025, 2024 and 2023, our revenue per launch was \\$8.5 million, \\$7.8 million and \\$7.1 million, respectively. Meanwhile, cost per launch was \\$4.8 million, \\$5.7 million and \\$7.0 million for the years ended December 31, 2025, 2024 and 2023, respectively."
The definition does not say whether research and development expense or selling,
general
and administrative expense enters the figure.

Two further quantities sit near these five.
The first is a cost per kilogram.
An audit of commercial resupply of the International Space Station,
dated 26 April 2018,
prints a row headed cost per kilogram with the values 63,200 dollars,
marked estimated,
for the first round of contracts
and 71,800 dollars,
marked projected,
for the second
[NASA OIG 2018][agency_nasa_oig_ig_18_016].
The denominator is the cargo capability to the station under those contracts.
The second is the statutory unit cost of the previous section,
which divides development and procurement by a number of end items.

The five quantities differ in at least four respects.
They differ in whose money they count,
since a price is a customer's cost and not a provider's.
They differ in whether development is inside the figure.
They differ in whether they record a past amount or project a future one.
And they differ in the denominator,
which may be a launch,
a mission,
a kilogram
or an end item.
A figure for cost per mission is therefore meaningful only with its definition attached.
That conclusion is the author's.

## A Commercial Launch Company's Reporting

A company reports to investors and not to an appropriating legislature,
and its statements answer different questions.
The example here is the annual report on Form 10-K of Rocket Lab Corporation for the fiscal year ended 31 December 2025.
The author read an archived copy from the company's investor site.
The article describes the form of the report and draws no conclusion about the company.

The report contains four consolidated statements,
namely balance sheets,
statements of operations and comprehensive loss,
statements of changes in stockholders' equity
and statements of cash flows.
The statement of operations sets revenues against cost of revenues to give gross profit,
and then deducts operating expenses.
The company defines the cost side.
"Cost of revenues consists primarily of direct material and labor costs, manufacturing overhead, freight expense, depreciation and amortization and other personnel-related expenses, which include salaries, bonuses, benefits and stock-based compensation expense, directly associated with generating revenues."

The company reports by segment.
"The Company has determined that it operates in two reportable segments: launch services and space systems."
Each segment shows revenues,
cost of revenues
and gross profit,
and the report names the measure by which management judges a segment.
"The CODM uses gross profit as the measure of segment profit or loss to assess performance and allocate resources."
CODM stands for chief operating decision maker.

Revenue on long contracts follows the cost.
"Over-time revenue recognition is generally based on an input measure of progress based on costs incurred compared to estimated total costs at completion."
The measure resembles the percentage complete of earned value management,
with the difference that it recognises revenue and does not price work at its budget.
That comparison is the author's.

The report defines backlog twice.
The management discussion defines it as future revenue.
"Backlog represents future revenues that we would recognize in connection with the completion of all contracts and purchase orders that have been entered into by our customers but have not yet been fulfilled, excluding any customer options for future products or services that have not yet been exercised."
The notes to the statements define it by transaction price.
"The Company's backlog represents the estimated transaction prices on performance obligations to the Company's customers for which work remains to be performed."
The report adds that customers may typically terminate contracts for launch services on notice and on payment of a fee,
which bears on how firm the figure is.

The per-launch measures are management metrics.
The report states a count.
"We launched ten Electron vehicles in 2023, 16 Electron vehicles in 2024 and 21 Electron vehicles in 2025."
It defines revenue per launch as the average transaction price of the launch contracts in the period in which the launch occurs,
and cost per launch as the previous section quotes it.
Both appear in the management discussion and neither is a line of the audited statements.

The contrast with the agency's reports is one of purpose,
as the author reads the two.
The agency's statement of budgetary resources supports the control of spending against appropriations.
The company's statements set revenue against cost.
Neither says what one mission cost without a further definition.

## The Launch Manifest

A manifest is a schedule of flights,
and the documents read here establish less about its form than about any other record in this survey.

NASA's budget request defines the term in its glossary.
The manifest of the agency's Flight Planning Board is a list that NASA publishes periodically
and that includes the expected launch dates of NASA missions.
The glossary stresses that the list moves.
"A launch manifest is a dynamic schedule that is affected by real world operational activities conducted by NASA and multiple other entities."
It warns that the dates are of two kinds.
Missions launching within about six months carry range dates that the glossary calls confirmed,
and missions beyond six months carry contractual or planning dates.
It also explains that the manifest date is normally earlier than the date in the Agency Baseline Commitment,
which leaves room for operational delays outside the project's control.
A NASA policy directive on orbital space transportation services gives the board its chair,
the Associate Administrator for the Space Operations Mission Directorate,
and has the board assess proposals to fly two or more payloads together
[NASA 2015b][agency_nasa_npd_8610_12h].

The author could not verify the fields of the board's manifest.
The author found neither the board's charter nor the manifest itself in public form,
and no NASA source that lists its columns.
What the author could verify is the public launch schedule on NASA's website
[NASA 2026a][agency_nasa_launch_schedule].
Each entry there carries a date expression and a mission name.
The date expression takes several forms,
which in November 2026 included a date and time preceded by the words no earlier than,
a bare year
and the words under review.
The page for a single event adds a time zone and a paragraph of description.
The auditor's project profiles add the launch location and the launch vehicle.

The European side is thinner still.
In November 2026 the website of Arianespace showed no table of forthcoming launches.
It announced its next flight in a press release of 14 October 2026
[Arianespace 2026][arianespace_va271_2026].
"The target date for the launch is no earlier than November 23, and a final launch window will be set closer to the mission."
That one release carries a flight designator,
the customer's mission name,
the launcher and its configuration,
the number of payloads,
the launch site,
the date in the form no earlier than,
the orbit,
the duration of the mission
and running counts of the operator's launches.
The author found no forward launch schedule on the website of the European Space Agency.

A manifest record therefore holds,
at the least,
a mission name,
a planned date,
a statement of how firm the date is,
a vehicle
and a site.
That list is an inference from a glossary,
a policy directive
and public pages,
and no document read here prescribes it.

## Lessons Learned

Lessons learned are part of programme management in NASA,
and this article does no more than name the policy that governs them.
The policy is NASA Policy Directive 7120.6A,
Knowledge Policy for Programs and Projects,
effective 16 December 2019
[NASA 2019][agency_nasa_npd_7120_6a].
The Project Plan requires a knowledge management plan that covers the capture of lessons learned throughout the life cycle,
and the directive on programmes and projects defines a lesson learned as
"Captured knowledge or understanding gained through experience which, if shared, would benefit the work of others."

## What the Practices Share

The regimes differ in vocabulary and agree in structure.
Thirteen elements appear in most of them.
The list is the author's synthesis,
and each item names the sources behind it.

1. **Write a plan that accounts for every section, and have it approved.**
   NASA's templates require each section and a rationale for any that does not apply.
   The ECSS plan has eleven fixed sections,
   and the customer approves it.
2. **Fix a baseline, and change it only by a controlled act.**
   NASA rebaselines its commitment only on stated criteria.
   ECSS reaches a current baseline cost plan only by adding approved changes to the original.
   NASA's schedule handbook keeps the schedule baseline under change control,
   and the defence performance report requires an analysis of baseline changes.
3. **Divide all the work in one tree, and count each piece once.**
   NASA's handbook,
   the defence standard
   and the ECSS planning standard each state the rule,
   and only the defence standard calls it the 100 per cent rule.
   The auditor's cost guide states the same rule for money.
4. **Give each element of the tree a written definition.**
   NASA's dictionary has eight items for each element.
   The ECSS work package description has eighteen,
   including the tasks that the package excludes.
5. **Build the schedule as a network with logic and a critical path.**
   NASA's handbook,
   the auditor's ten practices,
   the defence guide
   and the ECSS schedule definition all call for activities,
   links
   and a critical path.
   Only the ECSS annex states the call as a requirement.
   The others are guidance.
6. **Keep the baseline schedule apart from the current one.**
   NASA distinguishes the baseline from the current schedule.
   ECSS distinguishes the baseline schedule from the working schedule
   and says that the second need not reflect an agreed status.
   The auditor's tenth practice is the maintenance of a baseline schedule.
7. **Document the estimate with its assumptions and its uncertainty.**
   NASA's twelve tasks and the auditor's twelve steps both include ground rules,
   risk
   and documentation.
   The ECSS report requires the hypotheses and a confidence level.
   NASA commits at a stated joint confidence level.
8. **Report cost on the same tree, and reconcile it with the books.**
   NASA's Project Plan breaks funding down to the second level of the tree,
   and its earned value system reconciles actual costs with the financial system each month.
   ECSS requires that claimed expenditures be reconcilable with the supplier's accounts.
   The defence report requires each control account to trace to one element of the tree.
9. **Forecast the final cost again at every report.**
   The estimate at completion appears in NASA's handbook,
   the auditor's guide,
   the energy card,
   the defence performance report
   and the ECSS standard.
   The auditor's guide and the defence report both ask for more than one.
10. **Explain each variance beyond a threshold by cause, effect and remedy.**
    NASA's form asks for the problem,
    the impact
    and the corrective action.
    The defence report asks for the root cause,
    the impact
    and the mitigation plan.
    The ECSS schedule progress report asks for the justification of deviations and for remedy actions.
11. **Record each risk with a statement, two scores, a decision and an owner.**
    NASA recommends a form for the statement and sets the content that each disposition requires.
    The defence guide and the ECSS standard supply example registers.
12. **Report periodically, with an assessment or overview first.**
    The ECSS progress report opens with the manager's assessment.
    The defence executive summary opens with an overview of the contract and gives the manager's forecast at its end.
    NASA and defence practice report monthly,
    and ECSS leaves the period to the customer.
13. **Keep public accounts and programme performance reports as separate documents.**
    NASA's financial report states cost by theme.
    The cost of a project against its baseline appears in the budget request and in the auditor's assessment.
    The European agency's annual report shows budget and accrued cost for the agency and not for a project.

The regimes differ on points that a designer of records must decide.

| Regime | Measure of cost and schedule performance | How the baseline changes |
|---|---|---|
| NASA directive and handbooks | Earned value above 250 million dollars of life-cycle cost, with schedule status, progress and forecast reports | Replan below a threshold, rebaseline at 30 per cent development cost growth or on two stated judgements |
| United States defence contracts | Earned value on cost or incentive contracts from 20 million dollars, reported monthly | Baseline changes reported and analysed each month, and unit cost breaches reported under statute |
| ECSS management standards | Planned against actual cost per control work package, milestone trends and a supplier's estimate at completion | Contract change notice agreed by customer and supplier |
| European Space Agency optional programmes | Not established from the documents read | Right of withdrawal above a 20 per cent overrun of the financial envelope |

Four choices follow from the differences.

- **Earned value or baseline against forecast.**
  Earned value yields indices and a formula-driven forecast,
  and it demands a budget for every piece of work and an objective measure of completion.
  The European method needs only a baseline,
  actual figures
  and an honest forecast,
  and it yields no index.
- **A fixed top of the tree or a derived one.**
  NASA dictates the second level,
  the defence standard supplies a template for each kind of system,
  and ECSS derives the tree from the product tree of each project.
- **Fixed scales of risk or declared ones.**
  The defence guide prints typical numerical criteria.
  ECSS requires a project to declare its scales and offers examples.
  NASA's current handbook warns against relying on the matrix as the assessment.
- **What a cost per mission includes.**
  No document read here settles it,
  and the five examples of the earlier section settle it five ways.

The characterisations in the table and in the list of choices are the author's.

## Structures for a Small Programme

A small programme cannot run a validated earned value system or staff a monthly review board.
It can still keep the records,
because their structure costs little and the standards themselves scale down.
NASA makes earned value optional below its threshold for in-house work,
though not for contracts,
the defence regulation makes it a risk-based decision below its own,
and ECSS leaves the period and the level of detail of reporting to agreement.
The structures below are the author's consolidation for a programme of that size,
such as a campaign of simulated flights.
Each part names the document it comes from,
and the text marks each part that comes from none.

### The Programme Status Report

| Part | Content | After |
|---|---|---|
| Identification | Programme name, report number, reporting period with start and finish, status date, currency and price basis, author | ECSS cost and manpower report header, defence performance report |
| Manager's assessment | The current situation against forecasts and risks, in a few sentences | ECSS progress report item 1 |
| Baseline in force | The versions of the work breakdown structure, the baseline schedule and the budget, with each change approved since the last report | NASA Project Plan part 2.0, ECSS baseline cost plan, defence detailed analysis |
| Milestones | For each key milestone the baseline date, the current forecast or actual date, the variance and the margin that remains | NASA milestone status and variance reports, ECSS schedule progress report, auditor's project profile |
| Cost and earned value | For the period and cumulatively the planned value, earned value and actual cost, the two variances and the two indices, then the budget at completion, the estimate at completion, the variance at completion and the to-complete index | NASA earned value system description, energy card |
| Variance explanations | For each variance beyond the programme's threshold the cause, the impact and the corrective action | NASA variance form, defence detailed analysis |
| Technical performance | The agreed measures with their trend | ECSS progress report item 3, defence executive summary |
| Risks | The leading risks, each with its statement, likelihood, consequence, disposition and owner, and the changes since the last report | NASA risk directive, defence register, ECSS register example |
| Actions | Each open action with its identifier, origin, description, responsible person, close-out date and status | ECSS action attributes |
| Flights | The flights completed in the period and those forthcoming, each with its planned date and how firm the date is | NASA budget glossary and public schedule, as an inference |
| Forecast | The most likely, best case and worst case estimate at completion and completion date | Defence executive summary |

Five rules of use go with the table.

- **Status and analysis stay apart.**
  The milestone and cost parts give figures.
  The variance part explains them.
  NASA's schedule handbook draws the same line between status reporting and progress reporting.
- **Every figure is traceable to a record.**
  The actual cost in the report equals the ledger for the same period,
  after the ECSS rule of reconcilability and NASA's monthly reconciliation.
- **The baseline changes only by a recorded decision.**
  The report lists each change with its reason,
  after the ECSS contract change notice.
  A small programme should also state in advance the growth at which it will replan,
  after NASA's thresholds.
  The values of such thresholds are the programme's own choice,
  and no figure from a programme of billions transfers to a small one.
- **More than one estimate at completion appears.**
  The report shows the index-based forms and the manager's own estimate together,
  after the auditor's guide.
- **An unknown stays marked as unknown.**
  The report states a date under review or a cost not yet estimated in those words,
  as NASA's public schedule does with a date.
  This rule is the author's addition.

The part on flights is the weakest in its sourcing.
Its fields follow the author's inference about a manifest record,
and the three grades of firmness that a programme might use,
such as confirmed,
planned
and under review,
are the author's suggestion and come from no standard.

### The Financial Records

| Record | Content | After |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | The original budget, each approved change and the current budget, by element of the work breakdown structure and by period | ECSS baseline cost plans, NASA Project Plan section 2.4 |
| Ledger of actual costs | Each cost with its date, its element of the tree and its cost category | ECSS cost breakdown structure, accounting guidelines of earned value |
| Budget against actual statement | For each element, for the period and cumulatively, the planned cost, the actual cost and the deviation, with totals | ECSS cost and manpower report |
| Statement of resources | Resources available, resources committed, resources not yet committed and amounts paid, with the first equal to the sum of the second and third | Statement of budgetary resources under Circular A-136 |
| Statement of net cost | Gross cost less any revenue, by major element as the programme defines it | Statement of net cost under Circular A-136 |
| Estimate at completion | Issue date, cut-off date, currency, price basis and applicable schedule, then by element the actual cost to date, the estimate to complete and the estimate at completion, with the method of each estimate and the variance at completion | ECSS estimate at completion, energy card, auditor's guide |
| Cost per mission record | The stated definition, the costs included and excluded, the denominator, the period and the resulting figure | Inspector general's report, statutory unit cost, company metric |

The budget against actual statement and the statement of resources answer different questions,
and a programme that wants both should keep both.
The first compares a plan of cost with the cost incurred,
element by element.
The second shows what the programme had available,
what it committed
and what it paid.
The federal statement balances by rule,
and the small version should balance in the same way.

The cost per mission record needs the most care,
because the standards leave the quantity undefined.
The examples in this survey suggest what a definition must settle.

- **Whose cost.**
  The record states whether the figure is the programme's own cost or a price paid to a supplier.
- **Which phases.**
  The record states whether development is inside the figure,
  as in a life-cycle cost,
  or outside it,
  as in the inspector general's figure for production and operations.
- **Which overheads.**
  The record states whether facilities,
  management
  and other shared costs fall to the mission and by what rule.
- **Which denominator.**
  The record states whether the unit is a launch,
  a mission
  or a unit of payload mass.
- **Record or projection.**
  The record states whether the figure is an amount incurred or an estimate,
  and at what date.

A programme may reasonably keep two such records,
one for the recurring cost of a flight
and one for the life-cycle cost of the programme divided by its flights.
The two should not share a name.
The list of five points and the advice to keep two records are the author's.

### A Simulated Currency

A programme that runs in a simulation spends the simulation's own currency.
No document read for this article addresses such a currency,
and the author expected none to.
The structure carries over without change,
because none of it depends on what the unit of account is.
The work breakdown structure,
the baseline,
the three measured quantities of earned value,
the estimate at completion
and the two statements are relations among amounts in one unit.

Three points need attention.
The first is the price basis.
Several ECSS cost reports must name their currency and their economic conditions in the header,
namely the company price breakdown forms,
the milestone payment plan,
the cost and manpower report
and the estimate at completion.
The author takes the reason to be that prices move.
In a simulation prices move when the simulation changes its price tables or its rules,
so the report should name the version of the simulation or of the price data to which its figures refer.
The second is the boundary of cost.
A simulated programme should say whether its costs include only what the simulation charges
or also the effort of the people who run it,
which the simulation does not price.
The third is comparison.
A reader cannot compare a figure in a simulated currency with a figure in dollars or euro,
and a report should not suggest otherwise.
These three points are the author's additions and come from no standard.

## Conclusion

Space agencies and the aerospace industry keep a small set of records about their programmes and their money,
under vocabularies that do not match.
NASA requires a Program Plan and a Project Plan with every section accounted for,
two nested baselines,
a work breakdown structure whose second level it dictates,
an integrated master schedule
and a commitment at a stated joint confidence level.
United States defence practice adds a template of the tree for each kind of system,
a monthly performance report from the contractor
and unit costs defined by statute.
The European standards arrange the same records around a contract between a customer and a supplier,
and they change a baseline only by an agreed change.

The practices part company on measurement.
United States practice computes earned value,
with two variances,
two indices,
several estimates at completion
and an index of the efficiency still required.
The three European management standards read here contain none of this.
They set planned against actual cost for each control work package,
watch the trend of each milestone
and ask the supplier for an estimate at completion.
A small programme can use either method,
and the formulas of the first cost little once the programme has written down the budget of each piece of work.

The public reports answer other questions.
An agency's financial statements show resources and net cost at the level of the agency or its themes.
A project's cost against its baseline appears in the budget request and in the auditor's assessment.
A company's report shows revenue against cost by segment.
None of them yields a cost per mission without a definition,
and the primary examples show at least five definitions in use.
A programme that states which one it means,
keeps its baseline under control
and explains its variances has adopted the substance of the professional practice,
whatever its size and whatever its currency.
