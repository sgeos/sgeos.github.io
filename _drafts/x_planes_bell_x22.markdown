The [Bell X-22A][ref_x22] had four propellers, each inside a tilting duct, two forward and two aft. It first flew on 17 March 1966, one of the two aircraft was destroyed five months later, and the survivor went on flying until October 1984. **Eighteen years is longer than any other aircraft in this series managed**, and it is longer than the X-1, the X-2 and the X-15 put together. This article is the twenty-third in the [X-Planes series][related_post_a297_xplanes_framing], following the [X-1][related_post_a298_bell_x1], the [X-2][related_post_a299_bell_x2], the [X-3][related_post_a300_douglas_x3], the [X-4][related_post_a301_northrop_x4], the [X-5][related_post_a302_bell_x5], the [X-6][related_post_a303_convair_x6], the [X-7][related_post_a304_lockheed_x7], the [X-8][related_post_a305_aerojet_x8], the [X-9][related_post_a306_bell_x9], the [X-10][related_post_a307_north_american_x10], the [X-11][related_post_a308_convair_x11], the [X-12][related_post_a309_convair_x12], the [X-13][related_post_a310_ryan_x13], the [X-14][related_post_a311_bell_x14], the [X-15][related_post_a312_north_american_x15], the [X-16][related_post_a313_bell_x16], the [X-17][related_post_a314_lockheed_x17], the [X-18][related_post_a315_hiller_x18], the [X-19][related_post_a316_curtiss_wright_x19], the [X-20][related_post_a317_boeing_x20], and the [X-21][related_post_a318_northrop_x21].

**It did not spend those eighteen years being an X-22.** From 1968 it carried a variable-stability system that let it imitate the handling of other aircraft in flight, and what it produced was not data about four-ducted-propeller aeroplanes but data about how any vertical-takeoff aircraft ought to handle. **The most successful vertical-takeoff research aircraft of the period succeeded by ceasing to be a prototype and becoming an instrument.**

The standard inventory entry is [Jenkins Landis and Miller 2003 American X-Vehicles, An Inventory X-1 to X-50][book_jenkins_landis_miller_2003] and the vehicle compilation is [Miller 2001 The X-Planes, X-1 to X-45][book_miller_2001]. The programme's own retrospective is [The Bell X-22A V/STOL, Variable Stability Research Airplane, Lessons Learned][research_lessons_1990].

## The Research Question

The series has now met four ways of getting a propeller-driven aeroplane off the ground vertically. The [X-13][related_post_a310_ryan_x13] stood on its tail. The [X-14][related_post_a311_bell_x14] deflected jet thrust. The [X-18][related_post_a315_hiller_x18] tilted its whole wing and the [X-19][related_post_a316_curtiss_wright_x19] tilted four propellers on outriggers. **The X-22 tilts four propellers as well, but it puts each one inside a duct**, and almost everything that distinguishes this aeroplane follows from that decision.

A shroud around a propeller is not a fairing. It changes the momentum balance, it carries part of the thrust on its own inner surface, and in forward flight it behaves like a short annular wing. ## Programme Origin

The X-22A was a tri-service programme, ordered by the Navy on behalf of itself, the Army and the Air Force, to explore whether a ducted-propeller vertical-takeoff transport was practical. Bell had already flown the [X-14][related_post_a311_bell_x14] and had a long involvement in vertical flight, and the four-duct tandem layout had been under investigation in wind tunnels for several years before the contract, in [Larry A. ## The Aircraft

Four ducts of 7 feet diameter, each containing a three-bladed propeller. The rear wing spans 39 feet 3 inches and the forward one 22 feet 11 inches over the ducts, the aircraft is 39 feet 7 inches long, and it weighs 10,478 pounds empty against a gross weight of 15,287 and a maximum takeoff weight of 17,644.

Four General Electric YT58-GE-8D turboshaft engines of 1,250 shaft horsepower each drive all four propellers through a cross-shafted transmission, so that any engine can drive any duct.

$$A_{d} = \frac{\pi D^{2}}{4} = \frac{\pi \times 7^{2}}{4} = 38.48\ \text{ft}^{2}$$

Four of those give 153.9 square feet of disc, and at the 14,600 pound weight at which the aircraft is quoted as able to hover on three engines,

$$\frac{W}{A} = \frac{14{,}600}{153.9} = 94.8\ \text{lbf/ft}^{2}$$

**That is a very high disc loading.** A helicopter of the period sits between five and ten. The [X-18][related_post_a315_hiller_x18] and [X-19][related_post_a316_curtiss_wright_x19] used large propellers and sat far below it. The X-22 is closer to a lift jet than to a rotorcraft, and the consequences run through everything that follows.

## Sizing From First Principles

### What the Shroud Buys

Take a propeller inside a duct whose exit area is $\sigma$ times the disc area. Continuity and momentum across the disc give the thrust and the ideal power in terms of the exit velocity,

$$T = \rho \sigma A V_{e}^{2} \qquad P = \tfrac{1}{2} \rho \sigma A V_{e}^{3}$$

Eliminating $V_e$,

$$P = \frac{1}{2} \frac{T^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\rho \sigma A}} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad T \propto \sigma^{1/3}$$

**An open propeller is the case $\sigma = 1/2$**, because its wake contracts to half the disc area far downstream, and substituting that recovers the familiar $T^{3/2}/\sqrt{2\rho A}$. So a duct that merely prevents the contraction, with $\sigma = 1$, gives

$$\frac{T_{\text{duct}}}{T_{\text{open}}} = (2\sigma)^{1/3} = 2^{1/3} = 1.26$$

**Twenty-six percent more static thrust for the same power, from a ring of metal that does no work.** A mildly diffusing duct does better still. That is the whole reason for the configuration, and it is what makes a seven-foot propeller viable at ninety-five pounds per square foot.

The ideal power to hover 14,600 pounds on 153.9 square feet is 3,750 horsepower for an open rotor and

$$P_{\text{ideal}} = \tfrac{1}{2} \frac{W^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\rho A}} = 2{,}651\ \text{hp}$$

for the ducted case, which is $1/\sqrt{2}$ of it.

### The Quoted Margin Is Not a Margin

The programme is usually described as having 35 percent excess power so that it could hover with one engine out. **That figure is not a safety margin. It is the smallest number that makes a three-engine hover arithmetically possible.**

Four engines give 5,000 shaft horsepower and three give 3,750. If the installed power exceeds what hovering requires by 35 percent, then what remains after losing an engine is

$$\frac{3}{4} \times 1.35 = 1.0125$$

**One and a quarter percent.** The number was chosen so that three engines would just do, and not chosen with anything to spare.

That reading is checkable, because it fixes the figure of merit. If hovering at 14,600 pounds requires 5,000 divided by 1.35, then

$$\text{FM} = \frac{P_{\text{ideal}}}{P_{\text{required}}} = \frac{2{,}651}{3{,}704} = 0.716$$

**That is a thoroughly ordinary figure of merit for a ducted lift fan**, which is the first check. The second is stronger. Feeding 0.716 back through momentum theory gives the weight that three engines can actually lift,

$$W_{3} = W \left( \frac{P_{3} \, \text{FM}}{P_{\text{ideal}}} \right)^{2/3} = 14{,}721\ \text{lbf}$$

against the 14,600 the programme quotes. **Agreement to 0.83 percent, from a press figure and a momentum calculation that share nothing.**

The same arithmetic is unkind about the rest of the weight range.

| condition | weight, lbf | power required, shp | four engines | three engines |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| three-engine VTOL | 14,600 | 3,704 | +35.0% | +1.3% |
| gross | 15,287 | 3,968 | +26.0% | −5.5% |
| maximum takeoff | 17,644 | 4,920 | +1.6% | −23.8% |

**At maximum takeoff weight the X-22 could not hover on three engines and had 1.6 percent in hand on four.** Vertical takeoff was available at the bottom of the weight range and nowhere near the top, which is the recurring fact about every vertical-takeoff aircraft in this series and is stated here in numbers rather than in adjectives.

### The Downwash That Buys

High disc loading is not free at the bottom of the aircraft either. The exit velocity follows from the thrust per duct,

$$V_{e} = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho \sigma A_{d}}} = \sqrt{\frac{3{,}650}{0.0023769 \times 38.48}} = 200\ \text{ft/s}$$

**One hundred and thirty-six miles per hour, straight down, from four ducts at once.**

Disc loading is the parameter that sets all of this, and the relations are short. The induced velocity and the power loading follow from it alone,

$$v_{e} = \sqrt{\frac{W/A}{\rho}} \qquad \frac{T}{P} = \frac{2\,\text{FM}}{v_{e}}$$

so that lifting a pound costs power in proportion to the square root of the disc loading.

| $W/A$, lbf/ft² | $v_e$, ft/s | lbf per horsepower |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 45.9 | 17.2 |
| 10 | 64.9 | 12.1 |
| 40 | 129.7 | 6.1 |
| 94.8 | 199.7 | 3.9 |

**A helicopter of the period sat between five and ten and lifted about three and a half times as much per horsepower.** That is the standing charge for choosing ducts, and it is paid in every second of hover. ## What the Shroud Charges

A duct held at an angle to the oncoming air captures a mass flow and turns it onto the duct axis. Turning momentum requires a force, and the reaction on the duct is a normal force,

$$N_{\text{mom}} = \dot{m} V_{\infty} \sin\alpha \qquad \dot{m} = \rho A_{d} \left( V_{\infty} \cos\alpha + v_{i} \right)$$

The rounded lip adds a suction force of the same order, so the total is roughly twice the momentum term. **The duct is behaving as an annular wing**, and the theory and measurement of that are [Goodson and Grunwald 1962][research_goodson_grunwald_1962], [Mort 1965][research_mort_1965], [Curtiss and Howard C. 1969][research_curtiss_howard_c_1969], [Mann 1974][research_mann_1974], [Walker 1981][research_walker_1981], [Lee and Kwok 1983][research_lee_kwok_1983], [Gill and D’Andrea 2020][research_gill_dandrea_2020].

**This has to be evaluated along a conversion, not on a grid.** A duct angle of forty-five degrees at two hundred feet per second is not a flight condition, and tabulating it produces a normal force larger than the aeroplane.

### The Corridor Can Be Derived, Not Assumed

The wing area is not published, which looks like a dead end and is not one. At the end of a conversion the ducts are horizontal and the wings carry the whole weight, so the conversion speed fixes the area,

$$S = \frac{2W}{\rho V_{\text{conv}}^{{2}} C_{L,\max}} = \frac{2 \times 14{,}600}{0.0023769 \times 203^{{2}} \times 1.4} = 213\ \text{ft}^{{2}}$$

**That number has to survive a geometric check and it does.** Two thirds of it on the 39.25 foot rear span is a mean chord of 3.62 feet, and the remaining third on the 22.92 foot forward span is 3.10 feet. Chords of three to four feet on a forty-foot aeroplane are what the photographs show.

With an area the corridor follows from the force balance. Writing $i$ for the duct angle above the horizontal,

$$T \sin i + L = W \qquad T \cos i = D$$

$$\tan i = \frac{W - L}{D} \qquad T = \sqrt{(W-L)^{{2}} + D^{{2}}}$$

with $L = q S C_L$ and $D = q (S C_{D_w} + f)$, where $f$ is the equivalent flat-plate area of the four ducts computed below. **Thrust required falls monotonically through the conversion**, because the wing takes the lift faster than the drag builds, so the corridor was never thrust-limited. Whatever made the X-22 hard to convert, it was not power.

### That Answer Was Too Comfortable

The schedule above was assumed. Evaluating the same normal force along the **derived** corridor, where the wing pulls its full lift coefficient and the ducts make up the difference, gives something much worse. The ducts stay at large angles far later than a linear ramp suggests, and the momentum estimate climbs to 8,266 pounds per duct at 180 feet per second, or **2.26 times the whole aircraft weight over four ducts**.

**That is not a credible force and saying so is the point.** More than twice the weight, applied sideways in mid-conversion, would be unflyable and unmissable. So the model is wrong there, and the useful question is where it stops being right.

The momentum-turning argument assumes the duct captures its own streamtube and turns all of it. That holds while the crossflow component is small compared with the velocity the duct itself induces, so the validity parameter is

$$\mu = \frac{V_{\infty} \sin i}{v_{i}}$$

| $V$, ft/s | knots | duct angle | $\mu$ | $N/T$ | status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | 23.7 | 89.6 | 0.20 | 0.40 | model holds |
| 60 | 35.5 | 89.2 | 0.30 | 0.60 | model holds |
| 100 | 59.2 | 87.2 | 0.50 | 1.02 | marginal |
| 140 | 82.9 | 82.1 | 0.69 | 1.52 | model invalid |
| 180 | 106.6 | 60.7 | 0.79 | 2.26 | model invalid |
| 200 | 118.5 | 11.2 | 0.20 | 0.77 | model holds |

**The parameter passes 0.5 at 100 feet per second and comes back below it at 195.** The momentum model is therefore valid at both ends of the conversion and invalid in the middle, **and the middle is exactly where a tilt aircraft is hard to fly.**

What caps the force in that band is not momentum. It is that the lip cannot hold the flow round it at that incidence and separates, which is the known weakness of every ducted fan and which now has a computed boundary rather than a mention. **The honest statement is that the normal force reaches about the duct's own thrust where the model can be trusted, and that beyond it the binding limit is lip stall.** Over four ducts that is of the order of the aircraft weight, applied perpendicular to the duct axes, at exactly the speed where the wings are not yet working and the ducts are no longer pointing at the ground.

**That is the charge for the twenty-six percent.** The shroud that makes the hover affordable is an annular wing that has to be flown through the conversion, and the aeroplane which needs the shroud most in hover is the one that has the most trouble in between.

### The Ducts Are Also the Drag

The quoted maximum speed provides a check on all of this from a direction the hover calculation does not touch. At 278 knots and 10,000 feet, with an installed 5,000 shaft horsepower and a propulsive efficiency of 0.80,

$$D = \frac{\eta P}{V} = \frac{0.80 \times 5{,}000 \times 550}{469.2} = 4{,}689\ \text{lbf}$$

$$f = \frac{D}{q} = \frac{4{,}689}{193.3} = 24.3\ \text{ft}^{{2}}$$

**A clean aeroplane of this weight would have five to eight square feet.** The X-22 has 24.3, and the lift-to-drag ratio at that condition is 3.26.

Now the same number from geometry. Four ducts of seven-foot diameter present 153.9 square feet of frontal area, so the equivalent flat plate implies a duct drag coefficient of

$$C_{D,\text{duct}} = \frac{f}{4 A_{d}} = \frac{24.3}{153.9} = 0.158$$

which is an ordinary value for a short annular body with a propeller inside it. **The maximum speed and the duct geometry agree**, and they were not fitted to one another.

**That is the third face of the same trade.** The shroud buys twenty-six percent of thrust in the hover, charges an annular wing's normal force in the conversion, and then charges again in cruise by presenting four times thirty-eight square feet of frontal area to the airstream for the rest of the flight.

## Dependent Systems

### The Elevons Are in the Jet

In hover there is no free-stream dynamic pressure at all, so a conventional control surface has nothing to work with. The duct exits have plenty.

$$q_{\text{jet}} = \tfrac{1}{2} \rho V_{e}^{2} = 47.4\ \text{lbf/ft}^{2}$$

The duct normal force acts near the lip rather than at the duct centre, because the lip is where the flow is turned, so each duct contributes a nose-up couple of about

$$M = \frac{N \ell}{2}$$

for a duct of length $\ell$. At the largest defensible normal force and a four-foot duct that is 7,900 foot-pounds per duct and **31,500 foot-pounds over four**, and the tandem layout does not cancel it, because both duct pairs tilt the same way and both couples act nose-up about their own centres.

Something has to trim that. The X-22 carries elevons across the duct exits, immersed in the propeller slipstream, and they work from the first second of the hover. Twelve square feet of elevon at a lift coefficient of 0.9 in that jet gives

$$F = q_{\text{jet}} S_{e} C_{L_{e}} = 47.4 \times 12 \times 0.9 = 512\ \text{lbf}$$

and on a twelve-foot arm, four of them command about 24,600 foot-pounds. **That is the same order as the duct couple they exist to oppose**, which is why they are the size they are and why they sit where they do.

**The free stream does not reach that dynamic pressure until about 200 feet per second**, which is the end of the conversion. A four-poster with no surfaces in its own jets would have nothing but differential thrust for pitch and roll, and differential thrust on a shafted system is slow.

### The Cross-Shaft

Four engines drive four propellers through a common transmission, treated in [Leishman 1966][research_leishman_1966], [Overfield and Crawford 1967][research_overfield_crawford_1967], [Envia 2016][research_envia_2016], [Wandini et al 2016][research_wandini_2016], [Casadei et al 2019][research_casadei_2019], [Yan and Chen 2019][research_yan_chen_2019], [Yu et al 2020][research_yu_2020], [Leelaburanathanakul et al 2021][research_leelaburanathanakul_2021], [Hoogreef and Soikkeli 2022][research_hoogreef_soikkeli_2022], [Kang et al 2023][research_kang_2023], [Du et al 2024, Experimental and numerical invest][research_du_2024_2], [Du et al 2024, Investigation of a nozzle guide v][research_du_2024], [Zhang et al 2024, Four-Dimensional Generalized AMS][research_zhang_2024_2], [Aroussi 2025][research_aroussi_2025], [Hung and Dai 2025][research_hung_dai_2025], [Nabadova and Sabziev 2025][research_nabadova_sabziev_2025], [Jokar and Khoshnood 2026][research_jokar_khoshnood_2026], [Mokotoff and Cinar 2026][research_mokotoff_cinar_2026].

**Cross-shafting adds no power.** What it changes is the failure mode. Without it, losing an engine loses a duct, and losing one of four ducts on a machine with a rear semi-span of 19.6 feet applies

$$M = T_{\text{duct}} \, y = 3{,}650 \times 19.62 = 71{,}600\ \text{ft}\cdot\text{lbf}$$

of rolling moment that has to be trimmed out by the remaining three. With cross-shafting the same failure removes a quarter of the power and none of the symmetry.

The shaft itself is undramatic. At a 700 foot per second tip speed the propellers turn at about 1,910 revolutions per minute, so each absorbs

$$Q = \frac{P}{\omega} = \frac{1{,}250 \times 550}{200} = 3{,}440\ \text{ft}\cdot\text{lbf}$$

and with one engine out the three survivors deliver 938 horsepower to each of four propellers, so **312 horsepower has to cross the airframe**, a quarter of one engine.

**The cost of cross-shafting is not the shaft.** It is that every gearbox must accept power from either direction, and that the whole train becomes a single path whose failure takes everything. The first aircraft was lost to a failure in the propeller control system, which is the same lesson from the other side.

**This is the X-18's fatal deficiency, answered.** That aircraft had two engines with no interconnection, and the accident that ended it was the asymmetry rather than the shortfall. The X-22 was designed so that the asymmetry cannot happen, and the price is a transmission that has to carry full power to any duct from any engine.

## What the Data Changed

**The X-22 settled that the configuration flies.** Nothing in eighteen years of operation suggested that a four-duct tandem vertical-takeoff aircraft was uncontrollable or unsafe, which is a stronger negative result than the [X-18][related_post_a315_hiller_x18] or the [X-19][related_post_a316_curtiss_wright_x19] produced.

**What it did not settle is whether anyone wanted one.** The arithmetic of the useful load is short and unkind,

$$\frac{W_{\text{payload}}}{W} = \frac{1{,}200}{14{,}600} = 0.082$$

against an empty weight of 10,478 pounds and a useful load of 4,122, most of which is fuel. The disc loading that the duct makes affordable also produces a 136 mile per hour downwash, a severe weight sensitivity in the hover, and a payload of 1,200 pounds on an aircraft of 15,000. The configuration works and the mission arithmetic is unattractive, which is a different verdict from the one the earlier tilt aircraft received.

## Where the Framing Breaks Down

The thrust augmentation figure assumes an ideal duct with no internal losses and a specific exit area ratio which is assumed rather than measured. A real duct has friction, tip clearance and a diffuser that may separate, and the figure of merit derived here absorbs all of it into a single number.

**The normal force calculation is the weakest part of the article.** It is momentum turning multiplied by an assumed lip factor of two, and the sensitivity is large, giving a peak of anywhere between 0.54 and 1.35 times the aircraft weight over four ducts as that factor runs from one to two and a half. **The shape of the curve is robust and the magnitude is not.**

**The conversion corridor is now derived rather than assumed**, from a wing area that the conversion speed implies rather than one taken from a data sheet. That derivation rests on an assumed maximum lift coefficient of 1.4 and on the assumption that the wings carry the whole weight at the conversion speed, and the resulting area is checked against the two spans rather than against a published figure.

**The normal force is the weakest thing in the article and it is now weak in a documented way.** The momentum model overestimates it badly wherever the crossflow parameter exceeds about one half, which is most of the middle of the conversion, and the article says so rather than quoting the number the model produces there.

Nothing here treats the tandem interference, which the ground testing suggests is the hardest part of the configuration.

## The Source Base

The X-22 is well documented on the flying-qualities side and thinly documented on the ducts. The programme's own retrospective is [The Bell X-22A V/STOL, Variable Stability Research Airplane, Lessons Learned][research_lessons_1990], written twenty-four years after first flight, and the tri-service description of the aircraft is contemporary with the contract.

**The imbalance is a consequence of what the aircraft became.** Eighteen years of variable-stability work generated a large literature about handling qualities in which the X-22 is the instrument rather than the subject, and comparatively little was published about the ducted propulsion after the configuration stopped being a candidate for production.

### The Word That Belongs to Three Fields

**A ducted propeller is a marine technology, an aeronautical one and a wind-energy one**, and the harvest for this article returned all three.

The Kort nozzle is a duct around a ship's propeller and its literature is large, old and entirely irrelevant here. Diffuser-augmented wind turbines are ducted rotors whose momentum theory is the same and whose application is not. **Twenty-one marine records and several wind-turbine ones had to be read out of the pool**, and the reading was necessary because the titles are indistinguishable without it.

Three near-misses are worth recording because they went the other way. A paper on the United States **Marine** Corps and vertical takeoff is not marine engineering. A paper on **Building** Credible Flight Models is not building aerodynamics. And **tandem propeller** returned a submarine.

## Epistemic State

**Established and not in dispute.** The aircraft flew from 1966 to 1984, one of two was lost in 1966, and the survivor operated as a variable-stability in-flight simulator from 1968.

**Computed here from quoted figures.** The 26 percent thrust augmentation follows from momentum theory given the exit area ratio. The figure of merit of 0.716 follows from the quoted 35 percent excess power, and reproduces the separately quoted three-engine hover weight to 0.83 percent, which is the strongest check in the article.

**Inferred.** That the 35 percent was chosen as the minimum for three-engine hover rather than as a margin. The arithmetic is exact and the intent is inference.

**Weakly supported.** The magnitude of the duct normal force, which depends on an assumed lip factor. Its peak position in mid-conversion does not.

**Not addressed.** The tandem interference, the conversion corridor, and the structural and weight cost of the cross-shafted transmission.

## Out of Scope

The detailed design of the transmission. The variable-stability system's own control laws, which are a subject in their own right. The tri-service requirement that produced the programme and the politics of its cancellation. Comparison with the tilt-rotor line that eventually succeeded, which belongs to a later article.

## Conclusion

**The duct is the aeroplane.** It buys twenty-six percent more static thrust for nothing but a ring of metal, which is what makes a seven-foot propeller carry ninety-five pounds per square foot, and it charges for that in the conversion by acting as an annular wing whose normal force peaks at about its own thrust in the middle of the manoeuvre.

**The margin was never a margin.** Thirty-five percent excess power leaves one and a quarter percent after an engine fails, and at maximum takeoff weight it leaves nothing at all. The figure of merit that follows from those numbers checks out against an independent momentum calculation to better than one percent, which means the design was understood precisely and was simply tight.

**And the aeroplane outlived its own question.** It was built to find out whether four ducted propellers made a practical transport, and the answer was that they made a practical aircraft and an unattractive transport. What kept it flying for eighteen years was that somebody realised a machine which can hover and convert, and whose control system can be reprogrammed to feel like something else, is more valuable as a laboratory than as a prototype.

**No other aircraft in this series was rescued that way.** The X-18 was scrapped, the X-19 crashed, the X-20 was cancelled and the X-21 was retired to a photographic target. The X-22 was repurposed, and it is the only one of them still whole.
