## Display Equations 16 to 34, and the Pass Could Not Verify the Central Claim

**Lines 5,551 to 5,669, words 31,980 to 33,185, display equations 16 to 34, declared
symbols 34 to 52.** References unchanged at 2,567, because this pass adds relations rather
than sources.

**THE DRAFT ASSERTED THAT TWELVE PROPELLERS SUPPLY A FACTOR OF 2.38 IN LIFT AND DID NOT
CHECK WHETHER THE INSTALLED POWER COULD DO IT.** That is a computation the published
numbers permit, and it is the obvious thing an equation pass should try.

**Only seventy percent of the span is blown.** Twelve propellers of 1.9 feet diameter sweep
6.95 metres of a 9.94 metre span, so the blown part must work harder than a uniform
treatment suggests. Blowing the whole wing would need a 54 percent velocity increase.
**Blowing seventy percent of it needs 73 percent**, which at a 58 knot stall speed is 22
metres per second added to the flow.

**Treating the propellers as ideal actuator discs puts the required power at 139 kilowatts
against 151 installed, which is 92 percent.** An actuator disc has no profile drag, no
swirl loss, no tip loss, no motor loss and no controller loss. **At a propulsive efficiency
of 75 percent the requirement is 185 kilowatts, which exceeds the installed power.**

**That is a boundary on the explanation rather than a fault in the aeroplane.** Raising the
dynamic pressure over seventy percent of the span cannot by itself account for landing this
wing on this installed power. **Something else has to be doing part of the work**, and the
blown-wing literature says what it is, being swirl, a changed local angle of attack, and a
re-energised boundary layer that separates later. **The article states the boundary and
says it does not know from these sources how much of the work each effect does.**

## The Published Range Does Close, Which Is Worth Saying Because Other Things Do Not

**The stored energy, the drivetrain efficiency and the weight together imply a lift to drag

That matters in an article whose draft pass already found that **the published wing geometry

## The Motors Are a Smaller Advance Than the Architecture Suggests

**A cruise motor with its propeller gives 1.36 kilowatts per kilogram against about 1.17 for
the Rotax it replaced**, and that comparison already flatters the motor, because the Rotax
figure is a dry mass without a propeller while the motor figure includes one.

**What distributed electric propulsion buys is not power density but divisibility.** An
electric motor loses very little efficiency as it is made smaller and a piston engine loses
a great deal, which is why fourteen small motors are a sensible thing to build where
fourteen small engines never were.

**And the two propeller sets are visibly designed for different jobs.** The cruise pair runs
at an advance ratio of 1.34 and the high-lift set at 0.68, almost exactly half, which is
what a propeller built to move a lot of air slowly looks like beside one built to move a
little air quickly.

## What the Pack Is Asked to Do

**Running everything at once would draw six times the pack's own capacity per hour and empty
it in under ten minutes.** Nothing in the mission asks for that, since the high-lift motors
are used only at the ends of the flight, **but it bounds what the pack and its wiring had to
be built for.**
