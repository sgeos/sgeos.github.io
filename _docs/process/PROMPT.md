### A105 Topic

The topic for this article is:

"Neuromorphic CPUs and 3D Printable CPUs for Autonomous Probe Computing"

INTRODUCTION

- Begin with an introduction that forward-summarizes the article.
- Explain the semiconductor closure problem for autonomous probes.
- Introduce two potential approaches that may reduce the closure gap:
  - Neuromorphic computing
  - 3D printable CPUs
- Briefly explain what these technologies are and why they may be promising
  for long-duration autonomous systems such as Von Neumann probes.
- State the article’s central question: whether these approaches could provide
  practical computing architectures that are easier to manufacture and maintain
  in autonomous industrial systems.

MAIN SECTIONS

Write two main sections covering:

1. Neuromorphic CPUs
2. 3D Printable CPUs

For each section, address the following topics in order.

HISTORY

- Describe the origins of the technology.
- Identify the early theoretical work and initial experiments.
- Discuss major milestones in the development of the technology.

HISTORICAL AND MODERN EXAMPLES

- Provide examples of historical and contemporary implementations.
- Identify research projects, experimental systems, and commercial efforts.
- Describe partial solutions and notable prototypes.

STATE OF THE ART

- Describe the current technological capabilities.
- Explain the typical architectures used.
- Discuss performance, power consumption, and manufacturing requirements.

CONTEMPORARY APPLICATIONS

- Describe how the technology is currently used.
- Identify industries or research areas where it is deployed.
- Explain the advantages and limitations of the technology in practical systems.

VON NEUMANN PROBE REQUIREMENTS

Explain the computational requirements of autonomous probes, including:

- navigation and guidance
- manufacturing control
- system monitoring and repair
- communication encoding and decoding
- scientific data processing

Discuss how these requirements differ from conventional terrestrial computing.

STATE OF THE ART IN THE CONTEXT OF PROBE DEVELOPMENT

- Evaluate how well the technology meets probe requirements.
- Discuss advantages such as:
  - energy efficiency
  - fault tolerance
  - simplicity of manufacturing
  - robustness in radiation environments.
- Discuss limitations and unresolved engineering challenges.

WORK IN PROGRESS AND PARTIAL SOLUTIONS

- Describe current research directions.
- Identify experimental approaches that may improve the technology.
- Discuss emerging fabrication techniques or architectures.

HYPOTHETICAL AND EXTRAPOLATED APPROACHES

- Discuss how the technology might evolve if optimized for autonomous probes.
- Consider alternative architectures or simplified designs.
- Explore how probe civilizations might adapt or redesign these systems.

INFORMATION STORAGE AND MEMORY

Include a section discussing how probes using these computing approaches
might store and preserve information.

Topics to cover:

- long-term storage of probe software and blueprints
- storage of star maps and navigation data
- memory technologies compatible with neuromorphic or printable systems
- redundancy and error correction strategies
- longevity of stored information over extremely long missions.

COMPARISON AND ARCHITECTURAL IMPLICATIONS

Compare the two approaches.

Discuss:

- manufacturability in autonomous factories
- tolerance to imperfect fabrication
- power efficiency
- scalability
- suitability for distributed probe networks.

Explain how these technologies might integrate with other probe subsystems,
including mechanical control systems and analog electronics.

CONCLUSION

- Provide a conclusion that backward-summarizes the article.
- Restate the potential role of neuromorphic and printable computing
  in reducing the semiconductor closure gap.
- Discuss how these technologies could fit into hybrid probe architectures.

SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL

Include the following sections:

- Future Reading
- References
