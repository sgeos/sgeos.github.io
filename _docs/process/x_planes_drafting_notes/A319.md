### How the Variable-Stability System Worked

**The system was a response-feedback design**, the most common mechanization of the period, in which sensed motion variables command control deflections in proportion, so that changing the gains changes the closed-loop aircraft. [NASA TM-81156][ref_nasa_tm81156] describes it in detail. It is a four-axis system, in thrust, pitch, roll and yaw, primarily analogue with an on-board digital computer, with electrohydraulic servos driving the right-hand flight controls through the same linkages the right-hand pilot moves in normal flight. The evaluation pilot sits on the left, mechanically disconnected from the right-hand controls, and flies through artificial-feel servos whose force gradients run from 1.5 pounds per inch to a stiff stick in pitch and roll. The safety pilot on the right sets up each configuration in flight. Feedback gains are programmable with airspeed from 30 knots rearward to 150 knots forward, using an omnidirectional airspeed system with a second sensor on the nose boom for the vertical component in hover. A limited-authority feedforward system acts as a vernier on blade pitch and elevon angle, because a closed-loop variable-stability aircraft needs its actuators placed very precisely. The ducts rotate together between 0 and 95 degrees at no more than 5 degrees a second.

**A response-feedback aircraft does not know exactly what it is simulating.** The resulting dynamics are not known in advance, so each configuration has to be identified from its own flight records, which is the subject of [Lebacqz 1974][research_lebacqz_1974]. The Calspan team used a Kalman filter technique to estimate the derivatives the system had produced, among them the angle-of-attack stability and pitch rate damping varied in the first programme and every roll and yaw derivative varied in the second.

**The limits are stated as plainly as the capabilities.** The variable-stability system could not be used above about 125 knots, which excluded the upper end of the conversion. It had only limited uncoupled direct force in the longitudinal and vertical directions and none sideways. Hover was restricted by gearbox temperature as described above. And the airframe had an established service life of 500 flight hours, of which 80 were expected to remain at the end of 1980, so the aircraft would by then have flown about

$$500 - 80 = 420\ \text{h}$$

against the about 200 hours the Vertical Flight Society gives for its whole career. The two cannot both be right, and the NASA figure is the primary one.

### The Research Programmes

NASA TM-81156 summarises the programmes flown through 1979, and the Calspan papers report them in detail. The first three were sponsored by an interagency steering group of the Navy, the Air Force, NASA Langley and the Federal Aviation Administration, and the later ones by the Navy alone.

**The first programme measured short-term longitudinal dynamics for the steep approach of a short takeoff and landing, or STOL, aircraft.** Two pilots made 50 evaluations of 18 combinations of short-period frequency and damping at 65 and 80 knots, on approach paths of 6 to 10 degrees, visual and on instruments, reported in [Schuler et al 1972][research_schuler_1972] and [Smith 1973][research_smith_1973]. Lebacqz's account gives sixteen combinations at a 9 degree glide slope, and the two counts are not reconciled in the documents read. Essentially no data of this kind existed for STOL approaches before it, and supplying them for the V/STOL flying-qualities specification, MIL-F-83300, was a main purpose. It showed that steep instrument approaches could be flown satisfactorily with only instrument landing system information, given good short-term dynamics, and the results were applied in the development of the YC-14 and YC-15.

**The second measured lateral-directional dynamics and roll control power.** Three pilots flew 109 evaluations of 17 sets of dynamics at a 7.5 degree glide slope and 65 knots, varying the roll mode time constant, Dutch roll frequency, roll due to sideslip and yaw due to aileron, with roll authority limited electrically for some sets. Two findings bear directly on the specification. Satisfactory flying qualities needed about twice the roll damping that MIL-F-83300 specified, and about half its roll control power. Since the roll mode time constant is the inverse of the roll damping derivative,

$$\tau_{R} = -\frac{1}{L_{p}}$$

doubling the damping halves the time constant. The same programme expanded the system into the hover and made the first vertical landings ever performed on a variable-stability system.

**The third flew instrument transitions to the hover**, in [Lebacqz and Aiken 1975][research_lebacqz_aiken_1975]. The task was a hooded approach from 100 knots, capturing a 7.5 degree glide slope and decelerating at about 0.05 g to a hover at 100 feet. Decelerating uniformly from 100 knots, or 168.8 feet per second, at that rate takes

$$s = \frac{V^{2}}{2a} = \frac{168.8^{2}}{2 \times 0.05 \times 32.17} = 8{,}854\ \text{ft} \qquad t = \frac{V}{a} = 105\ \text{s}$$

which is consistent with the deceleration beginning about 8,000 feet out in still air, as the paper gives it, and continuing through the flare. On the glide slope at 100 knots the aircraft descends at

$$\dot{h} = 60\, V \sin\gamma = 60 \times 168.8 \times \sin 7.5^{\circ} = 1{,}322\ \text{ft/min}$$

Thirty-seven evaluations covered five display formats, from position information alone to four-axis control directors, against five levels of control augmentation, in a flight experiment of 45 hours. **The central result is a trade between control and display**, in which more augmentation needs less display and the reverse. Translational velocity had to be displayed for any acceptable system, and because the basic X-22A has low natural height damping near the hover, a thrust director was needed whenever the pilot also had to manage the duct angle. The programme also devised a duct-angle command, the Independent Thrust Vector Inclination Command, which follows the linear corridor described earlier.

**The fourth simulated the AV-8B** for a decelerating instrument approach. Forty-three evaluations of 22 configurations, drawn from six flight control schemes and seven head-up display formats, showed that no display gave consistently satisfactory ratings with the proposed rate stability augmentation, while rate-command and attitude-command systems did with several displays, and the resulting display formats were being considered for the AV-8B. **The fifth**, begun in April 1978 and planned through 1980, addressed hover and low-speed criteria for visual and instrument flight and then transition criteria.

The four completed programmes add up to

$$50 + 109 + 37 + 43 = 239$$

piloted evaluations in NASA TM-81156, each a configuration flown and rated.

The final flights are dated October 1984 in the summary of the Wikipedia entry and 1988 in its text and in the Vertical Flight Society's account. No primary document read here records the end of flying.
