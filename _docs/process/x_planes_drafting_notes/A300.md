and the normal Mach number ahead of the shock is $M_{1n} = M_1 \sin \beta_o$, so the total pressure loss is that of a normal shock at $M_{1n}$ rather than at $M_1$. Because the loss grows steeply with normal Mach number, splitting one strong shock into several weak ones recovers most of the total pressure. A two-shock system at Mach 2 recovers roughly 0.90 against 0.72 for a single normal shock, and a three-shock system approaches 0.95. Thrust scales with that recovery directly, so the difference between a fixed and a variable inlet at Mach 2 is roughly a quarter of the installed thrust. The remedy known at the time was a variable geometry inlet using oblique shocks, since a sequence of $n$ oblique shocks followed by a weak normal shock recovers far more total pressure than a single strong normal shock. The NACA was investigating precisely this while the X-3 was being built, through [NACA 1946][research_supersonic_inlet_new_1946], [NACA 1951][research_conical_spike_side_inlet_1951], [NACA 1952][research_normal_shock_side_inlet_1952], [NACA 1953][research_conical_spike_recovery_1953], [NACA 1953][research_conical_nose_inlet_1953], and later with throat bleed by [NACA 1957][research_ramp_inlet_bleed_1957] and active control by [NASA 1958][research_inlet_control_1958]. ### The Definition of Primary
**Primacy is a property of the document, and this survey derives it from the identifier.** A record is a report primary when its address is on the NASA Technical Reports Server or it carries a digital object identifier under the 10.21236 prefix, which Crossref registers for Defense Technical Information Center reports. **The NACA reports that carry this article's argument are report primaries under that rule**, and so are all of the X-3's own reports. **The rule does not reach the journal papers among the foundational sources**, such as Glauert on compressibility, Sears on minimum wave drag and Sutherland on viscosity, so 20.1 percent is a lower bound on how much of the survey is first-hand.
